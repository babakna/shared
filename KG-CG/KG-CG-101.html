What this course is

A self-paced introduction to knowledge graphs and context graphs: what each one is, what makes them different, how they bind together, and how that pairing produces an answer you can defend rather than a guess you have to trust.

How to move around

  • The bar under the header lists all ten sections — the eight teaching modules, then the glossary and the references. Click any one to jump straight to it; nothing is locked.
  • Previous and Next at the bottom of each module walk the intended order.
  • The home icon returns to the course overview and your progress.

Keyboard

  • ← and → move between modules.
  • Esc closes this dialog.

Progress and quizzes

  • Each of the eight teaching modules ends with five questions. Every answer explains itself, including the wrong ones. There is one attempt per question — reloading the module gives you a fresh set.
  • Four correct out of five marks a module complete, so the progress bar counts those eight. Progress is saved in this browser only — no account, no server, nothing leaves your machine.
  • Use Reset course progress below to clear it.

Interactive panels

Each of the eight teaching modules carries a hands-on panel — a sandbox, a slider, or a toggle. They are the point of the module, not decoration. Change the inputs and watch what the change does to the conclusion. The glossary and the references have no panel; they are reference material.

What is in a module

  • Formal definitions open most modules. Each term is given three ways — the formal statement, the plain-language reading, and the instance used in the worked telecom example — so you can take whichever you need and skip the rest.
  • Diagrams are numbered by module, and each caption states the argument the picture is making rather than describing what you can already see.
  • Worked fragments show real Cypher, SQL, Turtle, TriG, SHACL, OWL, PROV-O and JSON. They are illustrative and readable rather than runnable against any particular engine.
  • Onward reading closes each module: specific clauses or sections rather than whole documents, each with the reason to open it. Every source appears in full in the references.

What changed in V3.0 (September 2026)

A content pass across all eight modules, prompted by a fair complaint: the modules were too thin for anyone wanting the detail rather than a search-engine summary. The written material roughly doubled, and the additions are structural rather than more prose — 43 formal definitions, 17 numbered diagrams drawn to carry an argument, 12 comparison tables, 16 worked code and data fragments, and an onward-reading block per module pointing at specific clauses.

Several things were made precise that had previously been asserted: the traversal cost model and its dependence on degree rather than dataset size; the difference between SHACL validation and OWL entailment, and why an OWL cardinality does not reject anything; the decay formula, plotted from the formula rather than sketched, with its half-lives and hard expiries; the propagation algorithm including attenuation and degree normalisation; the scoring function with its weights and its temporal veto; and the five distinct reasons a system should refuse to act. The glossary grew from 111 terms to 132. The companion demo is unchanged.

On a phone the diagrams had been shrinking to about four pixels of label text, which is not reading. Each figure now sits in its own scroll region: below 820 px it holds a legible minimum width and scrolls sideways, and printing restores the full width. Diagrams are keyboard-focusable so the arrow keys move them.

Scope and accuracy

Concepts here are deliberately durable. The telecom examples describe a generic operator and are illustrative, not a specification of any real network. Where a standard is named, treat it as a pointer for further reading and confirm against the current published document.
