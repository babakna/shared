Platforms, Brokerages and DIY Tools

Whether you hire an adviser or not, your money lives on a platform: a brokerage, a robo-advisor, a fund company or a workplace plan. This module shows what separates a good platform from an expensive one, how robo-advisors work, which free tools let you run your own numbers, how to lock your accounts down, and how to move them when it is time.

The question this module answersWhere should my investments live, and what do I need to manage them well on my own?

Start here

Your money has an address

You probably chose your bank for convenience: a branch nearby, a good app, no monthly fee. For most people that is fine, because every insured bank account works about the same. Investment platforms are not interchangeable in the same way.

Two brokerages can hold the identical index fund and still leave you with different results, because of what they charge around it, what they pay on your idle cash, and how well they protect your login. INV-005 explained how brokers and trades work. This module is about choosing and running the platform. It starts with where your money actually sits (Figure 104.1). A brokerage is a custodian: it holds your securities in your name and keeps them separate from its own assets. Uninvested cash is usually "swept" somewhere else, and where it goes decides both what it earns and which protection applies.

What this module covers

  1. What to compare when choosing a brokerage, and what not to worry about.
  2. Cash sweeps: the quiet cost of idle cash, with a calculator.
  3. Robo-advisors: how they work, what they cost, and a calculator comparing them with doing it yourself and with a human adviser.

What matters

Five things to compare, and one to stop worrying about

A generation ago, the main cost of a brokerage was the commission on each trade. That era is largely over: Charles Schwab, for example, cut its online commission for US-listed stocks, exchange-traded funds (ETFs) and options from $4.95 to zero in October 2019 (options still cost $0.65 a contract), and $0 online stock and ETF trades are now common at large brokers. For a buy-and-hold index investor, what matters now is everything else.

What to compareWhy it mattersWhere to find it
1. Costs around the tradeAccount, transfer and paper-statement fees; commissions on mutual funds from other companies ("transaction-fee funds"); broker-assisted trade chargesThe firm's fee schedule and Form CRS
2. Fund selectionCan you buy the low-cost index funds and ETFs you want without a fee? Are the default choices the firm's own ("proprietary") funds, which may not transfer out?Fund screener; fee schedule
3. The cash sweep rateOn a large cash balance, the difference between sweep options can be worth more than every other cost combined (next tab)The firm's sweep-program disclosure and current rates
4. Service and featuresAccount types (IRA, Roth IRA, custodial, trust), automatic investing, fractional shares, phone support, a branch if you want one, beneficiary designations onlineThe firm's website; ask before opening
5. Protection and recordSIPC membership; the firm's disciplinary history; how it handles account takeoversSIPC member list; BrokerCheck from the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA) (firm search)
💡

What not to worry about. For long-term investors buying broad funds a few times a year, small differences in trade execution rarely matter. Brokers must publish quarterly reports on where they route stock orders, including the terms of any payment for order flow (Securities and Exchange Commission, or SEC, Rule 606); worth knowing about, but not the thing that decides your outcome.

Going furtherFund company, brokerage, or both?

Some large fund companies also run brokerages, and some brokerages sponsor their own funds. Buying a company's own funds on its own platform is often cheapest; buying them elsewhere may carry a transaction fee, and some proprietary funds cannot move to another broker at all (FINRA Rule 11870 lists them among "nontransferable assets"). If you expect to switch platforms, favor widely available ETFs, which any brokerage can hold.

The quiet cost

What your idle cash earns, and what protects it

Cash sweep program
What it is
The arrangement that moves uninvested cash in a brokerage or advisory account automatically into a default vehicle: usually a bank deposit at one or more partner banks, or a money market fund. The firm chooses the options and, for bank sweeps, it or its affiliated bank often sets the rate.
Used for
Holding cash between trades, collecting dividends, and paying fees.
What it means for you
The default sweep can pay much less than a money market fund you pick yourself. The SEC found in 2025 that at two large firms, during rising rates, the gap between bank sweep rates and other sweep options "at times grew to almost 4 percent."

With the default inputs, the Riveras' $41,000 of idle cash earns about $41 a year at 0.10%, against about $1,435 at a hypothetical 3.50%: a gap of about $1,394 a year, or about $4,334 over three years with compounding. The formula is simple: yearly gap = cash × (alternative rate − current rate). Their money is in checking, not a brokerage, but the arithmetic is the same.

Practitioner depthCash inside advisory accounts

Cash in an advisory account can cost twice: the adviser may charge its percentage fee on the cash, while the cash itself earns a low sweep rate. The SEC's 2025 orders against Wells Fargo and Merrill Lynch (Case studies tab) turned on exactly this: firms that offered bank deposit sweeps as the only option for most advisory clients, earned a significant benefit from that cash, and lacked policies to consider clients' best interests in choosing sweep options. If your adviser holds cash for you, ask what it earns and whether you pay a fee on it.

Automated advice

How robo-advisors work, and what they really cost

Robo-advisorthe SEC spells it "robo-adviser"
What it is
An automated investment manager. The SEC staff describes robo-advisers as typically registered investment advisers that use "online algorithmic-based programs" to provide discretionary management: you answer questions on a website or app, the program builds a portfolio, and then it manages the account. Some offer human planners too.
Used for
Low-maintenance investing: automatic rebalancing, automatic investing of deposits, and often automated tax-loss harvesting in taxable accounts.
What it means for you
A robo-adviser is a fiduciary like any registered adviser. Its advice is only as good as your answers to its questionnaire and the assumptions built into its algorithm, which its Form ADV brochure must describe.

What they cost. Robo-advisors usually charge a yearly advisory fee as a percentage of assets, on top of the expense ratios of the funds they use. Prices vary and change, so check the firm's current Form ADV brochure. As examples, when checked in September 2026 Betterment's and Wealthfront's pricing pages both listed 0.25% a year for their basic automated service, with Betterment charging $5 a month instead on balances under $24,000 unless you deposit at least $200 a month. Some robo-advisors charge no advisory fee at all and earn money another way, often from the cash they hold in your portfolio. That is the trade-off the calculator below lets you test.

With the default inputs, which follow Denise from $3,000 plus $6,000 a year for 40 years at 6% before costs, doing it yourself in 0.06% index funds ends at about $999,000. A 0.25% robo-advisor ends at about $932,000 ($67,000 less), and a 1% human adviser at about $759,000 ($241,000 less). Now set the robo's fee to zero and its cash share to 10% earning 2%: it ends at about $901,000, worse than the 0.25% robo with no cash. A "free" robo that holds a lot of cash is not free.

Going furtherWhat the SEC tells robo-advisers to disclose

The SEC staff's 2017 guidance asks robo-advisers to explain, among other things, that an algorithm manages accounts; what it does; its assumptions and limitations; the particular risks (for example, that it might rebalance more often than you expect, or not respond to prolonged market changes); any conflicts in who built or owns it; and how much human involvement there is. Read the brochure for these points before you sign up.

Run your own numbers

A do-it-yourself toolkit

If you manage your own money, three kinds of tool cover almost everything: a spreadsheet to know what you own, a backtesting and simulation tool to explore allocations, and planning calculators for specific decisions.

1. A spreadsheet that answers three questions

One tab, one row per holding: account, owner, tax type (taxable, pre-tax, Roth, HSA), fund name and ticker, asset class, value, expense ratio, and for taxable holdings the cost basis. That table answers the three questions that matter: what is my allocation (sum of value by asset class, divided by the total), what am I paying (the value-weighted expense ratio), and where is it (value by tax type, for INV-036's asset location).

Weighted expense ratio = Σ (valuei × ERi) ÷ Σ valuei    e.g. =SUMPRODUCT(G2:G20,H2:H20)/SUM(G2:G20)

Update it quarterly or when you rebalance. For your own return, a spreadsheet's XIRR function applied to your deposits, withdrawals and ending value gives the money-weighted return that INV-057 explains. INV-059 covers record keeping and consolidation.

2. Backtesting and simulation, with care

Portfolio Visualizer is a widely used free website for backtesting asset allocations on historical data and running Monte Carlo simulations. Tools like it are excellent for building intuition, and dangerous if you treat one historical path as a forecast. Figure 104.5 shows why: the same 60/40 mix of US stocks and 10-year Treasuries, rebalanced yearly, earned anywhere from 3.5% to 14.8% a year over 20-year periods, depending only on the start date.

Lock it down

Account takeovers and how to stop them

FINRA describes a customer account takeover as criminals stealing usernames, passwords or other security information, such as multi-factor codes, or using other cyber-enabled fraud, to get into your financial accounts. Common routes are phishing emails and texts, fake websites, callers impersonating your firm, passwords leaked in data breaches, malware, and SIM swapping, in which a criminal takes over your phone number so that your security codes go to them.

Multi-factor authenticationMFA, often two-factor (2FA)
What it is
Logging in with two or more different kinds of proof: something you know (a password), something you have (a phone, app or security key), or something you are (a fingerprint or face).
Used for
Stopping someone who has your password from getting in.
What it means for you
FINRA says enabling it "can significantly reduce the likelihood of a cybercriminal taking over your account." Codes sent by text can be intercepted in a SIM swap, so where your firm offers an authenticator app or a security key, prefer it, and ask your phone carrier about account protections.
Trusted contact person
What it is
Someone age 18 or older whom you authorize your brokerage to contact in limited circumstances. FINRA Rule 4512 requires firms to make reasonable efforts to get a trusted contact's name and details for individual accounts.
Used for
Under the rule, the firm may contact this person to address possible financial exploitation, or to confirm your contact information, health status, or the identity of a legal guardian, executor, trustee or power-of-attorney holder.
What it means for you
FINRA stresses that naming a trusted contact gives that person no authority to act on your behalf or trade in your account. It is an emergency contact, not a key.

If something goes wrong, FINRA's advice is to change passwords on the affected accounts, place fraud alerts with the credit bureaus, and report it to your firm and to FINRA, the SEC or your state securities regulator. Call your firm using the number on its website or your statement, never one from the suspicious message.

Switching

How to move an account, and when it is worth it

Most people who dislike their brokerage stay because moving sounds hard. It is not. Most transfers between US brokerages use ACATS, an electronic system run by the National Securities Clearing Corporation (NSCC), and FINRA Rule 11870 sets the timetable.

ACATS transferAutomated Customer Account Transfer Service
What it is
A standardized electronic transfer of your holdings from one brokerage (the "carrying" firm) to another (the "receiving" firm). You start it at the new firm. Under Rule 11870, the old firm must validate or take exception to the transfer instruction within one business day, and complete the transfer within three business days after validation.
Used for
Moving whole accounts or part of one, "in kind": the same shares move, rather than being sold.
What it means for you
You do not have to sell anything to change brokers. For the Harpers' taxable account, full of gains, selling to move could trigger a large tax bill (INV-035); moving in kind does not sell anything.

What can go wrong. Some assets are "nontransferable": the old firm's proprietary funds, a mutual fund the new firm will not hold, or a product with restrictions. The rule requires the old firm to contact you and offer choices: sell it (with any fees disclosed), leave it at the old firm, or transfer it directly into your name. Match your name, account type and account number exactly to your old statement; mismatches are the usual cause of rejections. Move retirement accounts into the same type of account (an IRA into an IRA). Check both firms' fee schedules for transfer charges, and watch for surrender charges on annuities and some funds before you move them.

When to switch

  • Your cash earns far less than you could get elsewhere and the firm will not offer you a better sweep option.
  • You are paying for funds or services you could get cheaper: transaction fees on the funds you use, account fees, a wrap fee you no longer need.
  • You are consolidating scattered accounts (old 401(k)s, several IRAs) to simplify rebalancing, required minimum distributions and your heirs' job (INV-059).
  • Security or service failed you: no strong login options, slow fraud response, no one to call.

Do not switch for a sign-up bonus alone, in the middle of a tax-sensitive sale, or to chase features you will not use.

Real enforcement

When platforms profited from the details

Case study 1A "no-fee" robo-adviser and its cash, 2022
What happened
The SEC found that from March 2015 to November 2018, Schwab's disclosures for Schwab Intelligent Portfolios said the portfolios' cash was set by a "disciplined portfolio construction methodology" seeking "optimal return[s]," while Schwab's own data showed that under most market conditions the cash would make clients less money for the same risk. Model portfolios held 6% to 29.4% in cash, swept to Schwab's affiliated bank, which earned the spread. The robo-adviser was advertised as having no advisory fee and no hidden fees. Without admitting or denying the findings, three Schwab subsidiaries agreed to pay $187 million.
What it teaches
If a service is free, find out how it is paid. A cash allocation earning less than the rest of the portfolio is a cost, even if no fee line appears.
Source
SEC (2022, June 13). Schwab subsidiaries misled robo-adviser clients about absence of hidden fees (Press Release 2022-104); and the SEC's order, Release No. 34-95087.
Case study 2Cash sweeps at two large firms, 2025
What happened
In January 2025 the SEC settled charges that Wells Fargo Advisors and Merrill Lynch offered bank deposit sweep programs as the only sweep option for most advisory clients, received a significant financial benefit from that cash, and lacked reasonably designed policies to consider clients' best interests in choosing sweep options. During rising rates, the gap between the bank sweeps and other options "at times grew to almost 4 percent." The firms paid $60 million in civil penalties, without admitting or denying the findings.
What it teaches
The default is chosen by the firm. Look up your sweep rate, compare it with a money market fund you could choose, and ask whether you pay an advisory fee on the cash.
Source
SEC (2025, January 17). SEC charges pair of Wells Fargo advisory firms and Merrill Lynch with compliance failures relating to cash sweep programs (Press Release 2025-16).
Case study 3The first robo-adviser enforcement actions, 2018
What happened
In December 2018 the SEC brought its first cases against robo-advisers. It found that Wealthfront told clients using its tax-loss harvesting that it would monitor all their accounts to avoid wash sales, which can reduce the benefit, but did not; wash sales occurred in at least 31% of enrolled accounts over more than three years. Separately, Hedgeable published performance comparisons with competitors that used fewer than 4% of its client accounts, ones with higher-than-average returns.
What it teaches
Automated does not mean flawless. Check that promised features work (your 1099 shows wash-sale adjustments), and treat marketing performance comparisons with suspicion.
Source
SEC (2018, December 21). SEC charges two robo-advisers with false disclosures (Press Release 2018-300).

Decisions

Platform decisions for three households

Scenario 1 · The Riveras

Marcus and Elena keep a $9,000 emergency fund plus $41,000 more in checking earning almost nothing. They want to start 529s for Sofia and Leo and keep some cash for a car in two years. What should they do with the $41,000?

Leave itSafe from loss, but at 0.10% against a hypothetical 3.50% it gives up about $1,394 a year, and inflation erodes it.
Split by goalRight. Money needed within a few years belongs in safe, higher-yielding cash (INV-008, INV-019); money for Sofia's and Leo's college, 11 and 14 years away, can be invested (INV-034).
All in stocksStocks can fall sharply over two years, which is the car's horizon. Match the investment to when the money is needed.
The principle: give every dollar a job and a place that fits the job.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers

The Harpers decide to leave their 1.25% adviser, keep an hourly planner, and move $1.1 million to a low-cost brokerage. Their taxable account holds 25 technology stocks with large gains. How should they move it?

Sell, then moveSelling $220,000 of appreciated stock at once could realize a large gain in one tax year, needlessly. Selling also leaves the money uninvested while it moves.
Transfer in kindRight. The same shares arrive in about a week. Then they can diversify on their own schedule, for example spreading sales over years or giving appreciated shares to charity (INV-035, INV-039). Before moving, they should check for any proprietary funds that cannot transfer.
The principle: move the account, not the money; decide what to sell separately.
Scenario 3 · Ruth

Ruth receives a text saying her IRA login was "locked for suspicious activity," with a link to "verify." Her accounts use a four-digit PIN and no second factor. What should she do?

Tap the linkThis is the classic phishing pattern FINRA warns about: urgency plus a link to a look-alike site that captures her login.
Call, then hardenRight. Calling a number she already trusts answers the question safely. Then strong login protection and a trusted contact close the gaps. Anna gets no power over the account; the firm just has someone to call.
Cash at homeTrades a small online risk for large risks of theft, fire and lost interest. Her IRA also has tax rules that make withdrawing it costly.
The principle: verify through a channel you chose, never one that chose you.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

The Riveras' $41,000 earns 0.10%. A money market fund pays 3.50%. How many dollars a year do they give up?

$41,000 × 0.034 = $1,394 a year ($1,435 − $41).
Exercise 2

A robo-advisor charges 0.25% a year. What is its fee, in dollars, on a $40,000 account?

$40,000 × 0.0025 = $100, plus the expense ratios of the funds it uses.
Exercise 3

A "no-fee" robo keeps 10% of a $100,000 portfolio in cash earning 2%, while the invested portfolio is expected to earn 6%. Roughly what does the cash allocation cost per year, in dollars?

$10,000 of cash × (0.06 − 0.02) = $400 a year of expected return given up: the same as a 0.40% fee on the whole account.
Exercise 4

Under FINRA Rule 11870, if the old firm validates a transfer on the last allowed day and completes it on the last allowed day, how many business days after the transfer instruction does it finish?

One business day to validate, then three business days to complete: 4 business days, if nothing is rejected.
