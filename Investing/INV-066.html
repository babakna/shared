Financing and Leverage for Investors

Most real estate is bought with borrowed money, and borrowing is what makes real estate returns look spectacular in good years and ruinous in bad ones. This module explains how investor loans differ from home loans, what lenders test, how leverage multiplies both gains and losses, how rising rates squeeze leveraged owners, and what happened when millions of borrowers found out in 2006–2012.

The question this module answersHow much should I borrow to buy investment property, and what can go wrong?

Start here

Leverage: the same idea as your home mortgage

If you have bought a home with a mortgage, you have used leverage: controlling an asset worth far more than the cash you put in. For most homeowners it has worked well, because they held on through downturns and home prices rose over decades.

The arithmetic is simple and unforgiving. Buy a $300,000 property with $75,000 down and a $225,000 loan. If the value rises 10% to $330,000, your equity goes from $75,000 to $105,000: +40%. If the value falls 10%, your equity falls to $45,000: −40%. If it falls 25%, your equity is gone, and you still owe $225,000. The loan does not care what the property is worth.

What lenders offer

Investment property loans cost more and require more down

Lenders and the government-sponsored companies that buy most US mortgages, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, treat a property you will rent out as riskier than one you live in. Borrowers tend to protect the home they live in first, and research on the 2000s bust found investors contributed disproportionately to defaults (Case studies tab).

Loan-to-value ratio (LTV)
What it is
The loan divided by the property's value. A $225,000 loan on a $300,000 property is a 75% LTV; your down payment is the other 25%.
Used for
Setting maximum loan sizes and pricing. The higher the LTV, the less cushion the lender has if prices fall.
What it means for you
Fannie Mae's Eligibility Matrix (August 5, 2026) allows up to 85% LTV to purchase a one-unit investment property and 75% for two to four units, versus up to 97% for a one-unit home you live in. Cash-out refinances on investment property are capped at 75% (one unit) and 70% (two to four units).
Loan-level price adjustments (LLPAs)
What it is
Risk-based fees Fannie Mae charges on loans with riskier features, as a percentage of the loan amount. Lenders usually pass them on as a higher rate or as upfront costs.
Used for
Pricing risk: investment-property occupancy, higher LTV, lower credit scores and other features each add a charge.
What it means for you
Fannie Mae's matrix dated September 9, 2026 adds, for an investment property, 2.125% of the loan at 70.01%–75% LTV, 3.375% at 75.01%–80% and 4.125% at 80.01%–85%, on top of other adjustments. That is why investor rates run above the headline rates in the news.
Going furtherLimits on how many properties you can finance

Fannie Mae's Selling Guide caps the number of financed properties a borrower may have for second-home or investment transactions (10 when the loan is underwritten through its Desktop Underwriter system), and requires additional cash reserves as the count rises. Investors who outgrow those limits typically move to portfolio loans held by banks, commercial loans, or DSCR loans (next tab).

Practitioner depthOccupancy is not a box to fudge

Because owner-occupied loans are cheaper, some borrowers have claimed they would live in a property they intended to rent. That is mortgage fraud. Researchers at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York found that investors in the 2000s boom raised default rates "in part by apparently misreporting their intentions to occupy the property" (Haughwout et al., 2011). The honest path to owner-occupied financing on a rental is to actually live there, as in house hacking (INV-070).

Lending on the property's income

DSCR loans: can the rent carry the debt?

DSCR loan
What it is
A loan underwritten mainly on the property's debt service coverage ratio — its income divided by the loan payments — rather than on the borrower's pay stubs and tax returns.
Used for
Self-employed investors, people with many properties, and buyers who would not qualify on personal income alone.
What it means for you
These are business-purpose loans. Under the Truth in Lending rules, credit to buy a rental property that is not owner-occupied is deemed to be for business purposes, so the consumer mortgage protections that apply to home loans generally do not. Read every term: rate, fees, prepayment penalties, and whether the loan is recourse.
Maximum loan from income: payment ≤ NOI ÷ required DSCR, then loan = present value of that payment over the term

With the defaults — $18,000 of NOI on a $300,000 property, a 7.5% rate and a 1.25× coverage requirement — the income supports a loan of only about $171,600, not the $225,000 a 75% LTV would allow. The buyer would need about $128,400 down, 43% of the price. At 5%, the same income would support about $223,500. Income tests, not LTV limits, are what bind investors when rates are high.

Going furtherHow lenders define "income"

DSCR lenders do not all compute coverage the same way. Some use gross rent against the full housing payment including taxes, insurance and HOA dues; others use a net figure closer to NOI. Some count a market-rent estimate from the appraisal rather than an actual lease. A 1.0 ratio under one lender's definition can be a 1.2 under another's. Ask for the lender's formula and run it yourself.

Practitioner depthWhere the business-purpose rule comes from

Regulation Z (12 CFR 1026.3(a)) exempts credit "primarily for a business, commercial or agricultural purpose." The CFPB's official interpretation, comment 3(a)-4, says credit to acquire, improve or maintain rental property that is not owner-occupied "is deemed to be for business purposes," including a single-family house rented to another person; a property the owner expects to occupy more than 14 days in the coming year is not treated as non-owner-occupied under that rule. Owner-occupied rentals follow different tests (comment 3(a)-5).

Worked math

When borrowing helps, and when it hurts

Leverage adds two things to the simple picture in the first tab. The property earns income (its NOI, or cap rate times value), and the loan costs interest. Whether borrowing raises or lowers your return on equity depends on which is larger.

Positive and negative leverage
What it is
Positive leverage: the property's return is higher than the loan's cost, so each borrowed dollar adds to your return. Negative leverage: the loan costs more than the property earns, so borrowing drags your return down unless prices rise enough to compensate.
Used for
Deciding how much to borrow. Compare the cap rate (income yield) with the loan's rate.
What it means for you
At a 6% cap rate and a 7.5% loan, the income alone produces negative leverage: with no price growth, 25% down earns about 1.9% a year over five years versus 6.0% all cash. At an 8% cap rate and a 6% loan, 25% down earns about 13.6% versus 8.0%.
Return on equity ≈ rproperty + (rproperty − rloan) × (debt ÷ equity)

The formula shows the trade in its simplest form (an interest-only loan, one year). With 25% down, debt ÷ equity is 3. Every point by which the property's total return (income plus growth) beats the loan rate adds three points to your return; every point it falls short subtracts three.

With the defaults — $300,000, 25% down, a 7.5% loan, a 6% cap rate and 3% annual growth for five years — the leveraged return is about 12.6% a year against 9.0% all cash. With 0% growth it is 1.9% against 6.0%. With prices falling 5% a year, it is about −32% a year against +1.0%, and the equity shrinks from $75,000 to about $19,000. The year-1 cash flow is −$879: the owner feeds the property while waiting for growth.

Going furtherLeverage and volatility

Leverage multiplies the swings in your equity roughly by the ratio of value to equity: four times at 25% down, ten times at 10% down. Since 1928, annual US home price changes have had a standard deviation of about 6% (Damodaran data). A property that moves that much in a typical year becomes, at 25% down, an equity position that moves around 24% — more volatile than the stock market. Comparing a leveraged rental's returns with an unleveraged stock index fund (INV-071) compares two very different levels of risk.

When rates move

Three ways rising rates hurt leveraged owners

  1. Higher payments on adjustable-rate loans, and on any loan that must be refinanced: a balloon or a short-term loan coming due.
  2. Smaller loans. The same NOI supports less debt at a higher rate (previous tab), so buyers bring more cash or pay less.
  3. Lower values. Investors demand higher cap rates when rates rise, so the same NOI is worth less (INV-065). Falling values raise your LTV and can trap you in a loan you cannot refinance.
Going furtherDefending against rate risk

Prefer long fixed-rate financing on properties you plan to hold; avoid loans that must be refinanced on a fixed date; underwrite at a rate above today's to see if the deal survives; and keep cash reserves for months of negative cash flow. These cost return in good times. They are what separates owners who survive a rate shock from those who are forced to sell into it.

When values fall

Negative equity, recourse and foreclosure

Negative equity
What it is
Owing more than the property is worth, often called being "underwater."
Used for
Describing borrowers who cannot sell or refinance without bringing cash.
What it means for you
With 10% down, a 10% price fall plus selling costs puts you underwater. You can keep paying and wait, bring cash to sell, or negotiate with the lender. The ability to keep paying is what decides which.
Recourse and foreclosure
What it is
Foreclosure is the legal process by which a lender takes and sells a property after the borrower stops paying. With a recourse loan, the lender may pursue the borrower for any shortfall (a deficiency) where state law allows; with a non-recourse loan, it is limited to the property.
Used for
Allocating losses when a property is worth less than its loan.
What it means for you
Your loss on a leveraged property is not always capped at your down payment. Know which kind of loan you have and your state's rules before you borrow.
Going furtherStacking leverage

Using a home equity line of credit on your own house to fund a rental's down payment means the rental is financed 100% with debt, and both loans depend on the same local housing market. If prices fall, you can lose the rental's equity and your home equity at the same time, with a variable-rate line that can rise just as values drop.

2006–2012

The foreclosure wave, seen through leverage

Case study 1The national toll
What happened
The Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission reported in January 2011: "About four million families have lost their homes to foreclosure and another four and a half million have slipped into the foreclosure process or are seriously behind on their mortgage payments."
What it teaches
Many borrowers with little equity, adjustable rates or loans they could not afford had no cushion when prices fell and credit tightened. Leverage converts a price decline into a solvency problem.
Source
Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission (2011), The Financial Crisis Inquiry Report, Conclusions, p. xv.
Case study 2Investors at the peak
What happened
Federal Reserve Bank of New York economists, using credit-report data, found that "in states that experienced the largest housing booms and busts, at the peak of the market almost half of purchase mortgage originations were associated with investors." Investors "took on more leverage" and contributed to higher default rates, in part by misreporting that they would occupy the properties.
What it teaches
Leveraged investors amplified both the boom and the bust. When prices fell, investors with thin equity and no personal attachment to the property defaulted readily, adding to the supply of distressed homes and pushing prices lower still.
Source
Haughwout, A., Lee, D., Tracy, J., & van der Klaauw, W. (2011). Real estate investors, the leverage cycle, and the housing market crisis. Federal Reserve Bank of New York Staff Report 514.
Case study 3Phoenix, the Riveras' city
What happened
From the June 2006 peak to the September 2011 trough, Phoenix home prices fell 56% (Figure 66.6). A buyer who put 20% down at the peak would, by the trough, have owed far more than the home was worth. The index did not regain its 2006 level on an annual-average basis until 2021; it then rose 27% from June 2021 to June 2022 and fell about 5% from June 2022 to June 2026.
What it teaches
Local markets can fall far more than national ones, and stay down for years. A leveraged investor needs the income and reserves to hold through a local slump, because selling into one locks in the loss.
Source
S&P Cotality Case-Shiller AZ-Phoenix Home Price Index (PHXRNSA), via FRED (2026).

Decisions

How much to borrow

Scenario 1 · The Riveras

Marcus reads that "the rich use other people's money" and proposes a $60,000 home equity line of credit (HELOC) on their Phoenix home to fund 20% down on a rental. Elena is uneasy. Who is right?

MarcusLeverage builds wealth when the asset earns more than the debt costs. At a 6% cap rate and 7.5% loan rates, it does not on income alone; the plan needs price growth, in a city that fell 56% last time.
ElenaRight. With the HELOC and the mortgage, the family would owe more than the rental is worth on day one, plus the HELOC's variable rate. A downturn could hit their home equity, the rental and Marcus's or Elena's job at once.
The principle: borrow to invest only when the investment's expected return clearly beats the cost of the debt, and when you can survive the bad case.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers

If the Harpers buy a $250,000 condo as a rental, should they pay cash or put 25% down and keep the rest invested?

Leverage always helpsOnly when the cap rate beats the loan rate. At a 5% cap rate and a 7.5% investor rate, each borrowed dollar costs more than it earns.
Compare the spreadRight. Heading into retirement, a loan adds a fixed payment their income must cover. If the condo's cap rate is below the loan rate, paying cash (from taxable savings) is the higher-return and lower-risk choice.
401(k) cashLarge 401(k) withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income at their current high bracket. It is usually the most expensive source of cash available.
The principle: the cheapest money is the money you do not have to borrow, when the property's yield is below the loan rate.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

You buy a $250,000 property with $50,000 down. Its value falls 12%. Ignoring income and costs, what is the percentage change in your equity?

Loss = 12% × $250,000 = $30,000. Equity falls from $50,000 to $20,000: −$30,000 ÷ $50,000 = −60%. With 20% down the multiplier is 5.
Exercise 2

A property returns 7% a year in total (income plus growth). You borrow at 5.75% with 20% down (debt ÷ equity = 4). Using the leverage formula, what is your approximate return on equity, in percent?

rE = 7% + (7% − 5.75%) × 4 = 7% + 5% = 12%. If the property returned only 4%, it would be 4% + (4% − 5.75%) × 4 = −3%.
Exercise 3

A lender requires DSCR of at least 1.25. The property's NOI is $19,800 and the proposed loan's payments are $18,000 a year. What is the DSCR, and does it pass? Enter the ratio.

$19,800 ÷ $18,000 = 1.10, below 1.25: it fails. Payments must fall to $19,800 ÷ 1.25 = $15,840 a year, meaning a smaller loan or a lower rate.
Exercise 4

Under Fannie Mae's August 2026 Eligibility Matrix, what is the largest conventional purchase loan on a $300,000 one-unit investment property, in dollars?

Maximum LTV 85%: 0.85 × $300,000 = $255,000, so at least $45,000 down plus closing costs. A two-to-four-unit property would be capped at 75%.
