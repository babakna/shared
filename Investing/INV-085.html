Beneficiaries and Titling

For most families, the largest assets — retirement accounts, life insurance, the house — never pass under the will at all. They pass by a one-page form or by the words on a deed. This module explains every common way of owning and passing property, why the forms win over the will, and the small wording choices that decide whether grandchildren inherit.

The question this module answersWho actually receives each of my accounts and my home, and does that match what I want?

Start here

Three ways property passes at death

When you opened your 401(k), bought life insurance or opened a brokerage account, you probably filled in a beneficiary line in a minute and never thought about it again. That minute may have decided more of your estate than any will you will ever sign.

The ordinary instinct — "my will says who gets my things" — is right for a narrow slice of property. Everything else passes in one of two other ways: by contract (a beneficiary designation that the plan, insurer or bank must follow) or by title (the way an account or deed is registered, such as joint ownership with right of survivorship). Courts enforce those arrangements as written, even when the will says something different and even when the result is plainly not what the person would have wanted today.

⚠️

General information, not legal advice. Property and inheritance law is state law and varies widely. Federal law governs employer plans. Confirm how your own accounts and deeds are titled, and ask an attorney in your state before changing a deed.

The one-page form

Why the form beats the will

A retirement account, life insurance policy or annuity is a contract between you and the institution. When you name a beneficiary, the institution promises to pay that person at your death. The institution is not a party to your will and is usually not even allowed to consult it: for employer plans, federal law directs the administrator to follow the plan documents, which include your form.

Beneficiary designation
What it is
Your instruction to an account custodian, plan or insurer naming who receives the asset at your death. It is filed with the institution, not with your will. See beneficiary designation.
Used for
401(k)s, 403(b)s, IRAs, pensions, life insurance, annuities, health savings accounts, and many bank and brokerage accounts.
What it means for you
The form wins even if it is 20 years old, names an ex-spouse, or names someone who has since died. Review every form after any marriage, divorce, birth or death.
Contingent beneficiaryalso "secondary beneficiary"
What it is
The person or entity who receives the asset if every primary beneficiary has died before you (or disclaims).
Used for
Keeping the asset out of probate, and out of the plan's default rules, if the primary beneficiary dies first or at the same time as you.
What it means for you
A blank contingent line often means the account is paid to your estate, which drags it through probate and, for IRAs, can shorten the time heirs have to withdraw it (INV-091).

Spouses have rights in a 401(k)

For most employer defined contribution plans, federal law requires that a married participant's account be payable in full to the surviving spouse at death unless the spouse has consented in writing to another beneficiary, and that consent must be witnessed by a plan representative or a notary public (29 U.S.C. § 1055(b)(1)(C) and (c)(2)(A)). IRAs are not covered by this rule, although community property law (below) can give a spouse rights in an IRA funded during marriage.

Do not name minors directly

Insurers and plans generally will not pay a large sum to a child under 18. If Sofia and Leo Rivera were named directly as contingent beneficiaries, a court-supervised guardian of their property might be needed, and each child would receive the balance outright at adulthood. The usual alternatives are naming a trust for the children (INV-087) or a custodian under the state's Uniform Transfers to Minors Act, if the plan permits it.

Going furtherNaming a trust as beneficiary

Naming a trust lets a trustee manage money for young, disabled or spendthrift heirs. For retirement accounts, the trust must be drafted with the required-distribution rules in mind, because the trust's terms can determine how quickly the account must be emptied and how much income tax results. INV-091 covers the 10-year rule; ask the attorney drafting the trust to address retirement assets specifically.

Practitioner depthDisclaimers as a fix

A beneficiary can refuse (disclaim) an inheritance, in which case it passes as though the disclaiming person had died first. A disclaimer that meets the federal requirements (for example, made in writing within nine months, before the person accepts any benefit) is not treated as a gift by the person disclaiming. Disclaimers let a family repair some designation problems after death, such as a surviving spouse disclaiming so the account passes to children, but they cannot redirect money to someone the form or the law would not otherwise reach.

Two Latin phrases worth knowing

Does a grandchild inherit if their parent died first?

Suppose Ruth's form, or a will, leaves an account "to my children equally," and one child has died leaving children of their own. Do those grandchildren take their parent's share, or does it go to the surviving children? The answer depends on a few words most people never notice.

Per stirpes"by the branch"
What it is
A method of dividing property that splits it into equal shares at the children's level and passes a deceased child's share down to that child's own descendants.
Used for
Making sure each family branch gets an equal share, however many members it has.
What it means for you
Many beneficiary forms have a "per stirpes" checkbox. If it is not checked, a deceased child's share usually goes to the other named beneficiaries, and that child's children receive nothing from the account.
Per capita"by the head"
What it is
A method that divides property equally among the individuals in a class who are alive at the time. On a beneficiary form, it usually means only the named people who survive share the account.
Used for
Treating a group of people identically; in a variant called per capita at each generation (used by the Uniform Probate Code), shares that would have gone to deceased children are pooled and split equally among all their children.
What it means for you
Neither method is "right." The point is to choose deliberately, and to use the same method in the will, the trust and every form.
Going furtherWhen per stirpes and per capita at each generation differ

The two methods give the same answer when each deceased child left the same number of children. They differ when branches are uneven. With one living child and two deceased children who left one and three children, $600,000 per stirpes gives the living child $200,000, the only child of the first branch $200,000, and each of the three in the other branch $66,667. Per capita at each generation also gives the living child $200,000, but pools the other $400,000 and gives each of the four grandchildren $100,000. The calculator above shows both.

Practitioner depthDefaults in statutes and documents

Intestacy statutes pick a method for you. Pennsylvania's rule (20 Pa.C.S. § 2104(1)) divides at the nearest degree with living members and passes a deceased person's share "by representation" to their issue; Arizona passes to descendants "by representation" as defined in its probate code. Wills and trusts can adopt a statutory definition or spell one out. Account custodians' forms vary: some default to per stirpes, some to per capita, and some to the estate. Read the fine print on each form.

Owning with someone else

Joint tenancy, tenancy in common and tenancy by the entirety

How two names appear on a deed or account decides what happens when one owner dies. The differences are easy to miss, because every version simply looks like two names.

Joint tenancy with right of survivorshipJTWROS
What it is
Co-ownership in which, when one owner dies, the survivor automatically owns the whole asset. See joint tenancy with right of survivorship.
Used for
Married couples' homes and bank accounts; sometimes a parent and adult child.
What it means for you
It avoids probate, but the survivor takes everything regardless of the will, and adding a child makes the child a real co-owner today.
Tenancy in common
What it is
Co-ownership with no survivorship: each owner holds a share (not necessarily equal) that passes under that owner's will or by intestacy.
Used for
Unmarried co-buyers, siblings who inherit a house together, and investors.
What it means for you
Your share goes through probate at your death unless held in a trust or covered by a TOD deed where allowed. Pennsylvania's intestacy statute makes heirs who inherit together tenants in common (20 Pa.C.S. § 2104(7)).
Tenancy by the entirety
What it is
A form of joint ownership available only to married couples, with survivorship, in which neither spouse alone can sell or encumber the property.
Used for
Married couples' homes in states that recognize it. North Carolina provides that a conveyance of real property to spouses vests title in them as tenants by the entirety (N.C.G.S. § 41-56); Pennsylvania's intestacy law passes property inherited by a husband and wife to them as tenants by the entireties (§ 2104(7)); Ohio's transfer-on-death statute still refers to spouses holding as tenants by the entireties under deeds recorded between 1972 and 1985 (Ohio Rev. Code § 5302.22).
What it means for you
In some states it also protects the property from creditors of just one spouse. Ask how your deed is worded before assuming which form you have.

How joint property is counted for estate tax

For federal estate tax, a married couple's qualified joint interest (joint tenancy between spouses only, or tenancy by the entirety) is included at one-half in the first spouse's estate (26 U.S.C. § 2040(b)). For joint owners who are not married, the whole value is included except the part the survivor can show they paid for (§ 2040(a)). Pennsylvania's inheritance tax exempts property held jointly between spouses, but taxes a non-spouse joint account on the decedent's fractional share — half, for two owners — even if the second name was added only for convenience (PA Department of Revenue, REV-584).

Going furtherThe trouble with adding a child to the deed

Adding an adult child as a joint owner is the most common do-it-yourself estate plan, and one of the riskiest. The child becomes a co-owner immediately: the child's creditors, divorce or bankruptcy can reach the property; the parent may need the child's signature to sell or refinance; the addition may be a gift that requires a gift tax return (INV-088); the child may lose part of the step-up in basis they would have received by inheriting (INV-090); and the survivorship right can disinherit other children the parent meant to treat equally. A power of attorney, a TOD registration or deed, or a revocable trust usually does the intended job with fewer side effects.

Beneficiaries for everything else

Transfer-on-death accounts and deeds

Bank accounts, brokerage accounts and, in many states, vehicles and real estate can carry their own beneficiary. The owner keeps full control while alive; the named person has no rights until the owner dies.

Transfer on death (TOD) and payable on death (POD)
What it is
A registration that names who receives an account at the owner's death: POD for bank accounts and CDs, TOD for brokerage accounts. See transfer on death.
Used for
Passing accounts outside probate without making the heir a co-owner.
What it means for you
For Ruth's $60,000 of CDs and savings, a POD designation naming Anna (with a contingent) moves them outside probate at no cost, without Anna's name on the accounts today.
Transfer-on-death deedalso "beneficiary deed"
What it is
A deed, signed and recorded during the owner's life, that transfers real estate to a named beneficiary only at death. It can be revoked.
Used for
Passing a home outside probate without a trust or joint ownership, in states that authorize it.
What it means for you
It exists only where state law creates it. A document that "names a beneficiary" for a house in a state without such a law does not work.

For Ruth, that leaves three ways to keep the house out of probate: a revocable living trust (INV-087), joint ownership with Anna (with the drawbacks above), or accepting probate for the house, which INV-086 walks through step by step. The Riveras, Maya and Denise can use a TOD deed if they want one (Maya's Ohio affidavit can name the trustee of a trust for her son, Eli); the Harpers in North Carolina cannot.

Going furtherRecording rules matter

Arizona's statute says a beneficiary deed is valid only if it is executed and recorded in the county where the property is located before the owner's death, and a revocation must also be recorded before death (A.R.S. § 33-405). Colorado similarly requires recording before death, and gives the beneficiary no rights in the property during the owner's life (C.R.S. § 15-15-402). A TOD deed signed but left in a drawer is typically worthless.

Practitioner depthTOD deeds and debts

Property that passes by TOD deed avoids probate, but it may still be reachable for the owner's debts and, in some states, for Medicaid estate recovery. A TOD deed also has no mechanism for a minor or disabled beneficiary: naming a trust as the beneficiary solves that where the statute allows it. Name contingent beneficiaries on the deed just as on an account.

If you live in Arizona and eight other states

Community property

Most states follow the "common law" approach: what you earn and buy in your name is yours, subject to divorce and survivor rights. Nine states instead treat most property acquired during marriage as owned half by each spouse. The IRS lists them in Publication 555: Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington and Wisconsin. Publication 555 also notes that Alaska, Tennessee and South Dakota have laws that let couples elect community property treatment.

Community property
What it is
Property acquired during marriage by either spouse while living in a community property state, other than property that can be identified as separate. Separate property includes property owned before marriage and gifts or inheritances received by one spouse (IRS Publication 555).
Used for
Dividing property at divorce and death; at death each spouse can leave only their own half.
What it means for you
The Riveras' paychecks, 401(k) contributions and home equity built during marriage are generally community property, whichever name is on the account. It also brings a tax benefit at the first death.

Arizona adds a survivorship option: a deed to spouses can expressly create "community property with right of survivorship," so the surviving spouse takes the whole property without probate (A.R.S. § 33-431(C)). That combines the avoid-probate feature of joint tenancy with community property's tax treatment.

The double step-up

When someone dies, most assets they own get a new cost basis equal to their value at death (the step-up in basis, INV-090). For jointly owned property of spouses in a common-law state, only the decedent's half is included and stepped up. For community property, federal law also steps up the surviving spouse's half, as long as at least half of the community interest was included in the decedent's estate (26 U.S.C. § 1014(b)(6)).

Going furtherMoving between systems

Property keeps its character when a couple moves, which gets complicated: community property acquired in Arizona generally stays community property after a move to a common-law state, and property acquired in a common-law state is not automatically converted when a couple moves to Arizona (some community property states have "quasi-community property" rules for divorce or death). A couple who has lived in both kinds of state should have their titles reviewed.

Practitioner depthRetirement accounts in community property states

Federal law (ERISA) governs who receives a 401(k), so the spousal-consent rule controls at death regardless of community property law. For IRAs, which ERISA does not govern, some custodians require spousal consent in community property states before a married owner names anyone other than the spouse. A married person in Arizona who wants to name children from an earlier marriage on an IRA funded during the current marriage should get legal advice.

The Supreme Court on forms nobody updated

Three ex-spouses, three outcomes

Case study 1Egelhoff v. Egelhoff (2001)
What happened
While married, David Egelhoff named his wife as beneficiary of an employer life insurance policy and pension plan governed by ERISA. Shortly after they divorced, he died without a will. His children from an earlier marriage relied on a Washington statute that automatically revoked a spouse's designation on divorce. On March 21, 2001 the Supreme Court held that the statute was pre-empted as applied to ERISA plans, because it interfered with administrators' duty to pay the beneficiary named under the plan.
What it teaches
State laws that "fix" old designations after divorce may not reach employer plans at all.
Source
Egelhoff v. Egelhoff, 532 U.S. 141 (2001).
Case study 2Kennedy v. Plan Administrator for DuPont (2009)
What happened
William Kennedy named his wife Liv as beneficiary of his DuPont savings and investment plan and named no contingent beneficiary. Their divorce decree divested Liv of her interest in the plan, but William never filed a new form. At his death the administrator relied on the designation form and paid Liv. The estate sued. On January 26, 2009 the Supreme Court held that the administrator did its ERISA duty by paying the benefits in conformity with the plan documents.
What it teaches
Even an explicit waiver in a divorce decree did not override the form. The fix takes one page and five minutes: file a new designation after a divorce.
Source
Kennedy v. Plan Administrator for DuPont Savings and Investment Plan, 555 U.S. 285 (2009).
Case study 3Sveen v. Melin (2018)
What happened
Mark Sveen married Kaye Melin in 1997, bought a life insurance policy in 1998 naming her as primary beneficiary and his two children from a prior marriage as contingent beneficiaries, and divorced her in 2007 without changing the form. He died in 2011. A Minnesota law enacted in 2002 revokes a former spouse's designation on divorce. Melin argued that applying the later law to her earlier policy violated the Constitution's Contracts Clause. On June 11, 2018 the Supreme Court held that it did not.
What it teaches
For an individually owned policy outside ERISA, a state revocation-on-divorce statute can redirect the money to the contingent beneficiaries. The result still took years of litigation to reach.
Source
Sveen v. Melin, 584 U.S. 811 (2018).

Decisions

Filling in the forms

Scenario 1 · The Riveras

Marcus's 401(k) names Elena as primary beneficiary and nobody as contingent. If both die in the same accident, the plan would pay his estate. What should the contingent line say?

The children directlyBetter than blank, but a 7-year-old and a 4-year-old cannot take the money; a court-supervised guardian of the estate would likely be needed, and each child would receive their share outright at adulthood.
A trust for the childrenRight. The trustee manages the money for both children under instructions the parents wrote, and the trust can hold it past 18. The trust must be drafted with retirement-account rules in mind (INV-091).
BlankThe account would go to Marcus's estate, through probate, and the estate would receive it under less favorable withdrawal rules. The will reaches it only by that longer road.
The principle: never leave the contingent line blank, and never name minors directly.
Scenario 2 · Ruth

A bank teller suggests Ruth "just add Anna" to her $60,000 CDs and savings so Anna can get the money quickly. What would you choose?

Joint ownershipIt works at death, but Anna becomes a co-owner today, exposing the money to Anna's creditors. For Pennsylvania inheritance tax, half of a two-owner joint account is taxable at Ruth's death, and if the joint account was created within a year of death the full value less $3,000 is taxable (REV-584).
POD plus a power of attorneyRight. At death the accounts pass directly to Anna outside probate; during life, Anna can help through the power of attorney without owning anything. Pennsylvania still taxes the transfer to Anna at 4.5%, but it would do that either way.
The principle: use a beneficiary designation to pass property at death, and a power of attorney to help during life.
Scenario 3 · The Harpers

The Harpers own their Raleigh home as tenants by the entirety. They ask whether to add a TOD deed naming Ethan.

Record a TOD deedA document that North Carolina law does not recognize would not transfer the property and could cloud the title.
Plan for the second deathRight. Tenancy by the entirety already handles the first death. For the second, a revocable trust (INV-087) or a will with probate are the options, and North Carolina's probate process is the relevant comparison (INV-086).
The principle: a technique is only as good as your state's law.

Six things people say about beneficiaries and titles

Apply it

What beneficiaries and titling mean for each household

Name someone. Her 401(k) is new; a parent or sibling as primary and a contingent takes two minutes. Colorado allows a TOD deed if she buys a home later.
Contingents and community property. Each 401(k) names the spouse (federal law requires consent to do otherwise); the contingent should be a trust for Sofia and Leo. Their deed could use community property with right of survivorship.
Every form should name Eli's trust, never Eli. Maya's $290,000 401(k), $40,000 Roth IRA and group life policy, a TOD registration on her $40,000 brokerage account, a POD on her savings and an Ohio TOD affidavit on her house should all point to a third-party supplemental needs trust for Eli, 13. Money left to Eli in his own name could cost him Supplemental Security Income and Medicaid (INV-087, INV-102).
Check the 20-year-old forms. Pre-tax 401(k)s of $820,000 and Roth IRAs of $60,000 likely name each other, with Ethan as contingent. Their taxable account can take a TOD registration; the house cannot take a TOD deed in North Carolina.
Walter's name. After Walter's death in 2023, Ruth should confirm her IRA names Anna (and a contingent), that no account still lists Walter, and that her CDs carry a POD designation. The house needs a trust or probate.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

An account leaves $600,000 to three children per stirpes. One child died first, leaving two children. How much does each of those grandchildren receive, in dollars?

Three branches: $600,000 ÷ 3 = $200,000 each. The deceased child's $200,000 is split between two children: $100,000 each.
Exercise 2

Same family, but the form names the three children without a per stirpes election, and the plan pays a deceased beneficiary's share to the surviving named beneficiaries. How much does each surviving child receive, in dollars?

$600,000 ÷ 2 = $300,000 each. The two grandchildren receive nothing from this account.
Exercise 3

$600,000 to three children per stirpes. A is living; B died leaving one child; C died leaving three children. How much does each of C's children receive, in dollars?

Each branch gets $200,000. C's branch splits it three ways: $66,667 each. Per capita at each generation would give each of the four grandchildren $100,000 instead.
Exercise 4

A couple bought a home for $300,000; it is worth $420,000 when the first spouse dies. How much higher is the survivor's new cost basis if the home was community property rather than held in joint tenancy, in dollars?

Joint tenancy: $150,000 (survivor's half of the original basis) + $210,000 (decedent's half, stepped up) = $360,000. Community property: both halves step up, $420,000. Difference: $60,000.
