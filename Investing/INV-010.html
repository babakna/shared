Bond Types and Bond Funds

INV-009 explained how every bond works. This module is about which bonds to own. The US government, states and cities, companies and home buyers all borrow, and each kind of bond answers a different question: how safe, how taxed, how protected from inflation. Then comes the practical choice between owning bonds directly in a ladder and owning them through a fund.

The question this module answersWhich kinds of bonds fit my goals and tax bracket, and should I own them in a fund or a ladder?

Start here

Who is borrowing, and on what terms?

Most people's first bonds arrive inside a 401(k) fund labeled "core bond" or "total bond market," and that is a perfectly good start. But inside that fund are very different loans: to the US Treasury, to mortgage borrowers, to corporations. Knowing what is inside lets you decide whether the mix fits you.

Every bond answers three questions. Who owes the money (which sets credit risk)? How is the interest taxed (federal, state, both, neither)? Is the payment fixed in dollars or adjusted for inflation? Figure 10.1 sorts the main US bond families along those lines.

The benchmark

Treasury bills, notes, bonds and FRNs

Treasury securities are the US government's debt and the yardstick for every other bond. They are sold at auction, in electronic form only, in $100 increments, and can be bought at TreasuryDirect or through any brokerage. Interest is taxed federally but exempt from state and local income tax.

Because Treasuries carry essentially no credit risk, their prices move almost entirely with interest rates. That makes them the purest diversifier against stock crashes caused by recessions (2008 in INV-009), and the weakest protection against inflation surprises (2022). Brokers also sell STRIPS, zero-coupon securities created by separating a note's or bond's coupon and principal payments; they have the longest duration for their maturity.

Going furtherTreasuryDirect or a brokerage?

TreasuryDirect charges no fees and lets you buy at auction; its pages note you can hold each security to maturity or sell it before, and explain the steps for selling early. Brokerages also buy Treasuries at auction and sell them on the secondary market in the same account as your other investments. Anyone who expects to sell before maturity, or to hold Treasuries next to funds, will usually find a brokerage account simpler.

Inflation protection

TIPS and real yields

TIPSTreasury Inflation-Protected Securities
What it is
Treasury notes and bonds whose principal is adjusted for changes in the CPI-U. They pay a fixed interest rate every six months on the adjusted principal, so the dollar coupon rises with inflation. At maturity you receive the adjusted principal or the original principal, whichever is greater.
Used for
Locking in a return above inflation: funding future spending in today's dollars, such as early retirement years.
What it means for you
Held to maturity, a TIPS delivers its real yield plus whatever inflation turns out to be. Sold before maturity, its price moves with real interest rates, and can fall even when inflation is high.

Worked example (illustration). You buy $10,000 of a new TIPS with a 2.00% real coupon. Inflation runs 1.5% in each half of the first year (3.02% for the year). After six months the principal is $10,150 and the coupon is 1% of that, $101.50. After a year the principal is $10,150 × 1.015 = $10,302.25 and the second coupon is $103.02. You received $204.52 in cash, and your principal grew by $302.25.

⚠️

The "phantom income" tax. Internal Revenue Service (IRS) Publication 550 requires holders of inflation-indexed debt (other than I bonds) to report any increase in inflation-adjusted principal as income for the year, as original issue discount. In the example, taxable income for the year is $506.77, although only $204.52 arrived as cash. That is why many investors hold TIPS in individual retirement accounts (IRAs) and 401(k)s. TIPS interest, like all Treasury interest, is exempt from state and local income tax.

Breakeven inflation rate
What it is
The nominal Treasury yield minus the TIPS real yield of the same maturity: the inflation rate at which the two would end up paying the same.
Used for
Reading the market's inflation expectations (plus a risk premium), and choosing between TIPS and nominal Treasuries.
What it means for you
If you expect inflation above the breakeven, TIPS should win; below it, nominal Treasuries should. TIPS also insure you against the scenario you fear most.

Principal is adjusted using the CPI-U, and TreasuryDirect publishes each issue's daily "index ratio," the factor by which its original principal has been scaled. Deflation reduces principal, but the maturity floor protects the original face value, so newly issued TIPS carry more deflation protection than older ones whose principal has already risen. TreasuryDirect notes the coupon rate is never set below 0.125%; when the auction's real yield is lower, as in 2020–2021, TIPS sell above par so the buyer's real yield can still be negative.

Tax-exempt income

Municipal bonds and the tax-equivalent yield

Municipal bond"muni"
What it is
Debt issued by states, cities, counties and other governmental entities to fund operations and projects such as schools and water systems. General obligation bonds are backed by the issuer's taxing power; revenue bonds only by a specific source such as tolls or fees; conduit bonds are issued on behalf of a private borrower such as a nonprofit hospital.
Used for
Tax-exempt income. Interest is generally exempt from federal income tax and may be exempt from state and local tax for residents of the issuing state.
What it means for you
Munis pay lower yields than taxable bonds because of the tax break. They make sense only when your tax rate is high enough that the after-tax income beats the alternatives, and only in taxable accounts; in an IRA the exemption is wasted.
Tax-equivalent yield = municipal yield ÷ (1 − your tax rate on the taxable alternative)

The tax-equivalent yield is the yield a taxable bond must pay to leave you with the same income after tax. A 3.20% muni for someone in the 24% federal bracket is worth 3.20 ÷ 0.76 = 4.21% taxable. If the muni is also free of a 4% state tax (bought in-state) and the alternative is a corporate bond taxed by the state, it is worth 3.20 ÷ (1 − 0.28) = 4.44%. If the alternative is a Treasury, which states cannot tax, compare with the federal-only figure.

Going furtherThree tax wrinkles
  • Net investment income tax. Higher earners pay an extra 3.8% on net investment income above modified adjusted gross income (AGI) of $200,000 (single, head of household) or $250,000 (married filing jointly). The IRS lists tax-exempt interest as excluded, so for those households munis are worth even more. The thresholds are not indexed for inflation.
  • Alternative minimum tax (AMT). Interest on certain private activity bonds can be a tax preference item for the AMT: generally bonds issued after August 7, 1986, except those issued in 2009 or 2010, and for bonds issued after 2010 only a portion of the interest (IRS Publication 550). Muni funds disclose how much of their income is subject to it.
  • Not tax-free everywhere. Tax-exempt interest must still be reported on your return, and out-of-state munis are usually taxed by your state.

The Municipal Securities Rulemaking Board's free Electronic Municipal Market Access (EMMA) website posts official statements, annual financial filings and trade prices for municipal bonds; under rules adopted by the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), it became the official repository for continuing disclosures on July 1, 2009. Most muni investors are better served by a diversified muni fund than by a handful of individual bonds, because single issuers can be thinly traded and dealers are paid through markups.

Lending to companies

Corporate and high-yield bonds

Companies pay more than the Treasury to borrow, because they can fail. The extra yield, the credit spread, is compensation for expected defaults plus a premium for bearing the risk that defaults bunch together in recessions.

Moody's / S&P and FitchCategoryWhat it means
Aaa to Baa / AAA to BBBInvestment gradeBelieved to have a lower risk of default; issued at lower yields
Ba and below / BB and belowHigh yieldHigher estimated default risk; higher coupons to compensate

SEC, Investor.gov glossary: "Investment-grade bond" and "High-yield corporate bond."

The SEC's investor site notes that high-yield issuers may be highly leveraged, in financial difficulty, or small and unproven. In practice, high-yield bonds behave partly like bonds and partly like stocks: their prices depend on the company's fortunes, and they tend to fall in a recession, when stocks fall too. That makes them a weak diversifier for a stock-heavy portfolio.

Going furtherCall features and seniority

Most corporate bonds are callable, which limits their upside when rates fall (INV-009). Bonds also differ in seniority: secured and senior bonds are repaid before subordinated bonds and preferred stock in a bankruptcy, and common stockholders come last (INV-011). A "corporate bond fund" label can hide very different mixes; check the fund's credit-quality breakdown on its fact sheet.

Home loans, packaged

Agency mortgage-backed securities

Mortgage-backed security (MBS)
What it is
A bond backed by a pool of home mortgages. Homeowners' monthly payments of interest and principal pass through to investors. Agency MBS are issued or guaranteed by Ginnie Mae, Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac; Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac have been in conservatorship under the Federal Housing Finance Agency since September 6, 2008.
Used for
A large share of the investment-grade bond market; a core holding of "total bond market" and "aggregate" index funds.
What it means for you
The main risk is not default but timing. Homeowners can repay early, and they do so when rates fall, so an MBS holder gets money back just when it can only be reinvested at lower rates.

The Federal Reserve's review of Silicon Valley Bank described the agency MBS the bank bought as "low risk from a credit perspective" — and they were. The bank's losses came from interest-rate risk: it put a large share of its deposits into long-dated securities whose prices fell as rates rose in 2022 (INV-008 tells the rest of the story). Agency status protects you from defaults, not from duration.

How to own bonds

Bond funds versus bond ladders

You can own bonds directly, usually in a bond ladder, or through a fund that holds hundreds or thousands of bonds. They behave differently in one crucial way: an individual bond matures, and a typical bond fund never does.

With the defaults — $20,000 in four rungs maturing in years 3 through 6, at the Treasury yields of September 25, 2026 — each rung is $5,000, the average yield is 4.98%, and the ladder pays $995 of interest in each of years 1 and 2, then $5,995, $5,748, $5,500 and $5,251 of principal plus interest in years 3 to 6: $4,484 of interest in all. That is Jordan's college problem, solved on paper.

Going furtherTarget-maturity funds

Some ETFs hold bonds that all mature in the same year and then liquidate, returning cash to shareholders. They combine a fund's diversification with a ladder's end date; several can be combined into a ladder. Check the fund's yield to maturity and expense ratio, and the date on which it will distribute its assets.

Real history

When bond funds surprised their owners

Case study 1A credit fund that could not meet redemptions, 2015
What happened
Third Avenue Focused Credit Fund, an open-end mutual fund, had estimated net outflows of $1.1 billion in 2015 through December 9 — more than 145% of its remaining net assets. In November alone its Institutional Class NAV fell from $7.81 to $7.08. The SEC's order describes an ongoing reduction in the liquidity of its holdings and an imbalance between sellers and buyers. On December 9 the board adopted a plan of liquidation; sales and redemptions stopped as of December 10; on December 16 the SEC issued a temporary order permitting the fund to suspend redemptions.
What it teaches
A fund that holds hard-to-sell debt can be forced to sell at bad prices when investors rush out, hurting those who stay. Today SEC Rule 22e-4 bars an open-end fund from buying illiquid investments if more than 15% of its net assets would then be illiquid. Core bond funds holding Treasuries, agency MBS and investment-grade corporates are far more liquid than a concentrated credit fund.
Source
U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission. (2015, December 16). Third Avenue Trust and Third Avenue Management LLC; Notice of application and temporary order (Investment Company Act Release No. 31943); 17 CFR 270.22e-4.
Case study 2TIPS in 2022: inflation up, prices down
What happened
Inflation for 2022 was 6.45%, the kind of year TIPS are built for. Yet the 10-year TIPS real yield rose from −0.97% on January 3 to 1.58% on December 30. A real-yield rise of about 2.5 points on a security with a duration near 9 implies a price fall on the order of 20%, far larger than a year of inflation adjustments could offset.
What it teaches
TIPS protect the holder to maturity against inflation; they do not protect a fund's share price against rising real rates. Match TIPS maturities or fund duration to when you need the money. After 2022, buyers could lock in real yields near 2–3%, which is exactly the protection that was missing in 2021.

Decisions

Pick the right bond for the job

Scenario 1 · The Harpers

The Harpers want to add bonds to their $220,000 taxable account in North Carolina. Assume their 2026 taxable income stays above $211,400, putting them in the 24% federal bracket. A broker offers an in-state muni fund yielding 3.20% (an illustration); 5-year Treasuries yielded 4.98% on September 25, 2026.

Munis alwaysNot at these illustrative yields. The muni's tax-equivalent yield against a Treasury is 3.20 ÷ 0.76 = 4.21%, below 4.98%. Munis win when their yields are closer to Treasuries' or your bracket is higher.
Compare after taxRight. Run the calculator with real quotes whenever they buy. The answer changes with the yield gap, their bracket, and whether the net investment income tax applies to them.
High yieldIt adds stock-like risk to a portfolio already concentrated in 25 technology stocks — the wrong direction for their biggest problem.
The principle: compare bonds on what you keep, not what they pay.
Scenario 2 · Jordan

Jordan needs about $5,000 for each of Ava's four college years, starting in three years. Should the $20,000 go into a short-term bond fund or a ladder?

Short-term fundReasonable, and the price swings are small if its duration is short (check the fact sheet). But its value on the day each tuition bill arrives is not known in advance.
LadderRight, for a fixed schedule. Held to maturity, each rung pays a known amount on a known date, and Treasury interest escapes Ohio's income tax. The ladder calculator shows the cash flows.
The principle: known dates favor ladders; open-ended allocations favor funds.
Scenario 3 · Ruth

Ruth worries about inflation eroding her retirement. Her IRA holds a nominal intermediate bond fund. Should she add TIPS?

Reject TIPS2022 hurt TIPS prices because real yields rose from deeply negative levels. Buying after that rise means locking in the higher real yields that caused the loss.
Add TIPS in the IRARight. Her Social Security already rises with inflation; TIPS can do the same for the part of her spending her IRA covers. Babak's Retirement Income Compass compares a TIPS ladder with a bond ladder and an annuity.
The principle: put inflation protection where inflation would hurt you, and in the account where it is taxed least.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

An in-state muni yields 3.20%. You are in the 24% federal bracket and pay 4% state tax. What yield must a corporate bond (taxed by both) offer to match it, in percent?

3.20 ÷ (1 − 0.28) = 4.44%. (This simple sum ignores the federal deduction some taxpayers get for state taxes.)
Exercise 2

$10,000 of TIPS with a 2% real coupon; inflation is 1.5% in each half-year. Principal is $10,150 after six months and $10,302.25 after a year. What is the year's federally taxable income, in dollars?

Coupons: 1% × $10,150 = $101.50 and 1% × $10,302.25 = $103.02. Principal increase: $302.25. Total $506.77, of which only $204.52 was received in cash.
Exercise 3

On September 25, 2026 the 10-year Treasury yielded 5.17% and the 10-year TIPS 2.83%. What was the 10-year breakeven inflation rate, in percent?

5.17 − 2.83 = 2.34%. If inflation averages more than that over 10 years, TIPS come out ahead.
Exercise 4

A $20,000 ladder has four $5,000 rungs maturing in years 3, 4, 5 and 6, yielding 4.94%, 4.96%, 4.98% and 5.02%. How much interest does it pay in total, in dollars? (Hint: all four rungs pay in years 1–3.)

Yearly interest with all four rungs: 5,000 × (4.94 + 4.96 + 4.98 + 5.02)% = $995, for years 1–3 = $2,985. Year 4: $748; year 5: $500; year 6: $251. Total $4,484.
