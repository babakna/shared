Your Investment Policy Statement

Professional investors write their rules down before they need them. An investment policy statement records your goals, your target mix, when you rebalance and what you will do in a crash, so that decisions in a panic are made by the calm version of you. This module shows what goes in one, tests rebalancing rules on 98 years of data, and builds your own.

The question this module answersWhat are my rules, written down, for how this money is invested and what I will do when markets fall?

Start here

Decide now what you will do later

Most people invest without written rules, and in calm years that works fine: money goes in each month and nobody touches it. The trouble starts when markets fall hard, headlines turn frightening and every instinct says "do something."

In the story of the Odyssey, Ulysses has himself tied to the mast so he can hear the Sirens without steering onto the rocks. Behavioral economists call this precommitment: binding your future self to a plan made with a clear head. For investors, the mast is an investment policy statement (IPS). The CFA Institute, which sets standards for investment professionals, says an IPS "can offer an objective course of action to be followed during periods of market disruption when emotional or instinctive responses might otherwise motivate less prudent actions."

Anatomy

The parts of an investment policy statement

Investment policy statement (IPS)
What it is
A written document stating the purpose of a portfolio, its goals and constraints, its target allocation, how it is rebalanced and reviewed, and who is responsible for each decision.
Used for
Keeping decisions consistent across good and bad markets, and giving anyone who helps you (a spouse, an advisor, an adult child) a clear set of instructions.
What it means for you
One or two pages is enough for most households. Its value is less in the document than in the thinking it forces, and in rereading it when you are frightened.

The CFA Institute's 2010 guidance, Elements of an Investment Policy Statement for Individual Investors, groups the contents into four parts: scope and purpose; governance (who sets, carries out and monitors the policy, and how it is reviewed); investment, return and risk objectives (including risk tolerance and constraints such as liquidity, time horizon, taxes and legal matters); and risk management (how performance is measured, and the process for rebalancing to target). Figure 28.2 translates them into the nine sections of a personal IPS used by this module's builder.

Going furtherWhat an IPS is not

An IPS is not a forecast, a list of stock picks or a financial plan. The financial plan (Stage 4 as a whole) sets goals and saving; the IPS governs how the invested part is managed. It should not name "hot" funds or depend on predictions. Good IPS rules are ones you could follow without looking at the news: a target mix, bands, a calendar, and what to do if a band is breached.

Practitioner depthGovernance for a household

The CFA guidance asks who is responsible for setting policy, executing it and monitoring results, and how the IPS is reviewed and updated. For a couple, name who places trades and who checks them. For an older investor, name who can act under a power of attorney and point them to the IPS. For anyone with an advisor, say what the advisor may do without asking (rebalance within bands) and what requires consent (changing the target). Writing this down is what turns a personal habit into a policy that survives illness, death or a change of advisor.

Section 4

The target mix and the ranges around it

The heart of an IPS is the strategic asset allocation: the long-run asset allocation of stocks, bonds and cash chosen from your willingness, ability and need (INV-025). Markets will push the actual mix away from the target every day, so the IPS also sets ranges, or rebalancing bands, that say how far it may drift before you act.

Strategic asset allocationthe policy portfolio
What it is
The long-term target percentages for each asset class, set from your goals and risk profile and changed only when your circumstances change, not when markets do.
Used for
Anchoring every other decision: contributions, rebalancing and withdrawals all aim back at it.
What it means for you
Pick it once, carefully. A planned glide path, such as reducing stocks by a few points a year before retirement, belongs in the IPS too; a reaction to the news does not.
Rebalancing bandalso tolerance band or threshold
What it is
The allowed range around a target. With a 60% stock target and a band of plus or minus 5 percentage points, the range is 55% to 65%.
Used for
Deciding when drift is large enough to trade, which limits both risk drift and unnecessary trading.
What it means for you
Bands of about 5 points for large holdings are a common choice. Smaller holdings often use relative bands, such as a quarter of the target: a 10% target might range from 7.5% to 12.5%.

Section 7

Choosing a rebalancing rule

There are three families of rebalancing rules. Calendar: rebalance on a fixed date, such as each January. Threshold: rebalance whenever an asset class leaves its band, checked often. Hybrid: check on a calendar, act only if outside the band. Vanguard's research on rebalancing concluded that the purpose is "to manage risk and emotion," not to maximize returns; that "no one rebalancing strategy is dominant"; and that "selecting and sticking with a reasonable rebalancing approach is better than not rebalancing at all."

At the defaults — 60% stocks, a 5-point band checked each year-end, from 1928 — the rule rebalanced 35 times in 98 years, and $10,000 became about $26.5 million before taxes and costs. Never rebalancing produced about $69.7 million, because stocks beat bonds over the long run and the unrebalanced portfolio drifted to as much as 99.6% stocks. That is not a win for doing nothing: it is a different, far riskier portfolio from the one chosen. With a 10-point band, the rule traded only 15 times and ended with a similar $26.6 million; rebalancing every year traded 98 times for $25.7 million.

RuleStrengthWeakness
Calendar (e.g., each January)Simplest to remember and automateTrades even when drift is tiny; ignores big moves between dates
Threshold (e.g., ±5 points, checked monthly or quarterly)Acts when it matters, including mid-year crashesNeeds monitoring; can trigger in a volatile year more than once
Hybrid (check yearly, act if outside band)Few trades, a clear date, most of the risk controlCan let drift build during a long one-way market
Going furtherRebalance with cash flows first

Selling in a taxable account can create capital-gains tax. The cheapest way to rebalance is to send new contributions to whatever is below target, take withdrawals from whatever is above target, and make any necessary trades inside tax-advantaged accounts, where they are not taxed. Only then sell in taxable accounts, choosing lots with losses or small gains (INV-035, INV-037). Vanguard's paper makes the same point about minimizing transaction and tax costs.

Practitioner depthWhy the calendar-year test is simplified

The tester sees only year-end values, so it cannot catch a crash and recovery inside one year, and it resets the whole portfolio to target when triggered. Real threshold rules check more often and may rebalance only partway back (to the band edge, or halfway). Vanguard's analysis, which uses global stock and bond indexes and simulated return paths, reaches the same broad conclusion: no single rule dominates, while a reasonable rule consistently beats never rebalancing on risk control. INV-056 goes deeper.

Section 8

What you will do in a crash

A crash plan is the part of an IPS you will reread most. It should answer, in advance: Will I sell? (No, not because of the decline itself.) Where does spending money come from? (The cash reserve.) Will I rebalance? (Yes, by the rule.) Who do I talk to before any change? (A named person, after a waiting period.) Figure 28.4 shows what the plan has to survive.

A crash-plan checklist

  1. Hold enough cash outside the portfolio for near-term spending, so no one is forced to sell (INV-019).
  2. State the rebalancing action: when stocks fall below their band, buy them with bonds or new money.
  3. Add a waiting period, such as 30 days, and a reread of the IPS before any change the rules do not require.
  4. Name a sounding board: a partner, advisor or friend who has agreed to remind you of the plan.
  5. Use the decline: tax-loss harvesting in taxable accounts, or a Roth conversion at lower values (INV-037, INV-038).
Going furtherPrecommitment, explained

Precommitment works because the person writing the rule and the person following it are in different emotional states. In a calm market, loss aversion and recency bias are quiet; in a crash they dominate, and research shows measured risk aversion rises (Guiso, Sapienza and Zingales, 2018, in INV-025). The written plan carries the calm person's judgment into the frightened person's hands. Making the rule automatic, such as scheduled contributions that continue regardless, is stronger still.

Going furtherFive tests for a finished IPS
  1. Could someone else follow it? If a partner or adult child had to manage the money, would they know what to do?
  2. Is every rule checkable? "Rebalance when outside 55%–65%" is; "be sensible" is not.
  3. Does the risk section use dollars?
  4. Does it say where emergency money comes from?
  5. Is it signed and dated, with a review month?

Rules meet history

Three crashes, one rule

These cases apply a simple rule — 60% stocks, 40% 10-year Treasuries, rebalanced at year-end if outside ±5 points — to real market history.

Case study 1End of 2008: the rule says buy
What happened
A $100,000 60/40 portfolio at the start of 2008 ended the year at about $86,100: stocks $38,070 and Treasuries $48,040, a stock share of 44.2%. The rule required buying about $13,600 of stocks with bond money. Stocks returned 25.9% in 2009. Meanwhile, research on bank clients found that risk aversion rose substantially after the crisis, with trading behavior consistent with fear.
What it teaches
The rule asked for the opposite of what fear suggested, at the moment it mattered most. Without a written rule, most people would not have bought.
Source
Computed from Damodaran (2026), NYU Stern; Guiso, Sapienza & Zingales (2018), Journal of Financial Economics.
Case study 21973–1974: a two-year grind
What happened
Stocks returned −14.3% in 1973 and −25.9% in 1974, amid high inflation. A 60/40 portfolio following the rule (no trade was needed at the end of 1973, when the stock share was 55.4%) fell about 19.6% over the two years, to about $80,390 per $100,000; by the end of 1974 its stock share was 47.4%, and the rule bought about $10,100 of stocks per $100,000 started. Stocks returned 37.0% in 1975, and the portfolio was back above its starting value by the end of 1976.
What it teaches
Crashes do not always end in a year. A rule checked at year-ends waits through the first year, then acts; the crash plan's cash reserve is what makes waiting possible.
Source
Computed from Damodaran (2026), NYU Stern.
Case study 32022: when rebalancing does nothing
What happened
Stocks and 10-year Treasuries each fell about 18% in 2022. The 60/40 portfolio stayed at a 59.9% stock share, so the rule required almost no trade (about $50 per $100,000). It was back above its starting value by the end of 2024.
What it teaches
Some crashes offer no rebalancing opportunity at all. The crash plan's other rules — do not sell, draw on cash, wait before changing anything — carried the whole load.
Source
Computed from Damodaran (2026), NYU Stern.

Decisions

Following the policy under pressure

Scenario 1 · The Harpers

A year after the Harpers retire, stocks fall 30%. Their IPS says: two years of withdrawals in cash and short-term bonds, 50/50 target, rebalance if outside ±5 points. Tom wants to "go to cash until things settle." What should they do?

Go to cashThis is what the IPS exists to prevent. "Settled" usually arrives after prices have recovered; in 2008 a seller missed a 25.9% rise in 2009.
Follow the policyRight. The cash reserve was built for exactly this, so no stocks are sold low for spending. The rule buys stocks while they are cheap. The waiting period lets emotion pass.
Freeze spendingModest spending cuts in a bad year help (INV-076), but stopping all withdrawals is not required when a cash reserve exists.
The principle: in a crash, the IPS decides, not the headlines.
Scenario 2 · Monica

After a strong year, Monica's savings have drifted from her 60% stock target to 67%. Her IPS says "rebalance each January if stocks are more than 5 points from target, inside the 401(k) first." A coworker says that with only nine years to go she needs every bit of growth to catch up. What now?

Suspend the ruleA rule that is suspended whenever it is uncomfortable is not a rule. Nine years from retirement, with one income and a son who will depend on what she leaves, a large loss just before 67 is the risk she can least afford (sequence risk, INV-077). Growth she needs comes from saving and the catch-up, not from drifting into more risk.
Follow the ruleRight. Rebalancing inside the 401(k) resets risk with no tax. The rule was written for the moment stocks look best.
The principle: the rules that feel wrong in the moment are usually the ones doing the most good.
Scenario 3 · The Riveras

The Riveras are writing their first IPS. Marcus wants a one-line version: "Invest aggressively." Elena wants detail. What belongs in it?

One lineIt cannot be checked, sets no range, and says nothing about the college money whose horizon is much shorter.
Goal-by-goal detailRight. Each pot has its own horizon (INV-024). Naming who places trades and when they review together makes it a joint policy, not one partner's preference.
The principle: an IPS is only useful if every rule can be checked.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A $500,000 portfolio is 60% stocks, 40% bonds. Stocks fall 30% and bonds rise 5%. What is the new stock share, in percent?

Stocks: 300,000 × 0.70 = $210,000. Bonds: 200,000 × 1.05 = $210,000. Total $420,000; stock share 50%.
Exercise 2

Same portfolio. How many dollars of stocks must be bought (with bond money) to restore 60/40?

Target stocks: 0.60 × 420,000 = $252,000. Buy 252,000 − 210,000 = $42,000, selling the same amount of bonds.
Exercise 3

An IPS targets 70% stocks, of which 18% is international. What share of the whole portfolio is international stocks, in percent?

0.70 × 0.18 = 0.126, or 12.6%; US stocks are 70 − 12.6 = 57.4%.
Exercise 4

A small holding has a 10% target and a relative band of 25% of the target. What is the lower edge of its range, in percent of the portfolio?

25% of 10 points is 2.5 points, so the range is 7.5% to 12.5%; the lower edge is 7.5%.
