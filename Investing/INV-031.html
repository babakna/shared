Workplace Retirement Plans

For most Americans the workplace plan is the largest investment account they will ever own. This module sorts out the alphabet of plan types, gives every 2026 limit that matters, including the larger catch-up at ages 60 to 63 and the new rule that sends some higher earners' catch-ups into Roth accounts, and ends with the decision every participant faces: traditional or Roth.

The question this module answersWhich plan do I have, how much can I put in for 2026, and should my contributions be traditional or Roth?

Start here

The account your employer already opened for you

Many people first meet a 401(k) as a checkbox in their new-hire paperwork, or discover they were enrolled automatically. The default is often a small percentage of pay into a target-date fund. For a lot of workers that default is a decent start.

But the plan can do far more than its default, and the rules are specific enough that a few choices change the outcome by tens of thousands of dollars: contributing enough to collect the whole employer match, knowing the 2026 limits and the extra room after age 50, choosing traditional or Roth contributions, and knowing when the money can come out. Each plan type described here is a tax wrapper: the investments inside are ordinary funds (INV-012), but growth inside is not taxed each year.

What this module covers

  1. The plan types: 401(k), 403(b), governmental 457(b), the federal Thrift Savings Plan, solo 401(k), simplified employee pension (SEP) and Savings Incentive Match Plan for Employees (SIMPLE).
  2. Every 2026 limit, including catch-up and the age 60–63 catch-up, with a calculator.
  3. Roth versions of workplace plans, and the new rule requiring Roth catch-ups for higher earners.
  4. Traditional versus Roth, with a calculator.
  5. Getting money out: the 10% additional tax, the age-55 rule, the 457(b) exception and required distributions.

Which plan do you have?

Seven plans, one idea

Every plan below lets you set money aside from pay, or from self-employment earnings, with a tax advantage. They differ in who can offer them, how much can go in and a few withdrawal rules. Which one you have depends on your employer, not on you.

Elective deferral
What it is
The part of your pay you choose to have your employer put into the plan instead of paying it to you, traditional (pre-tax) or Roth (after-tax).
Used for
Measuring your contributions against the annual limit, $24,500 for 2026, which applies per person across all your 401(k), 403(b), SIMPLE and TSP accounts, even with two jobs.
What it means for you
Employer matches and profit-sharing are not elective deferrals, so they do not use up your $24,500. They count toward a much larger total limit instead.

Going furtherTwo plans, two limits: the 403(b) plus 457(b) combination

Because a governmental 457(b) has its own limit, a public school teacher offered both a 403(b) and a 457(b) could defer $24,500 into each for 2026, $49,000 in total, before any catch-up. Few private-sector workers have anything like it. Governmental 457(b) plans may also offer Roth contributions and in-plan Roth rollovers (IRS, "IRC 457(b) deferred compensation plans").

Age 60–63 catch-upSECURE 2.0, from 2025
What it is
A larger catch-up for workers who turn 60, 61, 62 or 63 during the calendar year: $11,250 in 2026 instead of $8,000 (and $5,250 instead of $4,000 in SIMPLE plans). In the year you turn 64 it reverts to the regular catch-up.
Used for
Letting late savers put away more in the years just before a typical retirement.
What it means for you
Plans may, but do not have to, offer it. Tom Harper turns 60 in 2029 and Rachel in 2031; if they retire at 62–63, each gets three or four years of it.

With the defaults, which are Tom Harper's, the calculator shows $24,500 of deferrals plus an $8,000 catch-up, $32,500 in all, flags that his catch-up must be Roth, and shows $47,500 of room left under the $72,000 total-additions limit for employer and any after-tax contributions. The next tab explains why.

Practitioner depthThe $72,000 ceiling and after-tax contributions

Section 415(c) limits the total that can be added to one person's account in a year to $72,000 for 2026, not counting catch-ups. Deferrals and employer money usually leave room below it. Some plans let employees fill that room with after-tax, non-Roth contributions; if the plan also allows in-plan Roth conversions or in-service withdrawals, those dollars can be moved to Roth soon after, the "mega backdoor Roth" (INV-032). A worker deferring $24,500 with a $12,000 match could, in such a plan, add up to $35,500 after tax.

After-tax option

Roth 401(k)s and the new Roth catch-up rule

Designated Roth accountRoth 401(k), Roth 403(b), Roth 457(b), Roth TSP
What it is
A Roth option inside a workplace plan. Contributions are made after tax; qualified withdrawals, including all the growth, are tax-free. A withdrawal is qualified once the account has been held five years and you are 59½, disabled, or deceased.
Used for
Paying tax now at a known rate rather than later at an unknown one. Unlike a Roth IRA, there is no income limit for contributing.
What it means for you
You can split your $24,500 between traditional and Roth in any proportion. Since 2024, designated Roth accounts, like Roth IRAs, have no required minimum distributions while the owner is alive (IRS required minimum distribution (RMD) FAQs).
The Roth catch-up ruleSECURE 2.0 section 603; in effect for 2026
What it is
If your Social Security (FICA) wages from the employer sponsoring the plan exceeded $150,000 in 2025, any age-50-plus catch-up you make in 2026 must be a Roth contribution. Your regular $24,500 can still be traditional.
Used for
Raising tax revenue now from higher earners' catch-ups. It applies to 401(k), 403(b) and governmental 457(b) plans, but not to SEP or SIMPLE IRAs.
What it means for you
The test uses only wages from that employer, so self-employment income and a spouse's pay do not count. Tom Harper's $170,000 means his $8,000 catch-up is Roth; Rachel's $90,000 means hers can be pre-tax.

If your plan has no Roth feature at all, ask the plan administrator how it is handling catch-up contributions for employees above the wage threshold; the answer determines whether you can make one.

The central decision

Pay the tax now, or later?

With a traditional contribution you skip income tax today and pay it on withdrawal. With a Roth you pay today and never again. If the tax rate is the same at both ends, and you contribute the same take-home amount, the two come out exactly equal: multiplying by (1 − t) before growth or after growth gives the same answer. The whole decision is therefore about which rate is lower, today's or the one at withdrawal, plus a few second-order effects.

Traditional: C(1+r)n(1 − tlater)   vs   Roth: C(1 − tnow)(1+r)n

With the defaults, $10,000 of pay a year for 30 years at 6%, a 22% rate today and 15% at withdrawal, the traditional account leaves about $712,000 after tax and the Roth about $654,000. Switch to "Both at the same dollar limit": when you can only contribute a fixed dollar amount, as someone at the $24,500 limit can, a Roth dollar holds more after-tax money, and the Roth wins unless the later rate is well below today's.

Going furtherTax diversification

No one knows future tax rates. Holding both traditional and Roth money lets you choose, each year in retirement, which account to draw from to manage your bracket, the taxable share of Social Security, and Medicare's income-related premiums (INV-042, INV-086). Many planners see that flexibility as worth something even when the rate comparison is a tie.

Practitioner depthWhat the simple comparison leaves out

Traditional contributions lower adjusted gross income (AGI), which can raise credits (the saver's credit, the child tax credit phase-out) and lower phase-outs today; Roth contributions do not. In retirement, traditional withdrawals raise AGI and can make more Social Security taxable, so the effective later rate can exceed the bracket rate. Roth accounts in workplace plans escape required minimum distributions, and heirs inherit them tax-free (subject to the 10-year rule, INV-095). An honest comparison models your whole return in both periods.

Access

When can you use it?

The tax advantages come with strings. In general, withdrawals from traditional workplace plans before age 59½ are taxed as income and hit with the 10% additional tax, unless an exception applies.

  • Loans. Many plans let you borrow from your own account and repay through payroll; if you leave the job with a loan outstanding, the unpaid balance can become a taxable distribution. Plans are not required to offer loans.
  • Hardship withdrawals. Some plans allow them for defined needs; they are taxable and usually subject to the 10% additional tax.
  • Rollovers. When you leave, you can usually leave the money, move it to a new employer's plan, or roll it to an IRA. Rolling pre-tax money into an IRA can complicate a later backdoor Roth (INV-032).
  • Required minimum distributions. Under current IRS guidance, pre-tax balances must start coming out in the year you reach 73 (Ruth, born 1958, in 2031); if you are still working and not a 5% owner, a current employer's plan can wait until the year you retire (IRS RMD FAQs). INV-088 covers the rules for younger savers.

Real law

Two cases that shaped today's plans

Case study 1Tibble v. Edison International (U.S. Supreme Court, 2015)
What happened
Participants in Edison's 401(k) sued the plan's fiduciaries in 2007 for offering higher-priced retail share classes of six mutual funds when materially identical, lower-priced institutional classes were available. Lower courts held that claims about funds added in 1999 were too old. On May 18, 2015 the Supreme Court unanimously vacated that ruling: under the Employee Retirement Income Security Act (ERISA), drawing on trust law, fiduciaries have a continuing duty to monitor plan investments and remove imprudent ones, separate from the duty to choose well at the outset.
What it teaches
Your employer has a legal duty to watch the menu, including its costs. As a participant you still choose among the options; the plan's annual fee disclosure lists each fund's expense ratio (INV-007).
Source
Tibble v. Edison International, No. 13-550 (U.S. May 18, 2015) (Legal Information Institute).
Case study 2SECURE 2.0 and the delayed Roth catch-up rule
What happened
The SECURE 2.0 Act of 2022 required catch-ups by employees with prior-year wages above $145,000 (indexed) to be Roth. Plans and payroll providers said they could not be ready in time, and the IRS gave an administrative transition through the end of 2025 (Notice 2023-62). Final regulations were issued September 15, 2025. For 2026 the threshold is $150,000 of 2025 wages.
What it teaches
Tax rules often arrive in stages: statute, transition relief, proposed and final regulations. The date a rule "applies" and the date its regulations apply can differ. For a question such as whether your catch-up must be Roth, check your plan's current notices.
Source
IRS IR-2025-91 (September 15, 2025); IRS Notice 2025-67.

Decisions

Three households, three plan choices

Scenario 1 · Maya

Maya is in the 12% bracket and expects her income to rise a lot. Her plan offers traditional and Roth 401(k). What should her 6% go into?

TraditionalDefensible, but the break is only $446 a year. If her rate in retirement is 22%, she will pay more tax later than she saves now.
RothRight, for most people in her position. Her employer's match will still be pre-tax money, so she will end up with both kinds.
WaitSkipping the match forfeits $1,860 a year, far more than the 6.8% interest on the loan dollars she would free up. Capture the match first (INV-020).
The principle: low bracket now, Roth; always capture the match.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers

Tom and Rachel can each contribute $32,500 in 2026. They are in the 22% bracket, with $820,000 already pre-tax. Tom's catch-up must be Roth. What mix makes sense?

All traditionalSaves the most tax now, but $820,000 of pre-tax money growing to 73 could produce required distributions that keep them in the 22%–24% brackets for life. Deferring at 22% to pay 24% later is a poor trade.
Max both, blendedReasonable. The years between retirement at 62–63 and the start of required distributions may also be ideal for Roth conversions (INV-038).
Match onlyTheir taxable account is taxed every year; the plans are not. At $65,000 of combined room, the tax-sheltered option is the better default.
The principle: with large pre-tax balances, the question is not just today's rate but the rate your future RMDs will face.
Scenario 3 · Jordan

Jordan contributes 6% to the 401(k) and puts another 10% into the discounted employee stock purchase plan (ESPP). Money is tight with Ava's college three years away. Where should the next dollar go?

More ESPPThe discount is real, but Jordan already has $56,000 of $140,000 in employer stock. More concentration raises the risk in INV-004's concentration calculator.
Match, then diversifyRight. Selling ESPP shares soon after purchase captures the discount while avoiding concentration; the tax trade-offs are in INV-035.
The principle: tax advantages are worth having, but never at the price of betting your savings on your employer.

Exercise 3

A single $10,000 traditional contribution grows at 6% for 30 years and is withdrawn at a 15% tax rate. What is it worth after tax, in dollars (nearest $10)?

1.0630 = 5.7435, so $57,435 before tax; × 0.85 = $48,820. A Roth with the same take-home cost at a 22% rate today: $7,800 × 5.7435 = $44,799.
Exercise 4

A 45-year-old defers $24,500 in 2026 and receives a $12,000 match. The plan allows after-tax contributions. How much after-tax money can go in, in dollars?

The 2026 total-additions limit is $72,000: $72,000 − $24,500 − $12,000 = $35,500.
