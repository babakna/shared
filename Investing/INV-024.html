Goals and Time Horizons

Every investment decision in this course — how much risk, which account, which fund — depends on two facts: what the money is for, and when you will need it. This module turns vague wishes into priced, dated goals, works out the monthly saving each one needs, and shows how to choose when the goals compete for the same dollars.

The question this module answersWhat exactly am I saving for, how much will it cost when I get there, and how much must I put away each month?

Start here

A wish becomes a goal when it has a price and a date

Most people already have goals: a safer cushion, a home, a child's education, a comfortable retirement. For many everyday purposes, a loose sense of direction is enough. Saving "as much as I can" and hoping it adds up is not a bad start, and it beats not saving at all.

But a loose goal cannot answer the questions that decide how you invest. "Save for a house" does not tell you whether the money belongs in a savings account or in stocks, and it does not tell you whether $300 a month is plenty or hopeless. Two numbers do: how much the goal will cost and when you will need the money. The time between now and that date is the goal's time horizon, and organizing money around separate goals, each with its own amount, date and approach, is called goal-based planning.

There is a third number people forget: prices keep rising. A goal that costs $40,000 today will cost more in ten years. Figure 24.1 shows how much more, using the actual inflation of the past three decades.

Write it down properly

The SMART test for a financial goal

In 1981 a management consultant, George Doran, proposed that a useful objective should be Specific, Measurable, Assignable, Realistic and Time-related. Later writers often swapped "assignable" for "achievable" and "realistic" for "relevant," but the idea is the same: a goal should be clear enough that you can tell whether you are on track. Applied to money, it works like this.

SMART goalDoran, 1981
What it is
A goal that states exactly what you want, a dollar amount, who is responsible for it, whether it fits your budget, and a date.
Used for
Turning a wish ("save for college") into a number you can plan and invest for ("$48,000 in today's dollars by August 2037, $360 a month, from Elena's paycheck").
What it means for you
If you cannot fill in the amount and the date, you cannot choose an investment for the goal. Start with a rough number; you can refine it every year.
VagueSMART version
"Build a cushion."$9,000, three months of essential spending, in a high-yield savings account within 12 months, $750 a month by automatic transfer.
"Buy a house someday."A $40,000 down payment and closing costs by 2032; about $510 a month at a 3% return in savings and short-term bonds.
"Retire comfortably."Replace $70,000 a year of spending in today's dollars from age 65, with Social Security covering part; save 15% of pay, including the employer match.

Monthly figures in the table are illustrations computed at modest assumed returns; use the calculator to rerun them for your own numbers.

Going furtherOutcome goals and behavior goals

An outcome goal is the result ("$40,000 by 2032"). A behavior goal is the action that produces it ("$510 on the 1st of every month"). You control behavior; markets control part of the outcome. Good plans write both, then review the outcome once a year and adjust the behavior. When markets are kind, you may reach the outcome early and can lower risk; when they are not, you raise the saving or move the date.

Another useful split is needs, wants and wishes. A need is a goal whose failure changes your life (an emergency cushion, a basic retirement income). A want is important but flexible in amount or date (a larger home). A wish is lovely but optional (a vacation home). Needs get funded first and invested most cautiously relative to their date; wishes can wait for surplus money.

Practitioner depthFloors and upside

Retirement planners often split a single goal in two: an essential "floor" of spending that must be secured (by Social Security, pensions, bond ladders or income annuities) and discretionary "upside" that can ride on a risky portfolio. The same logic applies to any goal: if a college fund must cover in-state tuition but would like to cover a private college, the in-state amount is the floor, invested more safely as the date nears, and the extra is upside. Writing the floor and the upside as separate SMART goals makes the risk decision almost automatic (INV-025).

When you need it

The date decides how much risk the money can take

Stocks have been the best long-run investment, and also the most likely to lose money over any short stretch. The question is not whether stocks are "safe" but whether they are safe for money you need by a certain date. Figure 24.3 answers it with 98 years of data: for every possible starting year, how often did each investment lose money over the following 1, 3, 5, 10, 15 and 20 years?

HorizonTypical goalsWhere the money usually belongs
Short: under 3 yearsEmergency fund, next year's tuition, a car, a planned down paymentInsured savings, money market funds, Treasury bills, certificates of deposit (INV-008)
Medium: 3 to 10 yearsA home purchase, a child's college in a few years, a career breakA blend: high-quality bonds with a modest share of stocks that shrinks as the date nears
Long: over 10 yearsRetirement, a young child's collegeMostly stocks, diversified, with the mix becoming more cautious as the date approaches

Notice what the numbers do not say. A 20-year horizon did not guarantee a good result: the worst 20-year stretch for stocks still grew only 2.4% a year before inflation. And the worst three-year stretch was brutal: $20,000 in stocks at the start of 1930 was worth about $7,700 three years later. Money you cannot afford to see fall by half does not belong in stocks when its date is near.

Going furtherA horizon is usually a range, not a date

College is four years of bills, not one. Retirement is 25 or 30 years of withdrawals. Each withdrawal has its own horizon: money for age 66 is a short-term need when you are 64, while money for age 90 is still long-term. That is why target-date funds and "bucket" approaches shift gradually rather than all at once (INV-050, INV-076). Treat each year of spending as a separate small goal and the risk decision becomes clearer.

Practitioner depthMatching the horizon with bonds

A bond's duration measures how sensitive its price is to interest rates, and roughly the average time until its cash is received. If a bond fund's duration is close to your horizon, a rise in rates hurts the price now but raises the reinvestment rate, and the two effects roughly offset by the horizon date. That is the logic behind holding short-duration bonds for goals a few years away and individual Treasuries or CDs maturing on the goal date (a ladder) for dated needs such as tuition (INV-009, INV-010).

Price the goal

Price in today's dollars, then convert

It is hard to imagine what a car or a year of tuition will cost in 2037. It is easy to know what it costs today. So price every goal in today's dollars, then let inflation convert it to the future amount you will actually pay.

Today's dollarsalso called real or constant dollars
What it is
An amount expressed in the buying power of money now. $50,000 "in today's dollars" in 2036 means whatever sum will buy in 2036 what $50,000 buys today.
Used for
Setting and comparing goals without guessing future prices; reading retirement projections, which are much easier to judge in today's dollars.
What it means for you
Price the goal as if you were buying it now. Then either inflate the price to its date and use normal returns, or keep the price in today's dollars and use returns after inflation. Both methods give nearly the same answer.
Future cost = Cost today × (1 + inflation)years     Real return = (1 + return) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1

Worked example: tuition three years away. The College Board reports average published tuition and fees of $11,950 for 2025–26 at in-state public four-year colleges. Four years is $47,800 in today's dollars. For a student who starts in three years, the four bills fall due in years 3, 4, 5 and 6. If tuition rises 3% a year (the published in-state price rose 2.9% from 2024–25 to 2025–26), the bills are:

11,950 × (1.033 + 1.034 + 1.035 + 1.036) = 11,950 × 4.572 ≈ $54,630

That is $6,830 more than the today's-dollar figure. Housing, food and books are extra, and grants often reduce the price actually paid (the Case studies tab shows how much).

Worked example: Monica's retirement budget. Monica Walker spends about $5,200 a month on essentials and plans to retire at 67, in 2035, nine years from now. In today's dollars the goal is easy to state: $5,200 a month, or $62,400 a year. At 3% inflation, the same spending in 2035 costs:

5,200 × 1.039 = 5,200 × 1.305 ≈ $6,785 a month (about $81,400 a year)

Both figures describe the same goal in different units; the error is to mix them. Her estimated Social Security benefit of $2,300 a month at 67 is an assumption the course treats as today's dollars, so it should be set against $5,200, not $6,785. Her withdrawals will also owe federal income tax, about $3,500 a year. The gap her savings must fill is $62,400 of spending plus about $3,500 of tax, minus $27,600 of Social Security: about $38,300 a year, or about $3,190 a month, in today's dollars. At a 4% first-year withdrawal that implies a target of about $957,500; INV-075 turns a gap like that into a savings target.

Going furtherWhich inflation rate should a goal use?

General inflation (CPI-U) averaged 3.04% a year from 1928 to 2025 and 2.52% from 1996 to 2025 in the course dataset. Individual goals can inflate faster or slower. Published college prices have at times risen much faster than the CPI; the College Board's latest report shows in-state public tuition and fees up 2.9% in a year before adjusting for inflation. Health care has often outpaced the CPI. For a goal more than a few years away, using 2.5% to 3.5% and revisiting the estimate each year is a reasonable approach; for a goal with its own price history, use that history.

Practitioner depthWhy the two methods differ slightly

Growing a nominal goal at the nominal return is exactly equivalent to growing a real goal at the real return, because (1 + r)n ÷ (1 + i)n = [(1 + r) ÷ (1 + i)]n. The approximation "real return ≈ return − inflation" is what introduces small errors: at 7% and 3%, the exact real return is 3.88%, not 4%. Level nominal deposits also differ from level real deposits: a fixed $300 a month buys less each year, which is why the calculator offers deposits that rise with inflation.

The monthly number

How much to save each month

Once a goal has a future price and a date, the monthly saving it needs is a standard calculation: subtract what your existing savings will grow to, then find the level deposit that grows into the gap. Money saved this way, for a known future bill, is sometimes called a sinking fund.

Sinking fund
What it is
Money set aside in regular amounts for a specific, expected future expense, such as a car, a roof, property taxes or tuition.
Used for
Turning a large future bill into small monthly amounts, so it never becomes an emergency or a debt.
What it means for you
An emergency fund is for surprises; a sinking fund is for things you know are coming. Keeping them separate stops the car fund from quietly draining the cushion.
Monthly saving = [Future cost − Savings × (1 + r)n] × rm ÷ [(1 + rm)12n − 1],   rm = (1 + r)1/12 − 1

With the default inputs — $40,000 in today's dollars, $5,000 already saved, 10 years, 5% return, 2.5% inflation — the goal will cost $51,203. The $5,000 grows to $8,144, leaving $43,059 to come from deposits: $278.95 a month. You put in $33,473 in total; growth supplies about a quarter of the final balance. Switch to deposits that rise with inflation and the first year's saving falls to $251.47 a month, rising 2.5% each year.

Going furtherWhat return should you assume?

Use a return that matches how the money is invested, and be modest. For money under three years away, held in savings or Treasury bills, assume a rate close to what those accounts pay now, and remember that it can fall. For long-term money in a diversified portfolio, many planners use real returns of about 3% to 5% for balanced portfolios. The course dataset shows that a 60/40 mix grew 5.15% a year after inflation from 1928 to 2025 and 100% stocks 6.78%, but those were unusually good results for the United States and are not a forecast. Assuming too high a return is the most common way a plan looks funded when it is not.

Practitioner depthTiming conventions

The calculator assumes deposits at the end of each month (an ordinary annuity). Depositing at the start of each month (an annuity due) multiplies the future value of the deposits by (1 + rm), so the required saving falls by that factor, about 0.4% at a 5% return. It also treats the goal as a single payment on its date; for a goal paid over several years, such as college, run each year separately or use the average date. For deposits rising at inflation i, the required first deposit is the gap divided by Σk (1 + i)k × A12 × (1 + r)n−k−1, where A12 is the future value of twelve monthly deposits of $1.

When the budget runs out

Choosing among goals that compete for the same dollars

Almost every household has more goals than money. The Riveras want a larger emergency fund, a replacement car, college for two children and a secure retirement. Funding all of them fully at once would take far more than they can spare. The answer is not to spread a little money across everything equally, but to rank the goals and fund them in order.

Four questions sort almost any list of goals:

  1. What happens if this goal is missed? Missing an emergency fund can mean high-interest debt or a forced sale in a crash. Missing a vacation means no vacation.
  2. How flexible is it? Can the date move, or the amount shrink? A car can be bought used or a year later; next year's tuition bill cannot.
  3. Can it be borrowed for? Students can borrow for college, at a cost; nobody lends you a retirement. That is the main reason planners usually put retirement saving ahead of college saving.
  4. Is there free money? An employer match, or a state tax deduction for a 529 plan, raises a goal's effective return and moves it up the list.

With the default inputs, a $1,500 monthly budget fully funds the first two goals (about $1,011 and $407 a month), gives the third only $82 of the $341 it needs, and leaves nothing for the fourth. The tool makes the trade-off visible: the household can move a date, trim a goal, find more money, or accept that the lowest priority waits. For the Riveras, the $41,000 sitting idle in checking would fund Goal 1 immediately and free about $1,011 a month for the rest.

Going furtherOne pot or many?

Keeping a separate account for each goal is a form of mental accounting. Economists point out that money is interchangeable, so labels can lead to odd choices, such as holding cash for a goal while carrying a credit-card balance. But labels also help people stick to plans: money in an account named "Car, 2029" is less likely to be spent. A practical compromise is separate accounts for goals with different horizons (the risk differs) and a single account for goals with the same horizon.

Practitioner depthRanking by the cost of failure

A more formal method gives each goal a "cost of shortfall" per dollar: the extra interest you would pay by borrowing for it, the tax benefit lost, or a subjective weight. Fund the goal with the highest cost per dollar first, until its marginal cost falls below the next goal's, then move on. With an employer match of 50%, the first dollars to the 401(k) earn an immediate 50%; credit-card debt at 22% costs 22% a year; a student loan at 6.8% costs 6.8%. This produces the same ladder as Figure 24.5, with your own numbers.

Real evidence

Three facts that change how goals are set

Case study 1The $400 question, 2025
What happened
The Federal Reserve's 2025 Survey of Household Economics and Decisionmaking found that 63% of adults would cover a hypothetical $400 emergency expense entirely with cash, savings, or a credit card paid off at the next statement. The share was unchanged for three years and below the 2021 high of 68%. Only 35% of non-retirees thought their retirement savings were on track.
What it teaches
More than a third of adults lack the smallest short-term cushion, and most non-retirees doubt their long-term goal. The two failures are linked: without a cushion, a surprise bill becomes debt or a raid on retirement savings. That is why the starter cushion is step 1 of the ladder.
Source
Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System (2026), Economic well-being of U.S. households in 2025, executive summary.
Case study 2Sticker price and net price in college
What happened
Average published tuition and fees at in-state public four-year colleges were $11,950 in 2025–26. But after grant aid, the College Board estimates the average net tuition and fees paid by first-time, full-time in-state students at those colleges at about $2,300 in 2025–26, down from a peak of $4,450 (in 2025 dollars) in 2012–13.
What it teaches
Price a goal carefully before you fund it. A family that plans only on the sticker price may oversave for college at the expense of retirement. A family that expects the average net price may be surprised: aid depends on income and assets. Most colleges post a net price calculator on their websites; run it for your own income before setting the goal.
Source
College Board (2025), Trends in College Pricing 2025, highlights.
Case study 3Goals set in 2020, paid in 2023
What happened
Consumer prices rose 1.36% in 2020, then 7.04% in 2021 and 6.45% in 2022, the fastest two-year rise since 1980–81. A goal priced at $30,000 at the end of 2020 cost about $34,180 two years later.
What it teaches
A goal set in dollars and never updated shrinks in real terms. Reprice every goal once a year in that year's dollars, and raise the monthly saving with it.
Source
CPI-U, December to December, as carried in Damodaran, NYU Stern (2026).

Decisions

Match the money to the goal

Scenario 1 · Monica

Monica has earmarked $20,000 of her $40,000 taxable account, all in broad stock index funds, to replace her car in about three years. A coworker says stocks have returned about 10% a year and she should leave it invested to "catch up" before retirement. What should Monica do?

Leave it in stocksThe average is not the risk. In the worst three-year stretch, stocks turned $20,000 into about $7,700, and a bad market could arrive just as the car does. Money needed within three years cannot wait for a recovery; her retirement money, nine or more years away, can take that risk instead.
Match the money to its dateRight. The car is a short-term need; holdings that mature when the bill is due remove market risk from the part of her savings that cannot move. Selling in a taxable account can realize a gain, but in 2026 her taxable income of about $56,810 leaves roughly $9,390 of room under the head-of-household 0% ceiling for long-term gains (INV-030).
Leave it in checkingSafe from markets, but not from inflation. Insured savings accounts and Treasury bills pay interest with similar safety; idle checking gives that away.
The principle: the nearer the date, the less the money should depend on markets.
Scenario 2 · The Riveras

The Riveras have $41,000 idle in checking beyond their $9,000 emergency fund, no 529 plan, and two children aged 7 and 4. Which use of the $41,000 fits a goal-based plan best?

All to Sofia's 529Generous, but it skips two higher rungs: the emergency fund and retirement. It also ignores Leo. A 529 is the right tool for college money, not the right first use of every spare dollar.
Fund goals in orderRight. A larger cushion protects two children and a mortgage; retirement cannot be borrowed for; the rest seeds two 529s with 11 and 14 years to grow. Each pot is invested for its own date.
Bigger houseA legitimate want, but it has no date or amount yet, and it would lock the money into a less liquid asset while higher-priority goals stay unfunded. Write it as a SMART goal and rank it.
The principle: rank the goals first; then each dollar has an obvious home.
Scenario 3 · Jordan

Jordan earns $62,000, has $3,000 saved and $28,000 of student loans at 6.8%. Her employer matches 50% of contributions up to 6% of pay. She can spare $800 a month. What order makes sense?

All to the loansPaying 6.8% debt is a guaranteed 6.8% return, which is good. But she would give up the match (a 50% immediate return on up to 6% of pay, $1,860 a year) and stay one surprise away from credit-card debt.
Match, cushion, then splitRight. 6% of $62,000 is $3,720 a year, or $310 a month, which earns $1,860 of match. The rest builds the cushion first, then attacks the 6.8% loans (INV-020 compares paying debt and investing in detail).
The principle: take free money first, protect against surprises, then weigh debt against investing.

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A goal costs $30,000 in today's dollars and is 12 years away. At 3% inflation, what will it cost in dollars of that year?

1.0312 = 1.4258, so 30,000 × 1.4258 = $42,773.
Exercise 2

A portfolio is expected to return 7% a year and inflation is 3%. What is the exact real (after-inflation) return, in percent?

(1.07 ÷ 1.03) − 1 = 0.0388, or 3.88%. The shortcut 7 − 3 = 4% slightly overstates it.
Exercise 3

You need $20,000 in 5 years (already in future dollars), have nothing saved, and expect 4% a year. What level monthly deposit reaches it? Use rm = 1.041/12 − 1.

rm = 0.0032737. (1 + rm)60 = 1.21665, so the annuity factor is 0.21665 ÷ 0.0032737 = 66.18. 20,000 ÷ 66.18 = $302.21 a month.
Exercise 4

A $50,000 goal (future dollars) is 10 years away. You have $10,000 saved and expect 6% a year. What level monthly deposit closes the gap?

The $10,000 grows to 10,000 × 1.0610 = $17,908, leaving $32,092. rm = 1.061/12 − 1 = 0.0048676; the 120-month annuity factor is (1.79085 − 1) ÷ 0.0048676 = 162.47. 32,092 ÷ 162.47 = $197.52 a month.
