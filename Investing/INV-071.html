Securities vs Real Estate

Few money arguments are louder than "stocks or rentals?" Both sides can point to someone who got rich. This module puts them on the same scale: the long-run evidence on returns, what a mortgage does to those returns, and the costs that rarely make it into the argument — time, liquidity, taxes and concentration.

The question this module answersFor my household, is the next dollar better spent on an index fund or on a rental property?

Start here

Why the argument never ends

Most people's experience of real estate is their own home, bought with a mortgage and held for years while prices rose. Measured against the down payment, that often looks like a spectacular return. Most people's experience of stocks is a 401(k) statement that bounces around. No wonder real estate feels safer and better.

Both impressions contain truth and both mislead. The home looked spectacular because of leverage — a small down payment controlling a large asset — not because houses outgrow stocks. And a rental's steadiness is partly because nobody quotes its price every day. To compare fairly, we have to compare like with like: total returns, the same leverage, and all the costs. Figure 71.1 starts with price alone.

Concept 1

Count the rent, and the race is close

An investment's total return is its income plus its change in price. For stocks, income is dividends. For housing, it is rent — received by a landlord, or saved by an owner who lives there. Price charts like Figure 71.1 leave the rent out.

Total return ≈ net income yield + price growth
For a rental: net income yield = net operating income ÷ price (the cap rate)
The rate of return on everythingJordà and colleagues, 2019
What it is
A study of total returns on equities, housing, bonds and bills in 16 advanced economies from 1870 to 2015. It found that "residential real estate and equities have shown very similar and high real total gains, on average about 7% per year." Housing's price gains were low — around 1% a year after inflation — but its rental yield was "considerably higher and more stable" than stocks' dividend yield.
Used for
Comparing the two asset classes on equal terms: unleveraged, before taxes, including income. The authors note that since World War II equities outperformed housing on average, "but had much higher volatility."
What it means for you
Across a whole country's housing stock, real estate has been a good investment, not a magical one. What you can buy is not the national average: it is one property, with its own risks and costs, which the study's national indexes do not capture.
Going furtherWhy index smoothness overstates a single house's safety

A national price index averages millions of homes, so one city's slump is offset by another's boom. A single rental has none of that averaging: a local employer leaving, a new highway, a roof replacement or a bad tenant can dominate its return. Index volatility is a lower bound on what an individual owner experiences.

Practitioner depthTransaction costs in the long-run comparison

Jordà and colleagues estimate that round-trip housing transaction costs average about 7.7% of value across 13 of their 16 countries (citing the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, OECD), which, if incurred every 10 years, lowers annual housing returns by about 0.77 points. For stocks they cite New York Stock Exchange costs of about 0.40 points a year over 1900–2001. They conclude the difference is "likely to be small" at typical holding periods — which is another way of saying real estate punishes frequent buying and selling.

Concept 2

A mortgage magnifies whatever happens

Most real estate is bought with borrowed money; most stock portfolios are not. That is the single biggest reason the two feel so different. Leverage does not change what the property earns. It changes what your equity earns, and it cuts both ways. In the formula below, LTV is the loan-to-value ratio: the share of the price that is borrowed.

Levered return on equity ≈ (unlevered return − LTV × borrowing rate) ÷ (1 − LTV)

This is the formula Jordà and colleagues use to lever up housing returns. With 75% borrowed at 6.5%: an unlevered 7% becomes (7 − 0.75 × 6.5) ÷ 0.25 = 8.5%; an unlevered 5% becomes 0.5%; and a year in which the property loses 10% becomes −59.5% on your equity. Leverage helps only when the property earns more than the loan costs.

Why stock investors usually do not borrow
What it is
Buying stocks with borrowed money (margin) is possible, but margin loans can be called when prices fall, forcing a sale at the bottom. A fixed-rate mortgage cannot be called because the house's price fell, as long as you keep paying.
Used for
Explaining why real estate investors can hold through price drops with leverage that would be dangerous in a brokerage account — provided their cash flow and reserves hold up.
What it means for you
A company's stock already contains leverage, because most companies borrow. Jordà and colleagues point out that a fair comparison either levers up housing or de-levers equities. Comparing a mortgaged house with an unleveraged stock fund compares two different risk levels.

Concept 3

The costs that are not in the return

A fund's return arrives without your labor. A rental's return is partly payment for work: finding and screening tenants, repairs, bookkeeping, and the occasional 11 p.m. call about a leak (INV-067). If you hire a manager, that cost comes out of net operating income; if you do it yourself, it comes out of your evenings. Either way, a rental's return should be compared with an index fund's after paying for the work.

⚠️

Illiquidity is a risk you feel only when you need the money. If you lose your job in a recession, an index fund can be sold at a loss; a house may not be sellable at all without a deep price cut, and its mortgage is still due. Keep the emergency fund separate from the investment (INV-019).

Concept 4

One building versus thousands of companies

A total-market index fund spreads your money across thousands of companies in every industry. A rental puts a large share of your wealth into one building, in one neighborhood, whose fortunes are tied to one local economy — often the same economy that employs you. INV-004 showed that concentration is the risk most worth avoiding when the reward for bearing it is not higher.

That low correlation is the real argument for owning some real estate alongside stocks: they have often not fallen together. Jordà and colleagues call the "low covariance of equity and housing returns" a source of potentially significant diversification gains. But notice the exceptions: in a banking crisis, both can fall at once, and the leverage on the house makes its owner's loss far larger than the index's.

Going furtherYou may already own plenty of real estate

For most homeowners, home equity is already the largest single asset. A total-market fund also holds publicly traded REITs (INV-069). Before buying a rental "to diversify," add up the real estate you already own. For many households the diversifying move is the opposite: more of the broad market, less of one local housing market.

Concept 5

Two different tax systems

Each side has powerful tax advantages, and they are different in kind. Real estate's are deductions and deferrals; stocks' are low rates, tax-advantaged accounts and ease of management.

The largest practical difference is the retirement account. A household that has not filled its 401(k) and IRA contributions gets a tax deferral or exemption on stock returns that no rental can match, and does it with no records, no passive-loss rules and no recapture. Rental real estate's tax advantages are most valuable to people who have already used those accounts, have incomes low enough for the $25,000 allowance or high enough to benefit from deferral, or qualify under the professional or short-term rental rules (INV-070).

Concept 6

Same cash, two paths

The fair comparison gives both paths the same money at the same times. The calculator below buys a rental with your cash as the down payment and buying costs. When the rental needs extra cash from you, the index-fund investor invests the same amount; when the rental produces cash, it is invested in the same index fund. At the end, the rental is sold and its selling costs and mortgage are paid off.

With the defaults — $100,000, 25% down on a $357,143 property, a 6.5% mortgage, gross rent of 7.2% of price with 40% going to operating costs, 3.5% a year home-price growth, 3% rent growth, 8% stock returns and 6% selling costs — the two paths end 20 years later at about $562,561 for the rental and $558,557 for the index fund: a near tie. The rental needs about $4,888 of extra cash in year one. The paths tie at 3.47% a year of home-price growth; at 2% the rental falls $165,143 behind, and at 5% it pulls $226,753 ahead. Without a mortgage, the same rental ends at $421,067 against $466,096.

Real data

What leverage did in the housing bust, and what the long run says

Case study 1The US housing bust, 2006–2011
What happened
US home prices fell in every calendar year from 2007 through 2011, a cumulative decline of about 26%. Over the same five years the S&P 500 with dividends, despite its 36.6% loss in 2008, ended down only about 1%.
What it teaches
Housing's low volatility is real on average and can fail in a credit crisis. With 20% down, a 26% price fall is a total loss of equity on paper, and an owner who had to sell or refinance in that window had no way to wait it out.
Source
Damodaran, A. (2026). Historical returns on stocks, bonds and bills: 1928–2025 [Data set], NYU Stern; computed.
Case study 2The rate of return on everything, 1870–2015
What happened
Across 16 advanced economies, housing and equities earned similar real total returns of about 7% a year. Housing's capital gains were only about 1% a year after inflation; its higher, steadier rental yield made up the difference. Since World War II, equities outperformed on average, with much higher volatility.
What it teaches
The honest answer to "stocks or real estate?" is that both have rewarded patient owners. The choice between them turns on the things this module lists — leverage, effort, liquidity, taxes and concentration — not on one having a much higher long-run return.
Source
Jordà, Ò., Knoll, K., Kuvshinov, D., Schularick, M., & Taylor, A. M. (2019). The rate of return on everything, 1870–2015. Quarterly Journal of Economics, 134(3), 1225–1298; NBER Working Paper 24112.

Decisions

Three households, one question

Scenario 1 · The Riveras

The Riveras have $41,000 idle in checking and wonder whether to use it as a down payment on a small rental or invest it. They have a $9,000 emergency fund and no 529 yet.

Buy now$41,000 would cover a small down payment and closing costs, leaving no reserves for the rental or their family. Their own home is already a large, leveraged real estate position.
Base first, then compareRight. A landlord without reserves is a forced seller in the first bad year. After the base is built, the calculator can compare the two paths with their real numbers.
All into the 401(k)sBetter than idle cash, but it skips the emergency fund and education savings, and their six overlapping funds need consolidating first (INV-004).
The principle: build the base before choosing between investments.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers

The Harpers are tempted to sell part of their $220,000 of technology stocks to buy a rental "to diversify." They plan to retire in five or six years.

Swap into a rentalThat trades one concentration (25 tech stocks) for another (one building), triggers capital gains tax, and adds landlord work just as they plan to stop working. Their passive losses would be suspended at their income.
Diversify in the market firstRight. Broad funds cure the concentration cheaply and keep the money liquid for the retirement transition (INV-035, INV-037).
The principle: real estate diversifies a portfolio of broad funds; it does not fix a concentrated one.
Scenario 3 · Ruth

Ruth's friend says she should turn her paid-off Pittsburgh house into a rental and move to an apartment, "because real estate always goes up."

Become a landlordPossible, but at 68 she would take on repairs, tenants and one large illiquid asset, and her apartment rent would offset much of the income.
Compare the real alternativesRight. Home prices have not "always gone up" (2007–2011). If she sells within about three years of moving out she keeps the exclusion; after that, the gain becomes taxable (INV-068).
The principle: compare real alternatives with all their costs, not slogans.

An honest conclusion, household by household

Securities now; real estate through her own home later. Her 50% employer match on up to 6% of pay is a return no rental can match, and her 6.8% loans come next. When she buys, house hacking (INV-070) combines her home and her first rental.
They already own real estate. About $110,000 of equity in their Phoenix home is a leveraged property position. The next dollars go to reserves, 529s for Sofia and Leo, and consolidated 401(k) funds.
Liquidity wins. At 58, on one income, Monica already holds $215,000 of home equity in one Columbus house and needs money she can sell in pieces. Her next dollars belong in her emergency fund, catch-up contributions and Eli's planning (INV-102), not a rental.
A genuine choice, if they want the work. They are the one household with the cash, the diversified retirement accounts and the income to own a rental comfortably. The trade-off is time in their first retirement years, and suspended losses until they retire or sell.
Keep it simple and liquid. Her paid-off home is her real estate. Her IRA, Social Security and CDs should fund retirement without tenants; a REIT fund inside the IRA is the easy way to hold more property if she wants it.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A property earns 7% a year unlevered. You borrow 75% of its price at 6.5%. What is the approximate return on your equity, in percent?

(7 − 4.875) ÷ 0.25 = 2.125 ÷ 0.25 = 8.5%.
Exercise 2

Same loan, but the property loses 10% in a year. What is the approximate return on your equity, in percent?

(−10 − 4.875) ÷ 0.25 = −59.5%. The same leverage that added 1.5 points in a good year cost almost 50 points in a bad one.
Exercise 3

A rental's gross rent is 7.2% of its price per year, and 40% of rent goes to operating costs and vacancy. What is its cap rate, in percent?

Net operating income ÷ price = 7.2% × 0.6 = 4.32%. Add expected price growth to estimate the unlevered total return.
Exercise 4

Round-trip costs of buying and selling a home are 7.7% of value. Spread over a 10-year holding period, about how many percentage points a year is that?

7.7 ÷ 10 = 0.77 points a year, the figure Jordà and colleagues use. Hold for 5 years and it doubles.
