Widowhood and Survivor Planning

Losing a spouse is among the hardest things a person lives through, and it arrives with paperwork, deadlines and decisions that do not wait for grief to ease. This module separates what must be done soon from what should wait, explains survivor Social Security, shows why taxes often rise for a surviving spouse, and walks through the retirement account and titling choices that follow — using Ruth's experience after Walter's death.

The question this module answersWhat do I need to do after a spouse dies, what can wait, and how do I protect my finances for the years alone?

Start here

Grief first, decisions in order

If you are reading this soon after a loss, the most important message is this: very few financial decisions have to be made right away. A handful of tasks are urgent. Most of the large choices — selling a home, moving investments, buying products — are better made months later.

Many married people will one day be widowed, and it most often happens in retirement, when the household's income is already fixed. Ruth Kowalski's husband Walter died in 2023. She kept the house, receives the higher of their two Social Security benefits as a survivor ($2,900 a month), and holds $780,000 in a traditional individual retirement account (IRA). Her story runs through this module: what she had to do, what she was right to wait on, and the tax surprise that arrived the year after.

A checklist

The first 90 days

Nothing on this list needs to be done in a single day, and a trusted friend or adult child can handle much of it. The goal is to keep the household running, claim what is owed, and avoid mistakes.

Five items people miss

  • Employer benefits. Call the deceased spouse's employer or former employer: group life insurance, a pension with survivor payments, a 401(k), and sometimes a final paycheck.
  • Medicare premiums. Medicare's income-related monthly adjustment amounts (IRMAA), surcharges on Part B and Part D premiums, are based on the tax return from two years earlier, when the couple's income was combined. SSA treats the death of a spouse as a life-changing event: file Form SSA-44 to ask for a lower surcharge.
  • Credit cards. Know whether each card is a joint account or your spouse's account with you as an authorized user; the issuer's rules for each differ. Call before a card you rely on stops working.
  • Digital accounts and autopays. Subscriptions, utilities and insurance often charge the deceased spouse's card or account.
  • Your own beneficiaries. Your accounts probably name your late spouse. Update them now; the default may send the money through probate.
Going furtherWho does what: the executor and the survivor

The executor named in the will (often the surviving spouse) handles the estate: gathering assets in the deceased's name alone, paying final bills and taxes, and distributing what is left. Much of a married couple's property usually passes outside the will — joint accounts with right of survivorship, retirement accounts and life insurance with named beneficiaries — directly to the survivor. INV-086 covers probate and INV-085 beneficiaries and titling.

The largest benefit many widows receive

Survivor benefits from Social Security

Survivor benefit
What it is
A monthly Social Security payment to the family of a worker who died. A widow or widower can receive up to 100% of the deceased spouse's benefit. See survivor benefit.
Used for
Replacing part of the income a household loses at a death. For a retired couple, the survivor keeps the larger of the two checks; the smaller one ends.
What it means for you
SSA pays the higher of your own benefit or the survivor benefit, not both. That is why a couple's decision about when the higher earner claims affects the survivor for decades.

SSA's eligibility rules for a surviving spouse: you are 60 or older (50 to 59 if you have a disability), you were married at least nine months before your spouse's death, and you did not remarry before 60 (50 if disabled). You may be eligible at any age and regardless of how long you were married if you are caring for the deceased's child. A former spouse married at least 10 years may also qualify. Children under 18 (or up to 19 in high school, or disabled before 22) generally receive 75% of the parent's benefit, subject to the family maximum.

The switching strategy

Because survivor and retirement benefits are separate entitlements, SSA says you can "start with Survivor benefits and then change to Retirement at age 70 when that payment is highest" — or the reverse, taking your own reduced retirement benefit first and switching to a full survivor benefit later. A widow whose own benefit at 70 would exceed the survivor benefit can collect the survivor check in the meantime. The right order depends on the two benefit amounts, which SSA or a tool such as Babak's Social Security Family Planner can compare.

Survivor benefit ≈
deceased's benefit × claiming-age share
(71.5% at 60, 100% at full retirement age)
Going furtherWhy the higher earner's claiming age matters most

When one spouse dies, the survivor keeps the larger benefit. If the higher earner claimed early, that reduced benefit is what the survivor inherits (subject to special minimums). If the higher earner delayed to 70 and earned delayed retirement credits, the survivor inherits the larger amount. That is why INV-078 treats the higher earner's claiming decision as a joint decision: it is life insurance for the surviving spouse. Ruth's $2,900 reflects the benefit Walter had earned; had Walter claimed earlier or later, her check would be different for the rest of her life.

The widow's penalty

Filing status after a death, and why taxes often rise

Filing status in the years after a death
What it is
For the year a spouse dies, the survivor is considered married for the whole year and can file jointly (IRS Publication 501). After that, the survivor files as single, or as head of household with a qualifying dependent, unless they qualify as a qualifying surviving spouse.
Used for
Setting the brackets and standard deduction. The joint return for the year of death is usually the last year the survivor gets the wider joint brackets.
What it means for you
Ruth filed jointly for 2023, the year Walter died. From 2024 she files as single. The same kinds of income now meet narrower brackets.
Qualifying surviving spouse
What it is
A filing status that lets a widow or widower use the joint brackets and standard deduction for the two tax years after the year of death, if they have not remarried, maintain a home that is the main home of a dependent child or stepchild, pay more than half the cost of that home, and could have filed jointly in the year of death (26 U.S.C. 2(a)).
Used for
Softening the tax change for young widows and widowers raising children.
What it means for you
The qualifying surviving spouse status does not apply to most retired widows, including Ruth, because they have no dependent child at home.

The "widow's penalty" is the combination: income usually falls after a death, but tax often falls less, or even rises, because the survivor now uses single-filer rules. Every threshold that matters to a retiree is smaller for a single filer.

Practitioner depthPlanning around the penalty

Couples can prepare while both are alive. Partial Roth conversions in the years when the joint brackets are available move future required withdrawals out of the survivor's single-filer years (INV-038). The year of death itself is often a good year for a conversion, because the survivor can still file jointly. Qualified charitable distributions after age 70½ lower adjusted gross income for charitably inclined survivors (INV-039). And because the senior deduction ends after 2028 under current law, Ruth's tax on the same income would rise to about $4,946 in a year without it.

IRAs, 401(k)s and cost basis

A surviving spouse's choices for retirement accounts

A spouse who inherits an IRA or 401(k) has more options than any other beneficiary. The best choice depends mainly on the survivor's age and whether they need the money before 59½.

Spousal rollovertreating an inherited IRA as your own
What it is
A surviving spouse who is the sole beneficiary of an IRA may elect to treat it as their own, or roll it into their own IRA. A spouse can also roll over a distribution from a spouse's 401(k) (IRS Publication 590-B; IRS Retirement topics: Beneficiary).
Used for
Consolidating accounts, naming your own beneficiaries, and delaying required minimum distributions (RMDs) until your own required beginning age — 73 for people born in 1958, like Ruth.
What it means for you
Once it is your own IRA, withdrawals before 59½ can owe the 10% additional tax. A younger widow who needs cash should usually keep the account as an inherited (beneficiary) IRA at first, because distributions after the owner's death are exempt from the 10% tax (IRS Publication 590-B).

New cost basis for taxable assets

When a person dies, the assets included in their estate generally receive a new cost basis equal to their value at death: the step-up in basis. For property a married couple owned jointly with right of survivorship, only the deceased spouse's half is included in their estate (26 U.S.C. 2040(b)), so only that half is stepped up. In the nine community property states, both halves of community property receive a new basis at the first death (26 U.S.C. 1014(b)(6)). Ask the brokerage to record the new basis now; it can save large capital gains tax when the survivor sells.

Going furtherPensions and annuities

A pension paid as a joint-and-survivor annuity continues to the surviving spouse at the percentage the couple chose at retirement, often 50%, 75% or 100%. A single-life pension simply stops. Annuity contracts have their own death benefit and continuation rules; call each company with a death certificate. INV-080 covers pension choices, including why the survivor option matters at the moment of retirement.

Paperwork with consequences

Retitling, the home and the portability election

After a death, each asset passes according to how it was owned. The survivor's job is to move everything into their own name and then decide who will receive it next.

The two-year home-sale window

A married couple can exclude up to $500,000 of gain on the sale of their main home; a single person, $250,000. A surviving spouse who has not remarried keeps the $500,000 limit if the home is sold no later than two years after the date of death, provided the couple met the ownership and use requirements just before the death (26 U.S.C. 121(b)(4)). Combined with the step-up on the deceased spouse's share, this often means little or no tax on a sale inside two years. Walter died in 2023, so that window closed for Ruth in 2025 — which is fine, because she wants to stay.

Portability: a filing that can matter decades later

Portability of the estate tax exclusion
What it is
An election, made on a federal estate tax return (Form 706) for the first spouse to die, that lets the survivor add the unused part of the deceased spouse's exclusion — the deceased spousal unused exclusion, or DSUE, amount — to their own. See portability.
Used for
Sheltering a larger estate at the second death. The basic exclusion is $15,000,000 per person for 2026 (P.L. 119-21; Rev. Proc. 2025-32).
What it means for you
Estates not otherwise required to file can use a simplified method to make the election up to the fifth anniversary of the death (Rev. Proc. 2022-32). Ruth's estate of about $1.2 million is far below the exclusion, so she likely does not need it, but survivors of larger estates, or those worried about future law changes, should ask an estate attorney before the window closes.

Protecting the future

Avoiding rushed decisions

A new widow or widower is often approached quickly: by relatives with requests, by salespeople with products, and by people who read obituaries looking for targets. Money may also arrive in lump sums — life insurance, a retirement account — at a time when concentration and judgment are strained by grief.

Red flags after a death

  • Pressure to act now on an investment or insurance product, especially one with surrender charges (INV-007 showed how a 2.5% annual charge compounds).
  • Callers claiming a debt the deceased owed and asking you to pay personally. Ask for everything in writing, and before paying from your own money ask the executor or an attorney whether you actually owe it; debts of the deceased are normally dealt with through the estate.
  • New "friends" offering to manage money. Romance and confidence scams cost people 60 and over $584 million in reported losses in 2025, and investment scams $3.5 billion (FBI IC3).
  • Family requests for loans or early inheritances before the survivor has a new budget.
A decision-free period
What it is
A self-imposed stretch, often suggested as six months to a year, during which the survivor makes no irreversible financial decisions that are not required.
Used for
Giving the survivor time to learn the household's finances, build a single-person budget and grieve.
What it means for you
It is not doing nothing: benefits are claimed, bills are paid and cash is safe. It is refusing to be rushed into anything else.

Real law and one illustration

Three stories behind the rules

Case study 1A five-year window for portability (2022)
What happened
The IRS had received many requests for extra time to make portability elections from estates of people who died within five years. In Rev. Proc. 2022-32, it replaced an earlier procedure and allowed estates not otherwise required to file an estate tax return to make the election by a simplified method up to the fifth anniversary of the death, without a user fee.
What it teaches
Survivors who skipped the estate return in the first months still have time. A modest filing can preserve millions of dollars of exclusion if the survivor's wealth grows or the law changes.
Source
Internal Revenue Service. Rev. Proc. 2022-32, sections 1–3.
Case study 2The survivor's $500,000 home-sale window
What happened
The tax code lets an unmarried surviving spouse use the married couple's $500,000 exclusion on the sale of the main home, instead of $250,000, if the sale happens no later than two years after the spouse's death and the couple met the requirements before the death.
What it teaches
A widow who already knows she will move gains a real tax benefit by selling within two years. One who is undecided should not sell just for the tax: the step-up on the deceased spouse's share already removes much of the gain.
Source
26 U.S.C. 121(b)(4).

Decisions

Three survivors, three decisions

Scenario 1 · Ruth

Two months after Walter's death, a salesperson tells Ruth she should move $300,000 of the IRA into an annuity "before the market drops." What should she do?

Sign nowPressure plus grief is exactly the combination to avoid. A complex annuity with high charges and surrender penalties may lock up money for years (INV-007, INV-082).
Wait and reviewRight. Nothing about her situation requires action in month two. A simple income annuity may make sense later as part of a plan; she can decide when she is ready and has compared options.
The principle: if a decision cannot wait six months, ask why someone needs it made now.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers

Suppose Tom died at 60, leaving Rachel, 58, as sole beneficiary of his $500,000 401(k). She expects to need about $40,000 a year from it before she turns 59½. What should she do?

Roll to her own IRASimpler, but withdrawals before 59½ would then owe the 10% additional tax: about $4,000 on $40,000 (Figure 98.9).
Inherited IRA firstRight. Distributions after the owner's death are exempt from the 10% tax. At 59½ she can elect to treat the account as her own.
Cash outIt would add $500,000 of ordinary income in one year, much of it taxed at 24% to 35%. There is no reason to accelerate the tax.
The principle: for a younger survivor, the account's label matters as much as its investments.
Scenario 3 · The Riveras

Marcus and Elena ask: if one of us died now, how would Elena, Sofia and Leo get by? What should they check?

Social Security is enoughSurvivor benefits help a lot — children generally receive 75% of the parent's benefit and a parent caring for them may qualify at any age — but the family maximum caps the total, and the benefits end as the children grow up. The gap is usually large.
Estimate and insure the gapRight. SSA can estimate survivor benefits from their earnings records. Term life insurance on both parents fills the gap cheaply while the children are young (INV-023).
The principle: survivor planning for a young family is about income replacement, not estate tax.

What survivor planning means for each household

Name someone. Single with no children, Jordan's 401(k) and any group life insurance should name the person she wants to inherit. If she marries, her spouse becomes the default 401(k) heir (INV-095).
Replace income, not wealth. If either parent died, survivor benefits for Sofia, Leo and the surviving parent would cover part of the gap; term life insurance should cover the rest, plus the $310,000 mortgage.
Plan for Eli, for life. Monica's plan must name a guardian for Eli while he is a minor, nominate a successor if he needs one as an adult, and leave money to a third-party special needs trust, never to Eli directly, funded partly by 20-year term life insurance. If she died, Eli could receive a child's survivor benefit of up to 75% of her basic Social Security benefit, which can continue past 18 because his disability began before 22 (INV-102).

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A widow's late husband received $2,900 a month. If she claims survivor benefits at 60, about how much will she receive each month, in dollars?

SSA: payments start at 71.5% at 60. $2,900 × 0.715 = $2,073.50 a month, versus $2,900 at her full retirement age.
Exercise 2

In Figure 98.4, the couple owed about $1,586 of federal tax and the widow about $4,226. By how much, in dollars, did tax rise even though income fell?

$4,226 − $1,586 = about $2,640, while household income fell from $94,200 to $73,200. The single standard deduction and senior deduction ($24,150 versus $47,500) and the lower Social Security thresholds explain most of it.
Exercise 3

A widow sells the home three years after her husband's death, with a $420,000 gain, and has not remarried. How much of the gain is taxable, in dollars?

After two years she has the single exclusion of $250,000: $420,000 − $250,000 = $170,000. Inside two years the $500,000 exclusion would have covered it all. (The gain already reflects the step-up on her husband's share.)
Exercise 4

Walter died in 2023. Under Rev. Proc. 2022-32's simplified method, by what year must a portability election for his estate be filed?

On or before the fifth anniversary of the date of death: 2028, on the same date as the 2023 death.
