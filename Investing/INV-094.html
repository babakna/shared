Business Succession

For a business owner, the company is usually the largest and least liquid thing they own, and the hardest to pass on. Every owner leaves eventually, by sale, retirement, disability or death. Succession planning decides whether that exit happens on the owner's terms, at a fair price, with the taxes paid over time instead of in a fire sale.

The question this module answersHow do I get fair value out of a business I own, or pass it to family, partners or employees, without the exit breaking the business or the estate?

Start here

Every owner leaves; the question is how

If you own a coffee shop, a dental practice, a plumbing company or a small manufacturer, you already know it is more than an investment: it is your job, your employees' jobs, and often most of your net worth. Unlike a stock portfolio, you cannot sell a slice of it on a Tuesday afternoon.

That illiquidity is what makes succession hard. When an owner dies or becomes disabled without a plan, the family may inherit a business they cannot run, partners may find themselves in business with a spouse they never chose, and an estate may owe tax on a value it cannot turn into cash. The tools in this module — valuations, buy-sell agreements, installment sales, employee ownership, insurance and the estate-tax relief provisions — exist to prevent exactly those outcomes.

None of the five households with stated occupations owns a business today; the Patels' occupations are unspecified. This module therefore uses a clearly fictional example, Lakeside Coffee Co., alongside the real Supreme Court case that reshaped buy-sell planning in 2024, and shows where each household's situation connects.

What is it worth?

Fair market value, and three ways to estimate it

Fair market value26 CFR 20.2031-1(b)
What it is
"The price at which the property would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller, neither being under any compulsion to buy or to sell and both having reasonable knowledge of relevant facts," as the Supreme Court quoted the estate tax regulation in Connelly (2024).
Used for
Estate and gift tax values, the stepped-up basis heirs receive (INV-090), and the benchmark a buy-sell price is measured against.
What it means for you
It is a hypothetical price, not what you hope to get or what a particular buyer might pay. For a private company it must be estimated, usually by a qualified appraiser.
  • Income approach. Value the future cash the business will produce, either by capitalizing a normal year's earnings or by discounting a forecast. Small businesses are often priced as a multiple of "seller's discretionary earnings": profit before the owner's own pay and perks, interest, taxes and depreciation.
  • Market approach. Compare with prices paid for similar businesses, or with the valuations of similar public companies, adjusted for size and risk.
  • Asset approach. Add up what the assets are worth less the debts. It sets a floor, and it is the main method for holding companies and businesses that earn little.
Value ≈ normalized annual earnings × multiple
multiple ≈ 1 ÷ (required return − long-run growth)
Going furtherDiscounts for minority stakes and illiquidity

A 20% stake in a private company is usually worth less than 20% of the whole company, because the holder cannot control decisions (a discount for lack of control) and cannot easily sell (a discount for lack of marketability). Appraisers apply such discounts when valuing gifts of minority shares to family, which lowers gift and estate tax; they need to be supported by a qualified appraisal, because a gift tax return that adequately discloses the valuation is what starts the IRS's clock for challenging it (INV-092). The same logic cuts the other way when a buyer acquires control: a control premium.

Practitioner depthWhen a contract price binds the IRS

Owners often want the price in their buy-sell agreement to fix the estate-tax value. IRC 2703 says an option or agreement to buy at less than fair market value is ignored for estate and gift tax unless it (1) is a bona fide business arrangement, (2) is not a device to pass the property to family for less than full value, and (3) has terms comparable to similar arm's-length arrangements. Family-only agreements with stale fixed prices often fail those tests. In Connelly, the agreement called for an outside appraisal, but instead Michael's son and Thomas agreed between themselves on $3 million, and the courts did not treat that figure as the value.

The partners' contract

Buy-sell agreements and the insurance that funds them

Buy-sell agreement
What it is
A contract among the owners of a business (and sometimes the business itself) that says what happens to an owner's shares on events such as death, disability, retirement or divorce: who must or may buy them, at what price or by what formula, and how it will be paid for.
Used for
Giving a departing owner's family a buyer and a fair price, and giving remaining owners control over who their partners are.
What it means for you
An agreement that is unfunded, never updated, or that no one follows can be worse than none. Review the price and the funding every few years.

Two basic structures. In a cross-purchase agreement, each owner agrees to buy a departing owner's shares and usually owns a life insurance policy on each other owner to fund it. In an entity redemption (stock redemption) agreement, the company buys the shares back and owns the policies. Redemption is simpler with many owners — one policy per owner — but, as the Supreme Court held in 2024, it can raise the value of the deceased owner's shares for estate tax.

With the calculator's defaults, which are the facts of Connelly v. United States, the company is worth $3.86 million without the insurance and $6.86 million with the $3 million of proceeds it received. Michael Connelly's 77.18% stake is therefore about $5.29 million for estate tax, not the roughly $2.98 million the estate reported. A cross-purchase arrangement would have kept the proceeds out of the company.

Going furtherPrice mechanisms

Agreements set the price in one of three ways: a fixed price the owners update periodically (simple, but it goes stale); a formula such as a multiple of earnings or book value (automatic, but formulas drift from reality); or an appraisal at the triggering event (accurate, but slower and costlier). Many agreements combine them: a price updated annually, with an appraisal if the last update is more than a year or two old. Whatever the method, follow it: Connelly shows what happens when the owners do not.

Keeping it in the family

Gifts, sales and installment notes

Passing a business to children is part estate planning and part management training. The tax tools are the ones from INV-092 applied to shares of a company: annual-exclusion gifts of shares (valued by appraisal, often with minority discounts), use of the lifetime exemption, and sales at fair value paid over time.

A sale does something a gift does not: it gives the parents income for retirement. And selling on an installment note spreads the capital gains tax over the years in which the buyer pays.

Installment saleIRC 453
What it is
A sale in which at least one payment is received after the year of sale. Under the installment method, the seller recognizes each year the portion of the payments received that represents gain: principal received × gross profit ratio (gain ÷ contract price).
Used for
Selling a business to family, managers or an outsider who cannot pay all at once, while spreading the tax.
What it means for you
The seller becomes a lender to the buyer. If the business struggles, so do the payments. Interest on the note is taxable as ordinary income, and it should be at least the applicable federal rate.

For Lakeside Coffee at $1.2 million, with a $200,000 basis, the gross profit ratio is $1,000,000 ÷ $1,200,000 = 83.3%. A 10% down payment brings $100,000 of gain in the year of sale; a nine-year note at 4.61% pays about $149,300 a year, and roughly $83,000 to $119,000 of gain is recognized each year. Taxing $1 million of gain in one year at 18.8% would cost $188,000; the installment method starts with $18,800.

Practitioner depthRelated-party resales and what cannot be deferred

If a parent sells to a child on an installment note and the child resells the property within two years, IRC 453(e) generally treats the parent as receiving the child's resale proceeds at once (the two-year limit applies to property other than marketable securities, and it is suspended while the buyer's risk is hedged). Depreciation recapture on business equipment and the value of inventory cannot be deferred under the installment method, so the seller may owe tax in the year of sale even with little cash down. Sales to a trust the parent is treated as owning for income tax (an "intentionally defective grantor trust") are a separate technique used to move future growth out of large estates; they need specialist advice.

Selling to employees

Employee stock ownership plans

Employee stock ownership plan (ESOP)
What it is
A qualified defined contribution retirement plan, a stock bonus plan or combination, designed to invest primarily in the employer's own stock (IRS, Employee stock ownership plans). The IRS and the Department of Labor share oversight.
Used for
Letting an owner sell to the employees as a group, often gradually, with the company funding the purchase through tax-deductible plan contributions over time.
What it means for you
An owner gets a ready buyer that wants the business to continue; employees get retirement savings in company stock. It is complex and costly to set up, and needs an independent trustee and annual valuations.

The section 1042 rollover

IRC 1042 lets an owner who sells stock of a domestic C corporation to an ESOP defer the capital gain, if all of these hold:

  • The corporation has no stock readily tradable on an established market.
  • After the sale, the ESOP owns at least 30% of the company's stock (by each class, or by total value).
  • The seller held the shares at least 3 years and did not receive them from a retirement plan or through stock options.
  • The seller buys "qualified replacement property" — securities of domestic operating corporations — within a window from 3 months before to 12 months after the sale, and files the required election and statement.

The gain is then recognized only to the extent the sale proceeds exceed the cost of the replacement securities. The deferred gain carries into the replacement securities' basis; if the seller holds them until death, they may receive a stepped-up basis (INV-090).

Going furtherS corporations and ESOPs

Many small companies are S corporations, whose income passes through to owners. An S corporation can have an ESOP. But the section 1042 deferral in the statute text reviewed for this module applies to C corporation stock, and the IRS notes an ESOP-owned S corporation cannot deduct plan contributions in the same way. Owners of S corporations should get advice on whether converting, or a different structure, fits.

Liquidity when it's needed

Key-person insurance, and paying estate tax on a business

Key-person insurance
What it is
Life (or disability) insurance a business owns on an owner or employee whose loss would hurt it, with the business as beneficiary.
Used for
Covering lost profits, recruiting a replacement, reassuring lenders, or funding a buyout.
What it means for you
For employer-owned policies issued after the rules took effect, proceeds are excluded from the company's income only if, before the policy is issued, the employee is told in writing and consents in writing (IRC 101(j)); otherwise the exclusion is limited to the premiums paid. Get the consent form signed.

When the estate owes tax but holds a business

Estates above the 2026 federal exemption of $15,000,000 owe 40% estate tax on the excess, generally within nine months of death. If most of the estate is a private business, that deadline can force a sale. Three provisions help.

  • Installment payment of estate tax (IRC 6166). If a closely held business is more than 35% of the adjusted gross estate, the executor may defer the tax attributable to it: interest only for up to about four years, with the first of up to 10 annual installments due no more than five years after the normal due date. Interest on the "2-percent portion" — roughly the tax on the first $1,940,000 of taxable estate above the exemption for 2026 deaths, about $776,000 at 40% — is only 2%; on the rest it is 45% of the IRS underpayment rate, which is 7% for October–December 2026 (Form 706 instructions; Rev. Proc. 2025-32; IRS quarterly rates).
  • Special-use valuation (IRC 2032A). Farmland and real estate used in a family business can be valued at its farm or business use rather than its highest and best use, reducing the estate value by up to $1,460,000 for 2026 deaths. The property must be at least 25% of the adjusted gross estate (and farm or business property at least 50%), must pass to qualified heirs, and must have been used by the decedent or family, with material participation in 5 of the 8 years before death. If heirs sell or stop the qualified use within 10 years, an additional estate tax recaptures the saving.
  • Redemption to pay death taxes (IRC 303). A corporation can buy back shares from the estate up to the amount of death taxes and funeral and administration expenses, and the payment is treated as a sale, not a dividend.

At the defaults, a $20 million estate with a $12 million business owes about $2 million of federal estate tax (simplified). The business is 60% of the estate, so $1.2 million of the tax may be deferred: interest of about $28,900 a year for four years at a blended 2.41%, then ten installments of $120,000 plus interest.

Practitioner depthAcceleration and elections

The 6166 election is made on a timely Form 706. If the estate misses payments or disposes of or withdraws half or more of the business interest, the IRS can accelerate the remaining tax (IRC 6166(g)). The IRS may require a bond or a special lien. The 2032A election requires Schedule T of Form 706, an agreement signed by every person with an interest in the property consenting to the recapture tax, and the executor must list each recipient. Both elections interact with the alternate valuation date (INV-090), which may be elected together with special-use valuation.

Real law, real companies

Three succession stories

Case study 1Connelly v. United States (2024)
What happened
Brothers Michael and Thomas Connelly owned Crown C Supply, a building supply company. Their agreement gave the survivor an option to buy a deceased brother's shares and required the company to redeem them if he declined. The company bought $3.5 million of life insurance on each brother. After Michael died, the company paid his estate $3 million for his 77.18% stake. The IRS valued the company at $6.86 million, including the $3 million of insurance proceeds, and Michael's shares at about $5.3 million, assessing $889,914 more estate tax. On June 6, 2024, a unanimous Supreme Court agreed: the obligation to redeem the shares at fair market value did not offset the insurance proceeds.
What it teaches
Who owns the insurance matters. The Court noted that a cross-purchase agreement would have avoided the problem, at the cost of each owner paying premiums on the other. Existing redemption agreements funded with company-owned insurance should be reviewed.
Source
Connelly v. United States, 602 U.S. 257 (2024).
Case study 2Bob's Red Mill and employee ownership
What happened
Bob and Charlee Moore founded Bob's Red Mill in 1978. According to the company, Bob Moore turned down repeated offers from large corporations, created an ESOP on his 81st birthday in 2010, and the company became 100% employee owned on April 30, 2020. Employees receive company stock each year.
What it teaches
An ESOP can be a succession plan for an owner who values continuity and employees over the highest price. It took about a decade to complete.
Source
Bob's Red Mill Natural Foods, "Employee owned."
Case study 3Lakeside Coffee Co. (fictional illustration)
The setup
The 64-year-old owner of Lakeside Coffee, valued at $1.2 million with a $200,000 basis, wants to retire. Her manager of 12 years wants to buy it but has only $120,000.
The result
An installment sale with 10% down and a nine-year note at the AFR gives her about $149,300 a year, spreads the $1 million gain over ten tax years, and keeps the business running under someone who knows it. Key-person insurance on the manager protects the note.
What it teaches
The right buyer is often already on the payroll. Seller financing makes the deal possible, and the seller must weigh the risk that the buyer cannot pay.

Decisions

Succession choices

Scenario 1 · Marcus buys in (hypothetical)

Suppose Marcus Rivera buys a 30% stake in a friend's HVAC company. The two owners want a buy-sell agreement funded with life insurance. The friend proposes that the company own the policies. What should Marcus ask?

Company-ownedSimpler, but after Connelly the proceeds raise the company's value for the deceased owner's estate. With only two owners, cross-purchase is easy to arrange.
Ask about cross-purchase and priceRight. With two owners, two policies suffice. The Riveras should also make sure Elena would receive fair value quickly if Marcus died, and that his will and beneficiary forms match the agreement.
The principle: decide who buys, at what price and with what money before anyone needs to.
Scenario 2 · Lakeside Coffee's owner (fictional)

Lakeside Coffee's owner has three options: sell to an outside chain for $1.3 million cash, sell to her manager for $1.2 million on a nine-year note, or close and sell the equipment for $250,000. Which fits a retirement plan best?

Outside chainHighest price and no credit risk; the gain is taxed at once. A strong choice if she needs certainty, and she can still diversify the proceeds.
Manager on a noteContinuity and deferral, but her retirement now depends on the café's cash flow for nine years. Security, insurance on the buyer, and default terms are essential.
CloseWalks away from roughly $1 million of going-concern value. This is what happens by default without a plan.
The principle: price, certainty and continuity trade against each other; choose deliberately, years ahead.

Six things owners say about succession

Apply it

What business succession means for each household

An employee's view. If Jordan's employer is private, its succession plan affects her job. If it ever adopts an ESOP, she would receive shares in her retirement account over time.
If they ever buy in. Any stake Marcus or Elena takes in a business needs a buy-sell agreement, decided insurance ownership, and coordination with their wills and the children's trust.
A succession plan for a person, not a business. Monica owns no business, but the same three questions apply to Eli's care: who takes over (a guardian and a successor trustee), with what money (his trust, funded by her beneficiary forms and the 20-year term policy she still needs to buy), and with what instructions (a letter of intent). As in a buy-sell, the insurance must be payable to the right owner; for Monica, that is Eli's trust.
Consulting after retirement? If Tom or Rachel starts a practice at 62, a simple written plan for disability and death, and key-person thinking, belong in it from day one.
If Anna owns a business. Ruth's legacy to Anna could help fund a buy-sell or pay down business debt; how Anna's own business is structured affects what she should inherit and how.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A company is worth $3.86 million plus $3 million of life insurance proceeds it received on an owner's death. The owner held 77.18%. Under Connelly, what is the stake worth for estate tax, in dollars?

($3.86M + $3.0M) × 0.7718 = $6.86M × 0.7718 = $5,294,548.
Exercise 2

A business with a $200,000 basis is sold for $1,200,000 on an installment note. What is the gross profit ratio, in percent?

Gain $1,000,000 ÷ price $1,200,000 = 83.3% of each principal payment is taxable gain.
Exercise 3

An adjusted gross estate is $12 million, including a family business worth $5 million. What percentage of the estate is the business (it must exceed 35% for 6166)?

$5M ÷ $12M = 41.7%, so the estate qualifies to defer the share of estate tax attributable to the business.
Exercise 4

For a 2026 death, special-use valuation reduces a taxable estate by the maximum $1,460,000. At a 40% marginal rate, how much estate tax does that save?

0.40 × $1,460,000 = $584,000, subject to recapture if the heirs sell or stop the qualified use within 10 years.
