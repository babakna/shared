Market Timing and Tactical Allocation

Everyone would like to be out of the market before a crash and back in before the rebound. This module tests the three most popular ways of trying — valuation, trend and momentum rules — on 98 years of data, explains the famous "missing the best days" statistic and its limits, and shows why timing fails far more often than it works.

The question this module answersCan I improve my returns by moving in and out of stocks, and if not, what should I do with my fear of a crash?

Start here

"Buy low, sell high" is easy to say

You already know the rule. Every crash in history looks obvious afterward: the dot-com bubble, the 2008 housing collapse, the 2020 pandemic plunge. It seems that anyone paying attention could have stepped aside, waited, and stepped back in cheaper.

Some investors do get a timing call right. The difficulty is doing it reliably, in advance, twice per cycle — once to get out and once to get back in — and after taxes and trading costs. The best and worst days in the market tend to come close together, so stepping aside to avoid the pain usually means missing part of the recovery too. Figure 52.1 shows what that has cost over the last twenty years, using a statistic you may have seen before; the rest of the module shows both what it proves and what it does not.

What this module covers

  1. What market timing and tactical allocation are, and why you must be right twice.

The two-decision problem

You have to be right twice

Market timing
What it is
Moving money between stocks and safer assets based on a forecast of where the market is heading.
Used for
Trying to avoid declines and capture gains.
What it means for you
Each move requires a correct forecast of both direction and timing, and a second correct move to get back in.
Tactical asset allocation
What it is
A milder form of timing: shifting a portfolio's mix modestly away from its long-term target, for example from 60/40 to 50/50, based on valuations or trends.
Used for
Adding return or trimming risk at the margin, often by professional managers; T. Rowe Price's target-date funds, for example, allow themselves up to 5 points either side of their glide path (INV-050).
What it means for you
Smaller bets mean smaller mistakes, but the same forecasting problem.
Buy and hold
What it is
Choosing a mix and keeping it through market cycles, rebalancing back to targets rather than predicting.
Used for
Capturing the market's long-run return with few decisions, low costs and low taxes.
What it means for you
It is the benchmark every timing strategy must beat after costs, and the one it usually fails to beat.
P(both calls right) = pout × pin    e.g. 0.6 × 0.6 = 0.36

The simple formula understates the difficulty. Markets have risen in most years: in Vanguard's 1976–2022 data US stocks beat cash 76% of the time (INV-051). A rule that is out of the market even occasionally starts at a disadvantage and must make up for it with unusually good timing when it is right. And every switch in a taxable account can trigger capital gains tax (INV-030), a cost that compounds as surely as a fee.

Going furtherWhy the best and worst days cluster

Big up days and big down days are both symptoms of high volatility, and volatility comes in bursts. J.P. Morgan's 2026 analysis found that six of the ten best days from 2006 to 2025 occurred within two weeks of the ten worst days, and that in 2020 the second-worst day of the year, March 12, was immediately followed by the second-best. Someone who sells after a terrible day is very likely to be out for a great one.

The famous statistic

What "missing the best days" proves, and what it does not

The J.P. Morgan figure is real and carefully labeled. Over 20 years, 10 trading days out of roughly 5,000 made the difference between 11.0% and 6.6% a year. The point is that a large share of the market's long-run return arrives in a few short, unpredictable bursts, often in the middle of frightening periods.

⚠️

Read it honestly. The study imagines an investor who is out of the market on exactly the best days and in on every other day, including the worst. No real timer would be that unlucky; J.P. Morgan itself notes that such simulations are "designed with the benefit of hindsight." A symmetric exercise that skips the worst days looks spectacular for the same reason. The lesson is not a precise cost. It is that the best days are clustered next to the worst, so anyone who leaves in a panic is likely to miss some of them.

A fair summary: the statistic does not prove that timing always fails. It proves that timing has a steep hurdle, because the days that matter most are few, unannounced and sit right next to the days that make people want to sell.

Timing with the CAPE

Selling when stocks look expensive

INV-006 introduced the Shiller CAPE ratio: the S&P Composite's price divided by the average of ten years of inflation-adjusted earnings. High CAPE readings have tended to precede lower returns over the following decade. The obvious strategy is to hold stocks when the CAPE is low and step aside when it is high. Does it work?

Only one threshold beat buying and holding: 35, which kept the investor out of stocks in 1999–2001, 2022 and 2025. But look at what an investor in 1929, 1950 or 1990 could have known. The December CAPE never rose above 25.3 in any year before 1995. A rule that sells at 35 is a rule that could only have been written after seeing the late 1990s; choosing it now is look-ahead bias. Every threshold anyone could reasonably have chosen in advance lost money relative to buying and holding.

Going furtherWhy a real relationship still makes a poor timing signal

Across January starts from 1881 to 2016, the correlation between the starting CAPE and the following ten years' real return was about −0.48 in Shiller's data: meaningful, but leaving most of the variation unexplained. The CAPE also drifted upward: it has been above its long-run median for most years since 1990, partly because accounting rules, buybacks and the mix of companies changed. A signal calibrated on 1881–1990 data told investors to stay out for most of the following 35 years.

Practitioner depthHow the test avoids peeking

The decision for each calendar year uses only the CAPE for the previous December, which was known (subject to a short delay for earnings reports) before the year began. Returns are calendar-year S&P 500 total returns. That is generous to the rule in one respect: it ignores taxes on each switch. It is ungenerous in another: real investors could act monthly. Monthly tests reach similar broad conclusions but with many more trades.

Trend and momentum

Moving averages and "sell after a bad year"

A second family of rules ignores valuations and follows prices. The best known was popularized by Mebane Faber's paper "A Quantitative Approach to Tactical Asset Allocation" (2006 working paper; Journal of Wealth Management, 2007): buy when the monthly price is above its 10-month simple moving average, sell and move to cash when it falls below.

Trend following (moving-average rule)
What it is
A rule that holds an asset while its price is above its average over a recent period and exits when it falls below. A moving average is the average of the last N prices, recalculated each period.
Used for
Avoiding the middle of long bear markets, at the cost of whipsaws: selling after a drop, then buying back higher when the drop reverses.
What it means for you
Trend rules have tended to reduce the deepest drawdowns, and have often lagged buy and hold in total return, especially after taxes.

On yearly data, the 3-year trend rule grew 8.8% a year against buy and hold's 9.7% from 1929 to 2025, but its deepest year-end fall was 43% instead of 65%, because it left stocks during the early 1930s. The 2-year version cut the worst fall to 37% but switched 36 times and grew only 7.8%. The momentum rule — sell after a year that lagged T-bills — was the worst of all at 6.8% a year, because the year after a bad year was often a very good one.

💡

What trend rules really offer is insurance, not extra return. If you would otherwise panic and sell at the bottom of a 50% crash, a mechanical rule that cuts exposure earlier might keep you in the game. If you would hold on anyway, buy and hold has usually been the better deal. Either way, a rule only works if you follow it every time, including when it tells you to buy back in at a higher price.

The tester

Try your own timing rule

Choose a rule, a setting and a start year. Shaded bands show the years the rule was out of stocks. Try to find a setting that beats buy and hold from several different start years; then ask whether you would have picked that setting before seeing the data.

Some results worth reproducing: with the CAPE rule at 25 starting in 1990, the rule grew 7.5% a year against 10.7% for buy and hold and spent only 36% of the years in stocks. With the 5-year trend rule from 1950, it grew 10.6% against 11.6%, with the same deepest fall (37%, in 2008) because year-end data could not react fast enough to that crash. Changing the setting by a notch often changes the answer: the sensitivity itself is a warning sign that the rule's historical success, where it appears, is fragile.

Real history

Three episodes that tested the timers

Case study 1"Irrational exuberance," December 5, 1996
What happened
In a speech in Washington, Federal Reserve Chairman Alan Greenspan asked, "how do we know when irrational exuberance has unduly escalated asset values?" The CAPE stood at 27.7 that December, high by historical standards. Anyone who sold stocks for T-bills that month then watched the S&P 500 return 33.1% in 1997, 28.3% in 1998 and 20.9% in 1999 — roughly doubling. The 2000–2002 crash brought stocks back only to about level with T-bills by the end of 2002.
What it teaches
Even a correct warning from the most closely watched economist in the world can be years early. Being early looks the same as being wrong for a long time. By the end of 2025, $1 held in stocks from 1997 had grown to about $15; $1 in T-bills, to about $1.90.
Source
Greenspan, A. (1996), "The challenge of central banking in a democratic society," Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System; CAPE from Shiller; returns computed from Damodaran, NYU Stern.
Case study 2March 2020: the worst days and the best days together
What happened
As the pandemic spread, US stocks suffered some of their worst days on record. J.P. Morgan notes that the second-worst day of 2020, March 12, was immediately followed by the second-best day of the year. The S&P 500 still finished 2020 up 18.0% in the Damodaran data.
What it teaches
An investor who sold after the terrible day would have missed the rebound the next morning. Crashes and recoveries can happen within days, far faster than any person or yearly rule can react.
Source
J.P. Morgan Asset Management, Guide to Retirement 2026; Damodaran, NYU Stern.
Case study 3The expensive decade, 2015–2025
What happened
The December CAPE was above 25 at the end of every year from 2014 through 2025, reaching 39.6 in December 2025. A rule that held T-bills whenever the CAPE exceeded 25 was out of stocks for all eleven years from 2015 to 2025. Over that time stocks multiplied about 4.0 times; T-bills, about 1.25 times.
What it teaches
High valuations can persist for a decade or more. A valuation signal may still be right about the next ten years' average return (INV-006), but that is a planning input, not a trading signal.
Source
Computed from Shiller CAPE and Damodaran, NYU Stern.

Decisions

When the urge to time the market strikes

Scenario 1 · The Harpers

It is two years before the Harpers plan to retire. Tom has read that valuations are near record highs and wants to move their whole $1.1 million to cash "until things settle down." What should they do?

All to cashA timing bet with no exit plan. When would they get back in? The CAPE was high for most of 1996–2025. Selling in the taxable account would also realize large gains on the technology stocks.
Planned de-riskingRight. Near retirement the real risk is sequence risk (INV-078), and the fix is a lower-risk mix they will hold permanently, not a forecast. A reserve of cash or short bonds covers spending if markets fall early in retirement.
Do nothingBetter than timing, but their mix is about 80% stocks with a concentrated technology slice; doing nothing leaves a real risk unaddressed.
The principle: change your mix because your life has changed, not because you have a forecast.
Scenario 2 · Ruth

A newsletter promises that its moving-average signals "avoided every major crash" and costs $299 a year. Ruth's IRA is in a balanced fund. Should she subscribe and follow the signals?

Subscribe"Avoided every crash" is exactly what a backtest fitted after the fact can show. In yearly US data, trend rules reduced the worst fall but lowered long-run returns, and they switch often.
Be skepticalRight. Ask for a real-time record, before fees. In an IRA the trades are not taxed, but the fee is certain and the benefit is not. A balanced fund already caps how much she can lose far more reliably.
The principle: judge a timing service by its live record after costs, not by its backtest.
Scenario 3 · Jordan

Jordan plans to sell their $56,000 of employer stock to diversify, but wants to wait "for the stock to go back up" after a 15% drop. What is the problem with waiting?

Wait for a reboundThe drop says nothing reliable about the next move. Waiting prolongs the very concentration Jordan wants to escape.
Sell on a scheduleRight. Selling in planned steps, with attention to taxes and holding periods (INV-040), removes both the concentration and the temptation to guess.
The principle: anchoring to a past price is not a strategy.

What to do with the fear instead

Time is her edge; timing is not. With 40 years ahead and payroll contributions every two weeks, Maya benefits from falls: she buys more shares cheaply. The only real risk is stopping contributions in a panic.
Stop waiting for the dip. Their $41,000 in checking is a timing decision made by default. Investing it on a schedule (INV-051) ends the guessing.
Diversify on a schedule, not on a hunch. Selling employer stock in planned steps beats waiting for a rebound. Ava's college money, needed in three years, should not be in stocks at all.
De-risk permanently, not tactically. Moving toward their retirement mix over the next two years is a plan; moving to cash on a forecast is a bet.
A cash bucket beats a crystal ball. Two to three years of planned withdrawals in cash and short bonds lets Ruth ride out a crash without selling stocks at the bottom.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A forecaster is right 60% of the time on each call. Assuming the calls are independent, what is the chance of getting both the exit and the re-entry right, in percent?

0.6 × 0.6 = 36%. Nearly two times in three, at least one of the calls is wrong.
Exercise 2

Fully invested, $10,000 grew to $80,619; missing the 10 best days, to $35,866. What percentage of the fully invested ending value did the second investor keep?

35,866 ÷ 80,619 = 0.445, so about 44.5%.
Exercise 3

The CAPE was 40.58 in September 2026. What is the implied cyclically adjusted earnings yield (1 ÷ CAPE), in percent?

1 ÷ 40.58 = 0.0246 = 2.46%. A low yield hints at lower long-run returns, but says nothing about when (INV-006).
Exercise 4

Run the tester with its defaults (CAPE above 25, T-bills, from 1929). What percentage of buy-and-hold's ending wealth did the rule end with? Round to a whole number.

About $31.3 million versus $80.5 million from $10,000 before costs and taxes: 39%. The rule's 8.65% a year trailed 9.71% by about one point, compounded over 97 years.
