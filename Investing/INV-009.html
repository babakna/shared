How Bonds Work

A bond is a loan with a price tag. The borrower's promise never changes, but what that promise is worth changes every day with interest rates. In 2022 that simple fact cost owners of 10-year Treasuries almost 18% in a single year. This module builds bond math from zero — price, yield, duration, the yield curve — so that nothing about a bond's behavior surprises you.

The question this module answersWhat am I actually buying when I buy a bond, and what can make it lose value?

Start here

A bond is an IOU you can sell

If you have ever lent money to a friend and agreed on when you would be paid back, you already understand a bond. A company or government borrows money from investors, promises to pay interest on a schedule, and promises to return the amount borrowed on a set date.

Bonda tradable loan
What it is
A debt security. The issuer promises regular interest payments (the coupon) and repayment of the face value, also called par value or principal, on the maturity date.
Used for
Borrowers fund roads, schools, factories and government deficits. Investors use bonds for predictable income and to steady a portfolio that also holds stocks.
What it means for you
If you hold a high-quality bond to maturity, you know almost exactly what you will receive. If you sell before maturity, you get whatever the market will pay that day, which may be more or less than you paid.

What this module covers

  1. Why a bond's price moves opposite to interest rates, with the formula.
  2. The different "yields" — coupon, current yield, yield to maturity — and which one matters.
  3. Duration: the single number that tells you how much a bond will fall if rates rise.
  4. The yield curve, and how it looked in 2022, 2023 and September 2026.
  5. Credit risk and inflation risk.
  6. 2022, the worst year for 10-year Treasuries in the 98 years of data used in this course.

The seesaw

Why bond prices fall when rates rise

Imagine you own the bond in Figure 9.1, paying $40 a year. Tomorrow, new five-year bonds from the same kind of borrower start paying $60 a year because interest rates have risen. Nobody will pay you $1,000 for your $40 bond when $1,000 buys a $60 bond. To sell, you must cut the price until your bond offers the buyer the same return as a new one. When rates fall, the opposite happens: your $40 coupon is suddenly generous, and buyers pay more than $1,000.

The precise version uses present value from INV-002: a bond's price is the value today of each future payment, discounted at the current market yield.

Price = C₁/(1 + y)¹ + C₂/(1 + y)² + … + (Cₙ + F)/(1 + y)ⁿ

C is each coupon, F the face value, y the market yield per period and n the number of periods. For a bond paying twice a year, use half the annual coupon, half the annual yield, and twice the number of years.

Worked example. A 10-year bond with a 3% coupon, paying $15 every six months on $1,000 of face value. If market yields for 10-year bonds are 3%, it is worth exactly $1,000 — the coupon matches the market, so it trades at par. If yields rise to 5%, discount 20 payments of $15 and the final $1,000 at 2.5% per half-year: the price is $844.11, a 15.6% drop. If yields fall to 1%, the price is $1,189.87.

Premium and discount bonds
What it is
A bond trading above face value is at a premium; below face value, at a discount. Premium bonds have coupons above today's yields; discount bonds have coupons below them.
Used for
Reading a bond quote. Bond prices are quoted per $100 of face value, so "96.24" means $962.40 for a $1,000 bond.
What it means for you
A premium does not mean a bond is expensive, or a discount that it is cheap. At the same yield, the market has already priced them to give the same return. Held to maturity, a premium bond's price drifts down to par and a discount bond's drifts up.
Going furtherClean price, dirty price and accrued interest

Between coupon dates, the seller has earned part of the next coupon. The buyer pays the quoted ("clean") price plus accrued interest, together called the "dirty" or full price, and then receives the whole next coupon. Your trade confirmation shows both. The calculator in the next tab prices bonds on a coupon date, where accrued interest is zero.

Which yield?

Coupon rate, current yield and yield to maturity

"Yield" gets used for several different numbers. They agree only when a bond trades at par.

MeasureFormulaFor a 10-year 3% bond priced at $844.11
Coupon rateAnnual coupon ÷ face value$30 ÷ $1,000 = 3.00%
Current yieldAnnual coupon ÷ price$30 ÷ $844.11 = 3.55%
Yield to maturityThe single rate that makes the present value of all payments equal the price5.00% (by construction)
Yield to maturity (YTM)
What it is
The annual return you earn if you buy at today's price, receive every payment on time, hold to maturity, and reinvest each coupon at the same yield. It counts both the coupons and the gain or loss as the price moves to par.
Used for
Comparing bonds with different coupons, prices and maturities on one scale. Treasury's yield curves are YTM-style yields.
What it means for you
When comparing bonds or bond funds, look at yield to maturity (for funds, often shown as "yield to maturity" or as the "SEC yield," a standardized yield defined by the Securities and Exchange Commission), not the coupon or the current yield.
Quick approximation: YTM ≈ [C + (F − P) ÷ n] ÷ [(F + P) ÷ 2]

For our bond: [30 + (1,000 − 844.11) ÷ 10] ÷ [(1,000 + 844.11) ÷ 2] = 45.59 ÷ 922.06 = 4.94%, close to the exact 5.00%. The calculator solves it exactly.

Going furtherCallable bonds and yield to worst

A callable bond lets the issuer repay early, usually at par or a small premium, after a set date. Issuers call bonds when rates fall, so the investor loses the high coupon just when it is most valuable (call risk, as the SEC's investor site describes for municipal bonds). For a callable bond trading above par, dealers quote the yield to worst: the lowest of the yield to maturity and the yields to each possible call date. Treat yield to worst as the honest number.

The key risk number

Duration: how far the seesaw tips

Figure 9.2 showed that long bonds swing more. Duration turns that into one number. It has two meanings that come out almost the same.

Macaulay durationmeasured in years
What it is
The average time until you receive the bond's cash, with each payment weighted by its share of the bond's present value.
Used for
Seeing when, on average, a bond pays you back. A zero-coupon bond's Macaulay duration equals its maturity; coupons pull it earlier.
What it means for you
If your holding period roughly matches a bond's duration, a rise in rates hurts the price but lets you reinvest coupons at higher rates, and the two effects roughly offset. That is why matching duration to when you need the money matters.
Modified durationa sensitivity, in percent per point
What it is
Macaulay duration divided by (1 + yield per period). It estimates the percentage change in price for a one-percentage-point change in yield.
Used for
Estimating losses from a rate rise. Bond funds report it on their fact sheets, often simply as "duration."
What it means for you
A fund with a duration of 6 would be expected to lose about 6% if yields rose one point, and gain about 6% if they fell one point.
Macaulay D = Σ [t × PV(CFₜ)] ÷ Price     Modified D = Macaulay D ÷ (1 + y)     %ΔP ≈ −Modified D × Δy

Worked example: a five-year, 4% bond at a 5% yield (annual payments)

Macaulay duration is 4.62 years. Modified duration is 4.62 ÷ 1.05 = 4.40. So if yields rise from 5% to 6%, duration predicts a price fall of about 4.40%. Repricing exactly at 6% gives $915.75, a fall of 4.28%. The small gap is convexity.

Going furtherRules of thumb
  • Longer maturity means longer duration.
  • A lower coupon means longer duration (more of the value arrives at the end). Zero-coupon bonds have the longest duration for their maturity.
  • Higher yields mean slightly shorter duration, because distant payments are discounted more heavily.
  • At the par yields of September 25, 2026, modified durations were about 1.9 for a 2-year Treasury, 4.4 for a 5-year, 7.7 for a 10-year and 14.6 for a 30-year (computed).
Practitioner depthConvexity
%ΔP ≈ −Dmod × Δy + ½ × Convexity × (Δy)²

For the five-year bond, convexity is 24.5 (in years²). A one-point rise: −4.40 × 0.01 + ½ × 24.47 × 0.0001 = −4.278%, matching the exact −4.281% almost perfectly. Ordinary bonds have positive convexity: prices rise more when yields fall than they drop when yields rise by the same amount. Callable bonds and mortgage-backed securities can have negative convexity, because borrowers repay early when rates fall (INV-010).

Rates across maturities

The yield curve

Yield curve
What it is
A line connecting the yields of bonds of the same credit quality, usually Treasuries, from the shortest maturity to the longest. The US Treasury publishes it every business day.
Used for
Seeing what the market pays for lending for different lengths of time, and pricing every other kind of bond as a "spread" over Treasuries.
What it means for you
Its shape tells you what you are paid for tying money up longer. When the curve is flat or inverted, you give up little or nothing by staying short.
Going furtherWhat moves the curve

The Federal Reserve sets a target for overnight interest rates, which anchors the shortest end of the curve. Longer yields reflect what investors expect short rates to average over the bond's life, plus a term premium for the risk of locking money up. When investors expect the Fed to cut rates, long yields can sit below short ones and the curve inverts. Economists debate how much an inverted curve predicts; this course treats the curve as today's price list for time, not as a forecast.

Practitioner depthPar, spot and forward curves

Treasury's "par yield curve" gives the coupon at which a new bond of each maturity would trade at par. A spot (zero-coupon) curve gives the yield for a single payment at each date and is what you should use to discount individual cash flows. Forward rates implied by the spot curve are the break-even future short rates: lending for two years at the two-year spot rate earns the same as lending for one year and rolling at the one-year forward rate.

The other two risks

Will they pay, and what will it buy?

Interest-rate risk affects every bond. Two more risks depend on who borrowed and on what happens to prices.

Credit risk
What it is
The chance that the issuer pays late, pays less, or does not pay at all (default). Rating agencies such as Moody's, S&P and Fitch grade issuers; bonds rated Baa (Moody's) or BBB (S&P and Fitch) or above are called investment grade. Lower-rated bonds are high yield.
Used for
Setting the extra yield, the credit spread, that a borrower must pay over a Treasury of the same maturity.
What it means for you
Treasuries have essentially no credit risk; corporate bonds do. The riskier the borrower, the more the bond behaves like a stock in a recession, which is exactly when you want your bonds to hold up.

Over 1928–2025 as a whole, Baa corporate bonds returned 6.63% a year against 4.53% for 10-year Treasuries: the spread paid, on average, for its bad years. The decision is how much of your "safe" allocation you want to depend on companies' health.

Inflation risk

A bond's payments are fixed in dollars. If prices rise faster than expected, those dollars buy less. From 1946 to 1981, a period of rising inflation, 10-year Treasuries lost 1.77% a year after inflation; a dollar invested in 1946 had about 52 cents of buying power by the end of 1981.

Going furtherReinvestment risk

Reinvestment risk is the mirror image of price risk: when rates fall, coupons and maturing principal must be reinvested at lower yields. Short-term savers faced it in 2009–2021, when T-bills returned 0.48% a year. Long bonds lock in a rate (low reinvestment risk, high price risk); bills do the opposite. Duration matching balances the two.

Real history

Three years that explain bonds

Case study 12022: 10-year Treasuries fall 17.8%
What happened
Inflation reached 6.45% for 2022 (December to December), and the Federal Reserve raised short-term rates rapidly. The 10-year Treasury par yield rose from 1.52% on December 31, 2021 to 3.88% on December 30, 2022. The series used in this course, which prices a 10-year bond bought at the start of the year, returned −17.83%, the worst year since its data begin in 1928.
The math
A 10-year bond yielding about 1.5% had a modified duration of about 9. A yield rise of about 2.4 points times a duration of 9 predicts a loss of about 21%; convexity and a year of coupon income cut that to about 18%. Duration did exactly what it says.
What it teaches
High-quality bonds are safe from default, not from price swings. When yields start very low, there is little coupon income to cushion a rise. Investors who held the bonds to maturity will still be repaid in full; investors who needed to sell took the loss.
Source
Damodaran, NYU Stern (2026), historical returns data; U.S. Treasury Daily Par Yield Curve Rates, 2021–2022.
Case study 21946–1981: the long bond bear market
What happened
Over 36 years of generally rising inflation (4.67% a year on average in this data), 10-year Treasuries returned 1.77% a year less than inflation. Nothing defaulted; the losses were entirely in buying power.
What it teaches
A bond's worst enemy is unexpected inflation. This is the reason Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities and I bonds exist (INV-010, INV-008), and why long-term investors do not hold only bonds.
Source
Computed from Damodaran, NYU Stern (2026).
Case study 32008: when Treasuries did their job
What happened
In 2008 the S&P 500 lost 36.55%. Investors fled to safety, pushing Treasury yields down, and 10-year Treasuries returned 20.10%. Baa corporate bonds lost 3.44%.
What it teaches
In a recession-driven crash, high-quality, longer-duration government bonds can offset a large part of stock losses (INV-004 shows the 60/40 arithmetic). The same duration that hurt in 2022 helped in 2008. Knowing which kind of shock you are protecting against is the heart of bond allocation.
Source
Damodaran, NYU Stern (2026).

Decisions

Match the bond to the job

Scenario 1 · Ruth

Ruth's individual retirement account (IRA) holds an intermediate-term Treasury fund that fell in 2022. It has partly recovered. A friend says bonds are "broken" and she should move that money to cash.

Move to cashSelling locks in the loss and gives up today's higher yields for money she does not need for years. Cash also offers no offset in a 2008-style crash.
Keep, if it fitsRight. After the rise in rates, a bond fund's yield is its best rough guide to future returns. On September 25, 2026 the 5-year Treasury yielded 4.98%, against 1.37% at the start of 2022. What matters is that the fund's duration is shorter than the time until she will draw on it.
Go long to catch upLong funds recover faster only if yields fall; if yields rise, they fall further, in proportion to their longer duration (in September 2026, a modified duration of about 14.6 for a 30-year Treasury against 4.4 for a 5-year). That is a bet on rates, not a plan.
The principle: a bond loss caused by higher rates is partly repaid by higher future income. Judge a bond holding by whether its duration fits your timeline.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers

Five years from retiring, the Harpers want money for their first years of retirement spending to be safe. Their advisor suggests a long-term corporate bond fund "for the higher yield."

Long corporate fundIt adds two risks the job does not need: a long duration (a 30-year par Treasury had a modified duration near 14.6 in September 2026) and credit risk that tends to hurt in recessions, when retirement plans are most stressed.
Match the datesRight. A ladder (INV-010) turns price risk into a known schedule of cash. The Harpers' 1.25% advisory fee (INV-007) also takes a large bite out of bond yields; they should ask what they are paying for.
The principle: the bonds that fund spending should be dull. Reach for yield with the money that can wait.
Scenario 3 · Maya

Maya, 24, sees that her 401(k) target-date fund holds about 10% in bonds and wonders whether to switch to a 100% stock fund.

All stocksMathematically defensible for money 40 years away, and many investors do it. But a 40% fall in her first bear market is a real test; a small bond slice makes the ride smoother at a modest cost in expected return.
Choose what she will keepRight. At her age the savings rate and staying invested matter far more than a 10% bond slice (INV-025 on risk tolerance and capacity).
The principle: for long-term money, bonds are a shock absorber, not the engine. Size them to your nerves and your timeline.

Exercise 1

A 10-year bond pays a 3% coupon twice a year on $1,000 of face value. Market yields are 5%. What is its price, in dollars? (Use the calculator if you like.)

Price = 15 × [1 − 1.025−20] ÷ 0.025 + 1,000 ÷ 1.02520 = 233.84 + 610.27 = $844.11.
Exercise 2

A bond fund has a duration of 6.5. Yields rise by 0.75 percentage points. Roughly what is the change in its price, in percent?

%ΔP ≈ −6.5 × 0.75 = −4.9% (−4.875%). On a $50,000 holding, about $2,440.
Exercise 3

What is the current yield of the bond in Exercise 1, in percent?

Annual coupon $30 ÷ price $844.11 = 3.55%. The yield to maturity (5%) is higher, because it also counts the $155.89 gain as the price rises to par.
Exercise 4

Using the table in the Duration tab, what is the Macaulay duration of the five-year 4% bond at a 5% yield, in years?

0.040 + 0.076 + 0.108 + 0.138 + 4.259 = 4.62 years; modified duration 4.62 ÷ 1.05 = 4.40.
