Risk Tolerance, Capacity and Need

"How much risk should I take?" is really three questions. How much can you stomach? How much can you afford? How much do your goals actually require? This module separates them, scores each one with a self-assessment, and translates every mix of stocks and bonds into the dollars it has actually lost.

The question this module answersHow much of my money should be in stocks, given who I am, what I can afford to lose, and what I am trying to reach?

Start here

"I'm a moderate risk investor" — says who?

Most people answer the risk question with a feeling: "I'm fairly conservative" or "I can handle some ups and downs." That feeling is real and it matters. For many decisions, such as a first 401(k) contribution into a target-date fund, it is enough.

But a feeling is only one of three separate things. Your risk tolerance is how much loss you are willing to live through. Your risk capacity is how much loss you are able to absorb without your goals breaking. Your risk need is how much risk your goals require to be reached. The three often disagree, and the most expensive investing mistakes happen when someone acts on one while ignoring the other two.

What this module covers

  1. Willingness: what it is and how it is measured.
  2. Ability: the financial facts that limit risk.
  3. Need: the return your goals require, and the mix that has historically delivered it.
  4. What each mix has lost, in dollars, using 98 years of data.
  5. A scored self-assessment, and why such questionnaires mislead after long bull markets.

Tolerance

Willingness: the loss you can live through

Willingness is psychological. Two people with identical finances can react completely differently to seeing a balance fall by a third: one checks it daily and loses sleep, the other barely notices. Neither is wrong. But a portfolio that you abandon at the bottom is worse than a more cautious one you hold, because selling after a fall locks in the loss and usually misses the recovery.

Risk tolerance (willingness)
What it is
Your emotional willingness to accept investment losses and volatility in exchange for a higher expected return.
Used for
Setting the upper limit on risk from the behavioral side. Brokers and advisors must consider it: FINRA's suitability rule lists risk tolerance among the parts of a customer's "investment profile," along with age, financial situation, time horizon and liquidity needs.
What it means for you
The right question is not "Do I like risk?" but "Would I keep investing, and not sell, if this portfolio lost this many dollars next year?"

A key reason willingness is hard to judge is loss aversion: losses feel much larger than gains of the same size. Percentages also hide the pain. "Down 27%" is abstract; "down $68,000" is not. Figure 25.2 shows the worst calendar year for each mix of stocks and bonds since 1928.

Going furtherHow questionnaires measure willingness

Risk questionnaires mix several kinds of question: hypothetical choices ("Would you take a 50% chance of doubling or a sure gain?"), reactions ("What would you do after a 25% fall?"), experience ("Have you held through a bear market?") and self-description ("When you hear 'risk,' do you think of loss or opportunity?"). Answers are scored and summed. The better questionnaires are tested for consistency, so that someone answering on two different days gets a similar score. Even so, as the Self-assessment tab shows, the score moves with the market.

Practitioner depthTolerance, perception and composure

Researchers distinguish a person's underlying risk tolerance, which is fairly stable, from risk perception (how risky the market seems right now) and composure (whether they actually hold steady under stress). A long rise in prices lowers perceived risk, which raises the answers people give, even if their true tolerance has not changed. That is one reason professional standards ask for willingness to be assessed alongside capacity and objectives rather than on its own. The CFA Institute's guidance for individual investment policy statements notes that assessing risk tolerance "may be difficult and subjective" and suggests anchoring it to known liabilities where possible.

Capacity

Ability: the loss your plan can absorb

Ability is financial, not emotional. It asks what would actually happen to your life if the portfolio fell sharply and stayed down for years. A 25-year-old with a steady job and decades of saving ahead can absorb a crash; a retiree who must sell investments to pay rent next year often cannot, however calm they feel.

Risk capacity (ability)
What it is
Your financial ability to absorb losses without derailing your goals, based on time horizon, income stability, emergency savings, other income and obligations.
Used for
Setting the upper limit on risk from the financial side, independent of how you feel.
What it means for you
You can be willing to take risk but unable to afford it, or able to afford it but unwilling. The lower of the two is your ceiling.

Capacity also changes with age in a way that surprises people. For a young worker, the portfolio is tiny next to future earnings, their human capital. A 30% fall in Jordan's $3,000 costs $900, less than a week of pay. For a retiree, the portfolio is most of the wealth, and the paycheck is gone. The Your household tab applies this reasoning to all six households, including why David and Paula Patel cannot reduce three different horizons to one blended age.

Going furtherGuaranteed income raises capacity

Suppose Ruth's essential spending is about $4,000 a month (an illustration). Her Social Security of $2,900 covers 73% of it for life, with cost-of-living adjustments, whatever the market does. Only $1,100 a month, $13,200 a year, must come from her $780,000 individual retirement account (IRA) for essentials. That is a withdrawal of under 2% a year, so her IRA can carry more market risk than her age alone suggests. A retiree of the same age without Social Security or a pension, drawing 6% a year for essentials, has far less capacity.

Practitioner depthCapacity as a stress test

A practical way to measure capacity is a stress test. Take a severe but historical decline for the proposed mix (for 60/40, the 1929–32 year-end decline of about 40%), apply it immediately, and rerun the plan with the withdrawals and savings you actually expect. If the plan still reaches its essential goals, perhaps with a later date or lower discretionary spending, the mix is within capacity. If the essential goals fail, capacity is exceeded, however strong the investor's stomach. INV-077 extends this with Monte Carlo simulation.

Need

Need: how much return your goals require

Need is the easiest of the three to calculate and the most often skipped. Given what you have, what you will save and when you need the money, what yearly return after inflation reaches the goal? If the answer is low, you do not need much risk. If it is high, extra risk may not be the answer at all.

Find r such that: Savings × (1 + r)n + Yearly saving × [(1 + r)n − 1] ÷ r = Goal (all in today's dollars)

Then compare r with what each mix has earned after inflation. Figure 25.4 shows the real growth rates from 1928 to 2025, with two households' needs marked.

Risk need
What it is
The level of return, and therefore of risk, required to reach your goals with the savings and time you have.
Used for
Avoiding unnecessary risk once goals are within reach, and spotting goals that no reasonable risk level can reach.
What it means for you
If you already have enough, extra risk only buys the chance of having more than you need, at the cost of possibly having less. If you need more than markets have ever delivered, change the plan instead.

Two worked examples. Jordan, saving $9,000 a year (her 6% contribution plus the match, and a little more) toward $1.2 million in today's dollars by age 64, needs 5.32% a year after inflation: roughly what 70% stocks has delivered. Raising her saving to $12,000 lowers the need to 4.16%, about 50% stocks. The Harpers, with $1.1 million, saving $60,000 a year and aiming at $1.7 million in today's dollars in six years, need only 2.98%. Their 25 technology stocks carry far more risk than their goal requires.

Going furtherWhen need exceeds the ceiling

If your need is above what your willingness and ability allow, taking the extra risk anyway is the classic error: in a bad decade it can leave you further behind and more likely to panic. The four real levers are to save more, work or wait longer, lower the goal, or cut costs and taxes (every 1% saved in fees lowers the return you need by roughly the same amount; INV-007). Most plans use a little of each.

Practitioner depthNeed is a distribution, not a point

Matching a required return to a historical average is a first cut. Two portfolios with the same average can have very different chances of success, because volatility lowers the compound return and because the order of returns matters when money is flowing in or out. A better test is the probability of reaching the goal across many possible return paths, and the size of the shortfall when it fails. The course dataset's 5.15% real growth for 60/40 is also a single, fortunate US history; many planners haircut historical returns by one to two points for planning.

⚠️

Year-end figures understate the fall you would have watched. Prices move every day, and a calendar-year return records only where the year ended, not the lowest point along the way. Test yourself against a deeper number than the table shows, not a shallower one.

Going furtherLosses measured in time

Dollars are one measure of pain; time is another. After the 1929–32 decline, a 60/40 portfolio did not regain its end-of-1928 value until the end of 1935, and an all-stock portfolio until the end of 1936, measured at year-ends with dividends reinvested. In INV-003's crash replay you can see how long each mix spent below its prior peak in every major bear market. A mix whose recovery time exceeds your horizon is outside your capacity, even if its dollar loss seems bearable.

Score yourself

A three-part risk self-assessment

This tool scores willingness and ability separately, five questions each, and calculates need from your numbers. Each answer scores 1 to 4; each part's total of 5 to 20 maps to a stock share from 10% to 100%. The suggested starting point is the lower of willingness and ability, trimmed to your need. It is a teaching tool built for this course, not a validated psychometric test.

With the default answers, willingness scores 12 of 20 (50% stocks) and ability 16 of 20 (80%): this person could afford more risk than they are comfortable with. Their need, $200,000 growing to $900,000 in today's dollars in 20 years with $12,000 a year of saving, is 4.80% a year after inflation, which historically took about 60% stocks. The need is above the 50% ceiling, so the tool suggests 50% and flags that saving, time or the goal must close the gap.

Why questionnaires mislead in bull markets

A questionnaire records how you feel on the day you answer it, and that feeling follows the market. Two studies make the point:

  • Scores track last week's prices. John Grable, Ruth Lytton and Barbara O'Neill collected a 13-item risk questionnaire online between September and December 2002. The previous week's closing levels of the Nasdaq, the Dow and the S&P 500 each significantly predicted respondents' risk tolerance scores: when the market rose, scores the following week rose; when it fell, they fell.
  • Risk aversion jumped after 2008. Luigi Guiso, Paola Sapienza and Luigi Zingales surveyed clients of an Italian bank before and after the 2008 crisis. Both qualitative and quantitative measures of risk aversion rose substantially after the crisis, and the authors report that actual trading behavior was consistent with a fear-driven rise in risk aversion.

Put the two together and the danger is plain. After a long rise, such as 2009 to 2021, when US stocks gained in 12 of 13 calendar years, people rate themselves more tolerant than they will be in the next decline. They buy a portfolio sized for the good mood, then discover their real tolerance at the bottom. This is projection bias, a close relative of recency bias: assuming that the way you feel now is the way you will feel later.

Going furtherThree corrections for a bull-market score
  1. Answer in dollars. Replace "a 30% decline" with the dollar figure from the Losses tab for your own balance.
  2. Remember a real decline. If you invested through 2008, 2020 or 2022, what did you actually do? Behavior beats hypotheticals.
  3. Take the lower score. If you answered in a rising market, step down one level (for example 70% to 60% stocks). The cost of being slightly too cautious is small; the cost of selling at the bottom is large.
Practitioner depthWhat the rules require of advisors

FINRA Rule 2111 requires a broker to have a reasonable basis to believe a recommendation is suitable based on the customer's investment profile, which includes risk tolerance, time horizon, liquidity needs and financial situation, among other items. Retail recommendations are also subject to the SEC's Regulation Best Interest. Neither rule requires a particular questionnaire, and neither makes a questionnaire score a sufficient basis on its own. A good advisor treats the score as one input and tests it with dollar scenarios and your history.

Real evidence

Three episodes that tested willingness, ability and need

Case study 1Risk aversion after 2008
What happened
Using repeated surveys of an Italian bank's clients and their portfolio data, Guiso, Sapienza and Zingales found that after the 2008 crisis both qualitative and quantitative measures of risk aversion increased substantially, and that trading behavior was consistent with this. A laboratory experiment suggested fear was a plausible driver.
What it teaches
Willingness is not a fixed trait you can measure once. It falls when prices fall, which is exactly when selling does the most damage. Size your portfolio for the willingness you will have in a crisis.
Source
Guiso, L., Sapienza, P., & Zingales, L. (2018). Time varying risk aversion. Journal of Financial Economics, 128(3), 403–421; NBER Working Paper 19284 (2013).
Case study 2Risk scores that moved with the market, 2002
What happened
In an online survey in late 2002, the previous week's closing prices of the Nasdaq, the Dow and the S&P 500 each significantly predicted people's scores on a 13-item risk tolerance questionnaire, alongside gender and household income. Age, education and marital status were not significant.
What it teaches
The same person can score "moderate" one month and "aggressive" the next. Treat a questionnaire score as a range, and be most skeptical of it after strong markets.
Source
Grable, J., Lytton, R., & O'Neill, B. (2004). Projection bias and financial risk tolerance. The Journal of Behavioral Finance, 5(3), 142–147.
Case study 32022: when "conservative" was not safe
What happened
In 2022 the S&P 500 returned −18.0% and 10-year Treasuries −17.8%. Every mix of the two lost about 18%; a portfolio with 80% in Treasuries lost about as much as one with 80% in stocks.
What it teaches
Low stock exposure does not guarantee low losses. Long-term bonds carry interest-rate risk; for money needed soon, short-term Treasuries and cash are the true low-risk assets. Capacity must be measured against the holdings you actually own.
Source
Damodaran, A. (2026), historical returns data set, NYU Stern.

Decisions

When the three answers disagree

Scenario 1 · The Harpers

Tom Harper scores "aggressive" on his broker's questionnaire and wants to stay heavily in technology stocks until they retire in about six years. Their need, by the calculation in this module, is under 3% a year after inflation. What should they do?

Stay aggressiveWillingness is only one of three answers. Their ability is shrinking as retirement nears, and their need is low. A 2008-style year just before retirement could force a later date for no necessary gain.
Take the risk the goal needsRight. They have largely "won" already; extra risk buys a chance of more than they need. They still need growth for decades of spending, so the answer is a balanced portfolio, reached with attention to taxes on their gains (INV-035, INV-037).
All cashOvercorrects. Cash loses to inflation over a 30-year retirement, and 1.45% a year was the real growth of Treasuries alone. Their need is low, not zero.
The principle: when you have enough, stop taking risk you do not need.
Scenario 2 · Jordan

Jordan scores "conservative." Watching 2022 scared her, and she wants her 401(k) in the stable-value fund. Her ability is high (40-year horizon, steady job) and her need is about 5.3% a year. What fits?

Stable value onlySafe from swings, but with real returns likely near zero her need goes unmet, and the gap would have to be filled by saving much more.
A mix she can hold, plus more savingRight. Willingness is the binding limit, so respect it, but not all the way to zero. Automatic saving increases lower her need, and experience often raises willingness over time.
100% stocksHer ability says yes; her willingness says no. If she sells in the next decline, the high-risk plan produces a low-return result.
The principle: the ceiling is the lower of willingness and ability; closing a need gap starts with saving.
Scenario 3 · Ruth

Ruth's Social Security covers most of her essentials and her need is about zero after inflation. A salesperson says she is "too conservative" and should buy an aggressive growth product. What is the best frame?

Aggressive growthHer plan does not need it. Extra risk mainly serves the heirs or the seller. If she wants to leave more to Anna, that is a goal of its own with a long horizon, and can be invested for it deliberately.
Choose freely, within capacityRight. Guaranteed income gives Ruth capacity; low need gives her freedom. Her withdrawal-year money stays safe, and a long-horizon slice can hold stocks (INV-076).
The principle: low need is permission to take less risk, not an obligation to take more.
