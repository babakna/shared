Gifting and Family Wealth Transfer

Most families never owe a dollar of gift tax, yet the rules still shape how help is given: how much, in what form, to whom, and through which door. This module covers the tax-free routes — the annual exclusion, direct payment of tuition and medical bills, 529 superfunding — plus family loans, gifts of appreciated shares and the trust clause that makes gifts to trusts qualify.

The question this module answersHow can I help my children or grandchildren now, without tax surprises for either of us?

Start here

Birthday checks, tuition bills and the gift tax nobody pays

A grandparent writes a check for a graduation. Parents cover a son's tuition. An aunt helps with a down payment. All of these are gifts in the eyes of the tax law, and almost none of them will ever produce a tax bill.

That is because the federal gift tax has two large buffers. The first is the annual gift exclusion: for 2026, each person may give up to $19,000 to each recipient without filing anything. The second is the lifetime exemption, which for 2026 is $15,000,000 per person and is shared with the estate tax (INV-088). A gift above the annual exclusion is reported on Form 709 and uses up part of that $15 million; tax is owed only after it is gone.

So why does gifting need a module? Because the form of the help matters. Paying a college directly is treated differently from handing a student cash. Giving shares with a large gain passes the tax bill along with them, because gifts keep the giver's cost basis. A loan that charges no interest can be a gift in disguise. And a gift to a trust qualifies for the exclusion only if the trust is written a particular way.

The everyday route

$19,000 per person, per recipient, per year

Giftfor tax purposes
What it is
Any transfer of money or property to someone for less than full value in return. Cash, shares, forgiving a loan, adding a child's name to a deed, or selling to them at a bargain price all count.
Used for
Measuring what must be reported and what reduces the lifetime exemption. The donor (the giver) is responsible for any gift tax; the recipient owes no income tax on a gift.
What it means for you
Receiving a gift is not income. Giving one is a reporting question for the giver only if it exceeds the annual exclusion.
Present interestthe condition for the exclusion
What it is
A gift the recipient can use, possess or enjoy right away. The annual exclusion applies only to gifts of present interests (IRC 2503(b)); a gift that can be enjoyed only later, such as most gifts into a trust, is a future interest.
Used for
Deciding whether a gift is covered by the $19,000 exclusion.
What it means for you
An outright check qualifies. A gift to a trust qualifies only if the beneficiary has a genuine right to take it out (the Crummey tab).
Taxable gift for the year =
total gifts to a person − $19,000 (2026)
Lifetime exemption remaining =
$15,000,000 (2026) − taxable gifts so far

Gift splitting. A married couple can elect to treat every gift either of them makes to a third party as made half by each. That lets one spouse write a $38,000 check to a child with no taxable gift. The election requires a Form 709; the instructions say both spouses generally file, though only the donor spouse files if, for example, only one spouse made gifts, none exceeded $38,000 per recipient, and all were present interests. If spouses elect to split, they must split all gifts to third parties that year. Gifts to a spouse who is a U.S. citizen do not need to be reported at all.

With the defaults — a couple giving to three grandchildren for ten years — $1,140,000 leaves the estate with no exemption used, and at 6% growth it is worth about $1.59 million after ten years. For a family below the $15 million exemption, there is no estate tax to save, and the real reasons to give are the recipient's needs and the giver's pleasure in seeing it used.

Going furtherWhen a return is needed even though no tax is due

Form 709 is due April 15 of the year after the gift (with an income-tax extension extending it too) whenever a donor gives more than the annual exclusion to anyone, gives any future interest, elects gift splitting, or elects to spread a 529 contribution over five years. Filing is not paying: most returns report a taxable gift that is simply subtracted from the lifetime exemption. Filing also starts the IRS's clock for challenging the gift's value, provided the gift is adequately disclosed, which matters most for hard-to-value property such as a family business interest (INV-094).

Practitioner depthGifts, the exemption and death

The gift and estate taxes are unified: taxable gifts during life are added back when the estate tax is computed at death, so gifting above the exclusion does not avoid tax on the amount given. What it does remove from the estate is any growth after the gift date, and any gift tax paid (unless paid within three years of death). The generation-skipping transfer tax, a separate tax on gifts to grandchildren and later generations, has its own $15,000,000 exemption for 2026; annual exclusion gifts outright to a grandchild generally use none of it (INV-088).

Unlimited routes

Pay the school or the hospital directly

IRC 2503(e) says that tuition paid directly to a qualifying school, and payments made directly to a medical provider, are not gifts at all. There is no dollar limit, no return to file, and the $19,000 exclusion to the same person is still available on top.

  • Tuition only. The Form 709 instructions are explicit: no educational exclusion for books, supplies, room and board. Those can still be covered with annual exclusion gifts.
  • Directly to the institution. Reimbursing the student, or sending money to a parent who then pays, does not qualify.
  • Medical includes payments to doctors, hospitals and other providers for care that meets the tax code's definition of medical care, and premiums for medical insurance on someone else's behalf. It does not cover amounts the recipient's insurance later reimburses.

529 plans: a different door

A contribution to a 529 plan is a completed gift of a present interest, but the statute says it is not a qualified tuition transfer under 2503(e). It uses the annual exclusion instead. What makes 529s special is the five-year election: a donor who contributes more than the annual exclusion in one year may treat the contribution as if made evenly over five years (IRC 529(c)(2)(B)). With the 2026 exclusion of $19,000, that allows $95,000 per donor, or $190,000 for a couple, per beneficiary, in a single year.

529 superfundingthe five-year election
What it is
Contributing up to five years of annual exclusions to one beneficiary's 529 plan at once, and electing on Form 709 to spread the gift over five years.
Used for
Getting a large sum invested early so more of the growth is tax-free, and moving it out of a grandparent's estate.
What it means for you
The donor cannot make further exclusion gifts to that beneficiary for the rest of the five years (superfunding has this cost). If the donor dies during the five years, the portion allocated to the years after death is pulled back into the estate (IRC 529(c)(4)(C)).
Going furtherWhich is better for a grandparent: 529 or direct tuition?

Direct tuition payments are unlimited but only help when the bill arrives. A 529 contribution helps earlier, grows without tax if used for qualified expenses, and in many states earns a state income-tax deduction (INV-034). Many grandparents do both: superfund while the grandchildren are young, then pay tuition directly in the college years and let the 529 cover room, board and books. Financial-aid formulas treat grandparent-owned 529s differently from parent-owned ones; check the current rules before choosing whose name goes on the account.

Help that comes back

Lending to family at the applicable federal rate

Sometimes the right help is a loan: a down payment, a business start-up, bridging a gap. A family loan can charge far less than a bank and still be treated as a real loan, provided it charges at least the rate the IRS publishes each month.

Applicable federal rate (AFR)
What it is
Minimum interest rates the IRS publishes every month in three tiers: short-term (loans of 3 years or less), mid-term (over 3 and up to 9 years) and long-term (over 9 years).
Used for
Testing whether a loan is "below market." Interest at or above the AFR avoids gift and imputed-interest consequences under IRC 7872.
What it means for you
For October 2026, the AFRs with annual compounding are 4.25% short-term, 4.61% mid-term and 5.22% long-term (Rev. Rul. 2026-19). Use the rates for the month the loan is made.

Suppose Jordan's parents lend her $60,000 over nine years toward a condo. At the October 2026 mid-term AFR with monthly compounding, 4.52%, her payment is about $677 a month under the calculator's defaults. If a bank would charge 6.5%, the family loan saves her about $6,300 of interest over nine years, and the interest she pays stays with her parents, who report it as income. A mortgage loan from family can even be secured by the home and recorded, which matters if Jordan ever wants to deduct the interest.

When a loan becomes a gift

  • Below-market interest. If the loan charges less than the AFR, IRC 7872 treats the forgone interest as interest income to the lender each year. For gift tax, a loan payable on demand makes the forgone interest a gift each year; a loan with a fixed term is a gift once, when it is made, equal to the amount lent minus the present value of the payments at the AFR (IRC 7872(b) and (d)(2)). Exceptions: total loans between the two of $10,000 or less (unless used to buy income-producing assets), and, for income tax only, loans of $100,000 or less, where the imputed interest is limited to the borrower's net investment income and treated as zero if that is $1,000 or less.
  • No real expectation of repayment. A note that is never enforced, with no schedule and no payments, may be treated as a gift from the start, whatever the paperwork says.
  • Forgiveness. Forgiving a payment is a gift of that amount. Forgiving up to the annual exclusion each year, $19,000 per lender for 2026, is a common and legitimate way to turn a loan into a gift gradually.
Practitioner depthLoans to trusts and "freezes"

Wealthy families use the same rule on a larger scale: lend money at the AFR to a child or a trust, and any investment return above the AFR belongs to the borrower, outside the lender's estate. Selling an appreciating asset to a trust for an AFR note works the same way (INV-094 covers installment sales). These techniques only make sense for estates that could owe estate tax; for everyone else the AFR is simply the floor that keeps a helpful loan from becoming a tax question.

Shares instead of cash

Who pays the tax when you give appreciated stock?

A gift of shares carries the giver's cost basis (carryover basis, INV-090). The built-in gain does not disappear; it moves to the recipient, who pays tax at their rate when they sell. That can help or hurt.

When it helps. A parent in the 15% or 20% capital gains bracket gives shares to an adult child who is not subject to the kiddie tax and whose taxable income, including the gain, stays under the 0% ceiling: $49,450 single or $98,900 married filing jointly for 2026 (Rev. Proc. 2025-32). The same gain is then taxed at 0%. The kiddie tax, covered in INV-090, blocks this for children under 18, for 18-year-olds and full-time students aged 19 to 23 whose earned income does not cover more than half their support.

When it hurts. Appreciated shares the giver would otherwise hold until death would get a stepped-up basis. For an older giver, the lifetime gift may cost the family the step-up. And gifting shares that have lost value wastes the loss: the recipient's loss is limited to the value on the gift date. Sell the loser, keep the deduction, give cash.

Going furtherThe "upstream" gift and why it rarely works

An idea that circulates: give low-basis shares to an elderly parent, who leaves them back to you at death with a stepped-up basis. If the parent dies within one year of the gift and the shares return to you or your spouse, IRC 1014(e) denies the step-up. If the parent lives longer, the shares are the parent's legally — exposed to their creditors, their long-term care costs and their will. It can work in narrow cases; it is not a routine technique.

Gifts that need structure

Gifts to trusts and the Crummey power

Families often want to give to a trust rather than outright: for young children, for a beneficiary who should not control money yet, or to own a life insurance policy outside the estate (INV-087). The problem is that a gift to a trust is usually a gift of a future interest — the beneficiary cannot use it now — so it would not qualify for the annual exclusion.

Crummey powernamed for Crummey v. Commissioner, 1968
What it is
A clause in a trust giving each beneficiary a temporary right to withdraw their share of each new gift, usually up to the annual exclusion amount, for a limited window after being notified.
Used for
Turning a gift into a trust into a present interest, so it qualifies for the annual exclusion. Commonly used in trusts that hold life insurance, where the gifts pay the premiums.
What it means for you
The right must be real: beneficiaries (or a guardian for minors) must be told of each gift and given a genuine chance to withdraw. Most choose not to, and the money stays in trust.
Practitioner depthDetails trustees get wrong

Trustees should send written notice of each contribution and keep copies, allow a reasonable window before using the money, and keep enough liquid assets to honor a withdrawal if one is made. Paying premiums before the window closes, skipping notices, or quietly discouraging withdrawals invites the IRS to argue there was no real present interest. When a beneficiary lets a withdrawal right lapse, the lapse itself can have gift and estate consequences above certain limits; trust drafters handle this, and it is one reason these trusts should be drafted and administered with a lawyer.

Real law, real numbers

Three gifting stories

Case study 1Crummey v. Commissioner (9th Cir. 1968)
What happened
D. Clifford and E. E. Crummey created an irrevocable trust in 1962 for their four children, then aged about 11 to 22. Each child could demand, by December 31 of the year of a gift, up to $4,000 or the amount of the gift, whichever was less. Each parent claimed four $3,000 exclusions a year. The IRS allowed exclusions only for the adult children, arguing the minors' shares were future interests. The Ninth Circuit disagreed and allowed all the exclusions, because a guardian could legally have made the demand for a minor, even though no demand was ever made.
What it teaches
A genuine, legally enforceable right to withdraw turns a gift into a trust into a present interest. The clause now bears the family's name and appears in countless life-insurance and children's trusts.
Source
Crummey v. Commissioner, 397 F.2d 82 (9th Cir. 1968).
Case study 2Superfunding Sofia's and Leo's 529s (illustration)
The setup
Suppose Elena's parents want to help with college. In 2026 they each contribute $95,000 to a 529 for Sofia (7) and another $95,000 each for Leo (4), and elect five-year treatment on their Form 709s.
The result
$380,000 leaves their estate with no exemption used. At 6% a year, Sofia's $190,000 grows to about $360,700 by age 18. They make no further exclusion gifts to the children through 2030; if either grandparent dies before then, part of that grandparent's contribution returns to their estate.
What it teaches
Superfunding is powerful but only fits a donor who can spare the money for good. A smaller front-load plus annual gifts gives the same exclusion over time with more flexibility.
Source
IRC 529(c)(2)(B) and (c)(4)(C); 2026 exclusion from Rev. Proc. 2025-32. Computed: 190,000 × 1.0611 = 360,677.
Case study 3Jordan's family loan (illustration)
The setup
Jordan's parents lend her $60,000 in October 2026 for nine years at the mid-term AFR, with a signed note, a monthly schedule and a recorded mortgage on the condo.
The result
About $677 a month, roughly $13,100 of interest over the loan, all of it paid to her parents instead of a bank. If they later decide to forgive $19,000 a year each, the loan becomes a gift within the annual exclusion.
What it teaches
Paperwork and actual payments make a family loan a loan. Without them it is simply a gift, which is fine if that is the intent, but the family should decide which on purpose.
Source
IRC 7872; Rev. Rul. 2026-19 (October 2026 AFRs). Computed with the INV-092 loan calculator.

Decisions

Three ways to help

Scenario 1 · The Harpers and Ethan's college costs

Ethan's bill is $30,000 of tuition and $15,000 of room and board. The Harpers were going to transfer $45,000 into his checking account. Is there a better way?

No differenceA $45,000 cash gift exceeds their $38,000 of combined exclusions, creating a $7,000 taxable gift and a Form 709. No tax would be due, but it is avoidable paperwork and uses exemption.
Pay tuition directlyRight. Direct tuition is unlimited under IRC 2503(e), leaving $23,000 of exclusions unused for other help this year.
Route through a 529A contribution uses the annual exclusion and is not a 2503(e) transfer, so $45,000 still exceeds $38,000 unless they elect five-year treatment. Some states give a deduction for contributions, which could make it worth it (INV-034).
The principle: use the unlimited doors first, then the annual exclusion, and only then the lifetime exemption.
Scenario 2 · Jordan's down payment

Jordan's parents can spare $60,000 but may need some of it back in retirement. Should they give it or lend it?

Give it nowFine tax-wise: $60,000 exceeds the couple's $38,000 exclusion by $22,000, so they file Form 709 and use a sliver of exemption. But the money is gone if they need it.
AFR loan, forgive over timeRight for their situation. Interest at 4.52% is far below a bank rate, they keep a claim on the money, and forgiving up to $38,000 a year together can turn it into a gift gradually.
Interest-free handshakeAt $60,000, below $100,000, the imputed-interest rules may be minor if Jordan has little investment income, but with no paperwork the IRS or other siblings may see a gift, not a loan.
The principle: decide whether it is a gift or a loan, then document it as what it is.
Scenario 3 · Ruth's gifts to Anna

Ruth has $60,000 in CDs and savings and wants to give Anna $19,000 a year. Is that wise?

Yes, with a cushionRight. $19,000 is within the 2026 exclusion, so no tax or return. The real question is Ruth's own safety: three years of such gifts would use most of her liquid savings (INV-019, INV-083).
Gift taxNo. A $19,000 gift to one person in 2026 is fully excluded.
The principle: the tax rules rarely stop a gift; the giver's own security should.

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A married couple wants to give each of their three grandchildren as much as possible in 2026 using only annual exclusions. What is the total they can give, in dollars?

Two givers × three recipients × $19,000 = $114,000.
Exercise 2

A widow gives her son $100,000 in 2026 and does not split gifts. How large is the taxable gift she reports on Form 709?

$100,000 − $19,000 exclusion = $81,000, subtracted from her $15,000,000 exemption. No tax is due.
Exercise 3

Using the five-year election, what is the most a married couple can contribute to one grandchild's 529 plan in 2026 without using any lifetime exemption?

Each spouse: 5 × $19,000 = $95,000. Together: $190,000, with no further exclusion gifts to that grandchild through 2030.
Exercise 4

A parent lends a child $60,000 interest-free for eight years in October 2026, when the mid-term AFR with annual compounding is 4.61%. Roughly how much interest is forgone in the first year, in dollars?

$60,000 × 0.0461 = $2,766. For income tax, IRC 7872 treats that amount as interest income to the parent, unless the $100,000 exception limits it to the child's net investment income. For gift tax, because this is a term loan, the gift is counted once, when the loan is made: $60,000 minus the present value, at the AFR, of the repayments (IRC 7872(b) and (d)(2)).
