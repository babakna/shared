Credit

A credit score is a three-digit prediction of how likely you are to fall seriously behind on a debt. It affects the interest rate on a mortgage, the price of car insurance in most states, and whether a landlord says yes. This module shows what the scores measure, according to the companies that build them, how to read and protect your credit reports, and how to use credit cards without paying for them.

The question this module answersHow do credit scores work, and what should I do (and not do) to keep mine strong?

Start here

A score is a summary of your report, and your report is a history of your payments

You already know the basic rule: pay your bills on time and do not borrow more than you can handle. For most people that is 90% of good credit, and nothing in this module overturns it.

What the rule leaves out is how lenders see you. They rarely read your history line by line; they look at a credit score, a number computed from your credit report that ranks how likely you are to become seriously delinquent. Knowing what goes into the number explains a few rules that are not obvious: why paying in full can still show a high balance, why closing an old card can lower a score, and why checking your own score is harmless.

Two main models

FICO and VantageScore

Credit scoremost use a 300–850 range
What it is
A number from a statistical model that ranks borrowers by the likelihood of serious late payment, using only the information in one credit report.
Used for
Approving credit and setting its price. FICO says 90% of top lenders use FICO Scores; FICO describes 670–739 as a "good" score.
What it means for you
You do not have one score. You have many: different models and versions, each calculated separately from your Equifax, Experian and TransUnion reports.

VantageScore is a competing model created by the three nationwide credit bureaus. VantageScore 4.0, the version now accepted for mortgages sold to Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, uses the same 300–850 range and publishes its own weights.

Going furtherWhat FICO Scores ignore

According to FICO, its scores do not use your race, color, religion, national origin, sex or marital status; your age; your salary, occupation, employer or employment history; where you live; the interest rate on your cards; or your own checks of your credit. Income matters to a lender's decision, but it is not part of the score.

Practitioner depthInquiries and rate shopping

A hard inquiry occurs when you apply for credit. FICO says one additional inquiry takes fewer than five points off most people's scores; inquiries stay on a report for up to two years but affect FICO Scores for one. When you shop for a mortgage, auto or student loan, FICO counts multiple inquiries within 14 days (older versions) or 45 days (newer versions) as one, and ignores such inquiries made in the 30 days before scoring. Soft inquiries, such as checking your own score, do not count.

The raw material

What is in your credit report, and for how long

Credit report
What it is
A record kept by each nationwide credit bureau (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) of your accounts, balances, payment history, certain public records such as bankruptcies, and inquiries.
Used for
Computing scores, and by lenders, landlords, insurers and some employers (with your permission) to judge risk.
What it means for you
The three reports can differ, because not every lender reports to every bureau. An error on one report affects only scores built from that report.

Getting your reports free

Federal law entitles you to a free report from each nationwide bureau every 12 months, and the three bureaus have permanently extended a program that lets you check each report once a week, free, at AnnualCreditReport.com, the only site authorized to fill the free reports required by law (Federal Trade Commission, FTC). Reports do not include scores; many card issuers and banks show a score for free.

Fixing errors

The Fair Credit Reporting Act requires bureaus to let you dispute mistakes. The FTC's advice is to dispute with both the bureau and the business that supplied the information, with copies of your evidence.

Going furtherMedical debt on credit reports

Since July 1, 2022 the three bureaus no longer include medical collection debt that has been paid, and unpaid medical collections wait a year before appearing; since April 2023 they exclude medical collections with an initial balance under $500. A CFPB rule to remove nearly all medical debt from reports was vacated by a federal court on July 11, 2025, so larger unpaid medical collections can still appear (Case study 2).

Amounts owed

Utilization: the part of your score that can change next month

Credit utilization
What it is
The balances on your revolving accounts (mainly credit cards) divided by their credit limits, measured for each card and in total.
Used for
Part of "amounts owed" (FICO, 30%) and "credit utilization" (VantageScore 4.0, 20%). High utilization suggests reliance on credit.
What it means for you
The balance that counts is usually the one on your statement, which the issuer reports, even if you pay it in full days later. You can pay in full every month and still show high utilization.
Utilization = reported balances ÷ credit limits (per card and overall)

Denise's example. She puts about $1,800 of monthly spending on a $3,000 everyday card and pays it in full, has a $5,000 travel card she rarely uses, and a $2,000 store card with $200 on it. Overall that is $2,000 of balances on $10,000 of limits: 20%. But her everyday card shows 60%.

Going furtherWhy utilization is the fastest lever

Late payments stay for years; utilization is recalculated from each new report. In most scoring models a high balance hurts only while it is reported: pay it down, and the next report and score usually reflect it. That is why people applying for a mortgage are often advised to pay card balances down a month or two before applying. Neither FICO nor VantageScore requires you to carry a balance or pay interest to build a score.

Protect it

Credit freezes, fraud alerts and identity theft

Identity thieves open accounts in your name using your Social Security number. A new lender almost always checks a credit report first, so blocking access to your reports blocks most new-account fraud.

Credit freeze
What it is
A block on access to your credit report for new credit applications, placed separately at each of the three bureaus. Free by federal law since September 21, 2018.
Used for
Stopping new accounts opened in your name. You "thaw" it temporarily when you apply for credit yourself.
What it means for you
A freeze does not affect your score and lasts until you lift it. Requested online or by phone, a bureau must place it within one business day and lift it within one hour.

If your identity is stolen, the FTC's IdentityTheft.gov builds a recovery plan and an identity theft report. Victims can place an extended fraud alert lasting seven years.

Going furtherWho should freeze?

Almost anyone not planning to apply for credit soon. The cost is a few minutes to thaw a report before applying for a loan, a card, or sometimes a new utility or phone account. Freezes are especially useful for older adults, who are frequent targets of fraud, and for children, whose clean files can be misused for years before anyone notices.

The price of credit and more

Mortgages, insurance, renting

Mortgages. A higher score generally means a lower rate. The exact gap changes with market conditions and lender, so the example below uses illustrative rates, but the arithmetic is what matters: a small rate difference on a large, long loan is a large sum.

For loans sold to Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, the Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA) announced on July 8, 2025 that lenders could use either Classic FICO or VantageScore 4.0; since September 9, 2026 all approved lenders may use VantageScore 4.0 without prior approval. FICO 10T is approved but not yet eligible for delivery, and lenders still pull reports from all three bureaus.

Credit-based insurance score
What it is
A score built partly or entirely from your credit history that estimates how likely you are to file an insurance claim, not how likely you are to repay a loan.
Used for
Pricing auto and home insurance. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners cites a FICO estimate that about 95% of auto insurers and 85% of homeowners insurers use them in states where the practice is allowed.
What it means for you
Good credit can lower insurance premiums as well as loan rates. State laws limit how these scores may be used, and some states restrict or prohibit certain uses.

Renting and jobs. Many landlords check credit reports, and some employers may review a report (not a score) with your written permission. A freeze has to be thawed for a landlord's check.

Practitioner depthScores are one input, not the decision

A mortgage decision also weighs income, debt-to-income ratio, down payment, assets and the property. A high score with a high debt-to-income ratio can still be declined. INV-064 covers mortgage underwriting and pricing.

Real events

Three stories that shaped today's credit rules

Case study 1The Equifax breach and free freezes
What happened
In September 2017 Equifax announced a data breach that exposed the personal information of 147 million people. It later agreed to a settlement with the FTC, the CFPB and 50 states and territories including up to $425 million to help affected people. From September 21, 2018, federal law made credit freezes and unfreezes free at every bureau.
What it teaches
You cannot prevent a company from losing your data. You can make stolen data far less useful by freezing your reports.
Source
Federal Trade Commission, "Equifax Data Breach Settlement"; FTC Consumer Alert, "Free credit freezes are here" (September 2018).
Case study 2Medical debt: removed voluntarily, then a rule struck down
What happened
From July 1, 2022 the three bureaus stopped reporting paid medical collections and lengthened the wait before unpaid ones appear from six months to a year; on April 11, 2023 they removed medical collections with initial balances under $500, after which, they said, nearly 70% of medical collection tradelines had been removed from credit files. The CFPB finalized a broader rule on January 7, 2025; a federal court in Texas vacated it on July 11, 2025.
What it teaches
Check your reports after a medical bill dispute. Small and paid medical collections should not appear; if they do, dispute them.
Source
Experian plc (2023), joint press release of Equifax, Experian and TransUnion; CFPB, "Prohibition on Creditors and Consumer Reporting Agencies Concerning Medical Information (Regulation V)."
Case study 3Mortgage scoring opens up
What happened
For decades, loans sold to Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac required Classic FICO. On July 8, 2025 FHFA announced lenders could use VantageScore 4.0 instead; on September 9, 2026 the option was extended to all approved lenders.
What it teaches
Which score a lender uses can change. The habits that raise every model's score (on-time payment, low utilization) do not.
Source
Federal Housing Finance Agency, "Credit Scores" policy page (accessed September 2026).

Decisions

Three credit decisions

Scenario 1 · Denise

Denise wants to "clean up" her credit before she applies for an apartment. She is thinking of closing the $5,000 travel card she barely uses. What should she do?

Close itHer overall utilization would double from 20% to 40%, and she would lose one of her older accounts over time. It tends to lower a score, not raise it.
Keep it, pay earlyRight. Paying the everyday card down before the statement cuts reported utilization to about 5% with no change in spending, and the unused card keeps adding available credit and history.
Open two cardsNew accounts and hard inquiries weigh against her in the short term, just before a landlord checks. Apply for credit when you need it, not to game a ratio.
The principle: lower what is reported, keep what is old, and do not apply right before you need your score.
Scenario 2 · The Riveras

The Riveras may refinance a car loan and want quotes from five lenders, but worry that five inquiries will hurt their scores. What should they do?

One quoteProtects a few points and may cost far more in interest. Comparing offers is what the rate-shopping rules are designed to allow.
Shop within two weeksRight. Older FICO versions group auto, mortgage and student-loan inquiries within 14 days, newer ones within 45. Keeping it within 14 days is safe under both.
The principle: shop for loans, but shop in a short window.
Scenario 3 · Ruth

Ruth receives a call claiming to be her bank, asking her to "verify" her Social Security number. She hung up, but worries. She has no plans to borrow. What should she do?

Do nothingHanging up was right, but a freeze would stop most new-account fraud if her number were ever exposed elsewhere.
Freeze and monitorRight. It costs nothing, does not affect her score, and she can thaw it in an hour if she ever needs credit. Weekly free reports let her check for accounts she did not open.
Close everythingA freeze protects against new accounts; closing her own cards does not, and it can shorten her history and raise utilization if she ever uses credit again.
The principle: freeze first; it is the cheapest strong protection there is.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

Three cards have limits of $4,000, $6,000 and $10,000 and statement balances of $2,400, $600 and $0. What is overall utilization, in percent?

Balances $3,000 ÷ limits $20,000 = 15%. But the first card alone is at 60%.
Exercise 2

Same cards. If the $10,000 card is closed, what does overall utilization become, in percent?

$3,000 ÷ ($4,000 + $6,000) = 30%: double, from closing a card with no balance.
Exercise 3

A $300,000, 30-year mortgage costs $1,847.15 a month at 6.25% and $1,995.91 at 7.00%. What is the monthly difference, in dollars?

$1,995.91 − $1,847.15 = $148.76 a month; × 360 months ≈ $53,550. Payment formula: P × i ÷ (1 − (1 + i)−n), i = rate ÷ 12, n = 360.
Exercise 4

A collection account first appeared as delinquent in 2025. Using the seven-year rule of thumb (counting whole years), in what year should it stop being reported?

2025 + 7 = 2032. The CFPB notes most negative information can be reported for seven years; bankruptcies up to ten. For collections the federal clock starts 180 days after the original delinquency, so the exact date can fall a few months later.
