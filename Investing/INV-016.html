Options and Derivatives Basics

An option is a contract that gives its owner a right, not an obligation, to buy or sell something at a set price by a set date. Used carefully, options can insure a position or generate income. Used as lottery tickets, they transfer money from individual traders to professionals, and the research on that is unusually clear.

The question this module answersHow do options work, when can they help a long-term investor, and why do most individuals who trade short-dated options lose?

Start here

A deposit that holds a price

Suppose you pay a seller $500 today for the right to buy a house for $400,000 any time in the next six months. If prices jump, you buy at $400,000 and profit. If prices fall, you walk away and lose only the $500. You have bought an option.

Stock options work the same way, standardized and traded on exchanges. A call gives the right to buy 100 shares at a set price; a put gives the right to sell. Everything interesting about options follows from two facts: the buyer's loss is limited to what was paid, and the payoff is lopsided, zero on one side and growing on the other. Figure 16.1 draws it.

Options are one kind of derivative, a contract whose value depends on another asset. INV-013 covered futures, which obligate both sides. An option obligates only the seller, called the writer, who collects the price, called the premium, in exchange for taking on that obligation.

The vocabulary

Five words that define every option

Call and put
What it is
Internal Revenue Service (IRS) Publication 550 defines a call as the right to buy from the writer, before a specified future date, a stated number of shares at a specified price, and a put as the right to sell to the writer on the same terms.
Used for
Calls: betting on a rise, or selling upside for income. Puts: betting on a fall, or insuring shares you own.
What it means for you
A buyer can lose at most the premium. A writer's risk depends on what else they hold: small if covered by shares or cash, unlimited for a call written on shares they do not own.
TermMeaningExample
Strike priceThe price at which the holder may buy (call) or sell (put)An XYZ 55 call: buy at $55
PremiumThe price of the option, quoted per share$2 × 100 shares = $200 per contract
ExpirationThe last day the option existsThe third Friday of a month, a weekly date, or today
Contract sizeThe Options Clearing Corporation (OCC) states that most single-stock options cover 100 sharesOne contract = 100 shares
StyleAmerican-style: exercisable any time before expiration. European-style: only during a set period, which OCC says is currently the expiration date itselfCheck each contract's specifications
Intrinsic value and time value
What it is
Intrinsic value is what the option would be worth exercised now: for a call, stock price minus strike, if positive. Time value is the rest of the premium. The Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) defines it as the portion of the premium that exceeds intrinsic value, larger the longer the time to expiration.
Used for
Understanding what you are paying for: part certainty, part possibility.
What it means for you
An option that is out of the money (a call with a strike above the stock price) is all time value, and time value falls to zero at expiration.
Call value at expiration = max(stock price − strike, 0)     Put value at expiration = max(strike − stock price, 0)
Going furtherBefore you can trade options

OCC states that before buying or selling an option, investors must read Characteristics and Risks of Standardized Options, the options disclosure document; broker-dealers must distribute it under Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) Rule 9b-1. Brokers also approve customers for options in levels, from buying calls and puts up to writing uncovered options, under the options rule (Rule 2360) of the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA). The approval questions about experience and finances exist to keep beginners away from unlimited-loss strategies.

Practitioner depthHow options are taxed

IRS Publication 550: a bought option that expires is a capital loss, short- or long-term by holding period; a written option that expires gives the writer a short-term capital gain equal to the premium, whatever the holding period. Exercised calls add their cost to the stock's basis. Options on broad-based stock indexes, such as the S&P 500 index, are "nonequity options" and section 1256 contracts: marked to market at year-end and taxed 60% long-term, 40% short-term. Buying a put on stock held a year or less can reset its holding period.

Breakeven price
What it is
The stock price at expiration at which a position neither makes nor loses money, counting the premium paid or received.
Used for
Judging how far the stock must move before a trade pays off.
What it means for you
For a bought call it is the strike plus the premium: the stock has to rise past that, before expiration, just to get your money back.

Two patterns stand out. Every bought option has a capped loss and a skewed chance of a large gain; every written option has a capped gain and a larger possible loss. And a covered call behaves much like a cash-secured put with the same strike: both collect a premium in exchange for giving up upside above the strike while keeping most of the downside.

Going furtherWhy a covered call and a sold put look alike

Owning 100 shares and selling a call has the same payoff shape as holding cash and selling a put with the same strike and expiration: a capped gain and most of the downside. That equivalence follows from put–call parity. It means the "income" from either strategy is compensation for accepting the same risk, not two different sources of free money.

Practitioner depthPut–call parity
C − P = S − K e−rT

For European-style options on a stock paying no dividends, a call minus a put with the same strike K and expiration equals the stock price minus the present value of the strike. If the relationship breaks, traders can profit risklessly, so market prices keep it close. Dividends and early exercise add terms for American-style stock options.

Two strategies for long-term investors

Selling upside, buying insurance

Covered call
What it is
Owning 100 shares and selling one call on them. You keep the premium; if the stock ends above the strike, your shares are sold at the strike.
Used for
Earning income from shares, or selling a position at a target price while being paid to wait.
What it means for you
OCC's disclosure document puts it plainly: the covered call writer gives up the opportunity to benefit from a rise above the strike but continues to bear the risk of a decline. The premium is not free income; it is the price of your upside.

Worked example. An employee owns 100 shares of their employer's stock at $48 and sells a three-month call with a $52 strike for $1.50 ($150). If the stock ends at $50, the call expires worthless: the employee keeps the shares and the $150. If it ends at $60, the shares are called away at $52: the gain is ($52 − $48 + $1.50) × 100 = $550, instead of $1,200 from simply holding. If it ends at $35, the shares have lost $1,300 and the premium offsets only $150.

Protective put
What it is
Owning shares and buying a put on them, so your loss below the strike is capped until expiration.
Used for
Insuring a concentrated position through a risky period, for example between a vesting date and a planned sale.
What it means for you
Insurance you must rebuy every few months. Protecting a whole portfolio this way year after year can cost more than simply holding less stock.
Going furtherThe collar

Combining the two, selling a call and using the premium to buy a put, creates a collar: the stock can move only within a band until expiration. Collars are sometimes used by executives and early employees with large single-stock positions who cannot sell immediately. They reduce risk for a period but do not diversify; the tax consequences can be complex and deserve professional advice (INV-040).

Practitioner depthDividends and early assignment

Stock options are not adjusted for ordinary cash dividends. OCC notes that a call holder who exercises before the ex-dividend date receives the dividend, so a covered call writer's chance of being assigned may increase as the ex-date approaches. Writers of in-the-money calls on dividend-paying stocks should be prepared for their shares to be called away just before the ex-dividend date.

Why timing is everything

A melting ice cube with a lever attached

An option buyer must be right about direction and timing. OCC's disclosure document describes an option as a "wasting asset which becomes worthless when it expires": if the price does not move far enough before expiration, the holder may lose all or much of the investment, whereas a stock buyer can wait.

Time decay
What it is
The day-by-day loss of an option's time value as expiration approaches, all else unchanged. Traders call the daily amount theta.
Used for
Estimating what it costs to hold a bought option, and what a writer collects each day.
What it means for you
Decay accelerates near expiration. An option bought "cheap" a few days out is cheap because the clock is almost out.

At the defaults (stock $100, strike $100, 30% implied volatility, 60 days), the model values the call at about $5.17 a share, $517 per contract. If the stock price does not move, it loses about $158 per contract over the first 30 days and $359 over the last 30. The Black–Scholes model used here is the standard way to price such options; the market's implied volatility is whatever volatility makes the model match the traded price.

Leverage, in OCC's own example

The disclosure document compares three investors with $5,000 each, when XYZ stock is $50. A buys 100 shares. B buys one six-month 50 call for $500 and puts $4,500 in Treasury bills (earning about $73). C spends all $5,000 on ten calls.

Going furtherWhy buyers overpay around events

Implied volatility rises before scheduled events such as earnings announcements, because a big move is more likely. Options then cost more. Tim de Silva, Eric So and Kevin Smith find that retail investors buy options disproportionately before earnings announcements with high expected volatility, and overpay relative to the volatility that actually follows (next tab).

Practitioner depthThe Black–Scholes formula
C = S N(d1) − K e−rT N(d2),   d1 = [ln(S/K) + (r + σ²/2)T] ÷ (σ√T),   d2 = d1 − σ√T

S is the stock price, K the strike, r the risk-free rate, T the time to expiration in years, σ volatility and N the standard normal distribution. A put is P = K e−rT N(−d2) − S N(−d1). The model assumes constant volatility and no jumps, which real markets violate; practitioners adjust by quoting a different implied volatility for each strike and expiration.

The evidence

Short-dated options and individual traders

Options trading by individuals has grown rapidly. Svetlana Bryzgalova, Anna Pavlova and Taisiya Sikorskaya (Journal of Finance, 2023) show that retail trading, facilitated by payment for order flow, recently reached over 60% of total options market volume, with nearly 90% of that payment for order flow coming from three wholesalers. They find retail investors prefer cheaper, weekly options with an average bid-ask spread of 12.6%, and lose money on average.

Why the consistent result? Three costs stack up. Spreads: cheap options have wide bid-ask spreads relative to their price, so a round trip can cost a large share of the premium. Overpaying for volatility: buyers pay most when excitement is highest. Time decay: the clock runs against the buyer. On the other side are market makers, professional firms that price these costs in. The 0DTE market in particular has exploded: Cboe reports that same-day (0DTE) options on the S&P 500 index, ticker SPX, averaged 2.3 million contracts a day in 2025, 59% of all SPX option volume.

Assignment risk for option writers

Assignment
What it is
The notice that a holder has exercised an option you wrote, obligating you to deliver shares (call) or buy them (put). OCC's disclosure document says the writer of an American-style option can be assigned at any time until expiration, and may not receive notice until one or more days after the assignment.
Used for
Settling exercised options. Brokers must have fixed procedures for allocating assignments among customers, such as random selection or first-in, first-out.
What it means for you
Once assigned, you can no longer close the position. A cash-secured put writer can suddenly own 100 shares per contract; an uncovered call writer can be forced to buy shares at any price.
Going furtherWho does well with options?

Beckmeyer, Branger and Gayda find that retail losses in 0DTE options come mainly from single-leg trades, trades that require paying up front, and high-implied-volatility options; multi-leg trades, and trades that capture the compensation for volatility and jump risks, were significantly more profitable. That is consistent with the broader pattern: the reliable profits go to whoever sells overpriced insurance and lottery tickets at scale, with risk controls, not to whoever buys them.

Practitioner depthPayment for order flow

Many retail option orders are routed to wholesalers, firms that pay brokers for the order flow and trade against it. Bryzgalova, Pavlova and Sikorskaya document that this payment for order flow facilitated the boom in retail options trading and that nearly 90% of it came from three wholesalers. It is how "commission-free" options trading is funded; the cost to the trader shows up in the bid-ask spread.

Real history

Approval, disclosure and leverage

Case study 1FINRA and Robinhood's options approvals, 2021
What happened
In a June 30, 2021 settlement, FINRA found that since Robinhood began offering options in December 2017, it had relied on computer algorithms, "option account approval bots," with limited oversight. The bots approved customers based on inconsistent information, including customers younger than 21 who claimed more than three years of options experience, and approved customers the firm had rejected minutes earlier. FINRA also found Robinhood falsely told customers they would "never lose more than the premium paid" on a debit spread when many lost far more. Robinhood consented, without admitting or denying the findings, to a censure, a $57 million fine and about $12.6 million in restitution plus interest.
What it teaches
Approval for options trading is not a sign that options suit you, and app screens can mislead. Read the disclosure document and understand the worst case before the first trade.
Source
FINRA, Letter of Acceptance, Waiver and Consent, Robinhood Financial LLC (June 30, 2021).
Case study 2Archegos Capital Management, 2021
What happened
Archegos, Bill Hwang's family investment firm, used security-based swaps, derivatives that the SEC notes let investors take huge positions in stocks by posting limited funds up front. The SEC alleged that from March 2020 to March 2021 Archegos grew from about $1.5 billion of capital and $10 billion of exposure to more than $36 billion and $160 billion of exposure at its peak, concentrated in a handful of stocks. In late March 2021 prices fell, Archegos could not meet its margin calls, and its counterparties lost billions of dollars. Hwang was convicted in July 2024 of racketeering conspiracy, securities fraud, market manipulation and wire fraud, and sentenced to 18 years in prison.
What it teaches
Derivatives can multiply exposure far beyond capital. When the underlying positions are concentrated, a modest price move becomes a total loss, and the losses spread to whoever lent the leverage.
Source
SEC press release 2022-70 (April 27, 2022); U.S. Attorney's Office, Southern District of New York, press release on Hwang's sentencing (2024).

Decisions

Three households, three option ideas

Scenario 1 · Monica

A free retirement-income seminar shows Monica how to sell a covered call every month on the index fund shares in her $40,000 taxable account, "collecting rent on stocks you already own." She is 58 and would like more income. What should she weigh?

Covered calls every monthThe premium caps her upside while leaving nearly all of the downside. Written options that expire are short-term gains in a taxable account, and shares called away can realize capital gains she did not plan for.
Keep holding; plan income laterReasonable. She does not need income from this account for about nine years. Trading the index fund's strongest months for small premiums works against savings that must last through her retirement and help support Eli (INV-076).
Buy callsAdds leverage and time decay to money she cannot afford to lose. The opposite of what the situation needs.
The principle: a covered call is a sale of your upside; write one only at a price where you would be glad to sell the shares.
Scenario 2 · Jordan

Jordan sees videos of people turning $500 into $5,000 in a day with same-day S&P 500 options. Her broker's app offers to approve her for options in two minutes. What should she do?

Try itThe loss on one bought option is capped, but people rarely stop at one. Beckmeyer and co-authors estimate retail traders lost about $241,000 on an average day in these options; the videos show the survivors.
Skip itRight. A 50% employer match is a certain gain; a 0DTE option is a bet with a negative average.
The principle: a capped loss per trade is not a capped loss per habit.
Scenario 3 · The Harpers

Nervous about their $220,000 in 25 technology stocks before retirement, the Harpers ask their advisor about buying protective puts on each stock every quarter. What is a better first step?

Puts foreverQuarterly insurance on 25 stocks is expensive, and the premiums repeat. It treats the symptom and keeps the concentration.
Diversify, hedge selectivelyRight. Lower concentration is permanent risk reduction; options can bridge a short gap (INV-035, INV-037, INV-039).
The principle: insurance is for risks you cannot remove; concentration is a risk you can.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

You buy one call with a $50 strike for $3.00 a share. At expiration the stock is $58. What is your profit, in dollars?

Value at expiration: $58 − $50 = $8 a share. Profit: ($8 − $3) × 100 = $500 on a $300 outlay.
Exercise 2

A put has a $40 strike and costs $2.50. Below what stock price at expiration does the buyer profit, in dollars?

Breakeven = strike − premium = $37.50. At $35 the put is worth $5, a profit of $2.50 a share.
Exercise 3

You own 100 shares bought at $48 and sell one call with a $50 strike for $1.50. At expiration the stock is $56 and the shares are called away. What is your total profit, in dollars?

Share gain to the strike: $50 − $48 = $2; plus premium $1.50; times 100 = $350. Holding the shares without the call would have made $800.
Exercise 4

In OCC's example, Investor C spends $5,000 on ten XYZ 50 calls at $5 a share. At expiration XYZ is $54. What is C's percentage return?

Each call is worth $54 − $50 = $4 a share, $400 a contract; ten are worth $4,000. Return: $4,000 ÷ $5,000 − 1 = −20%, although the stock rose 8%.
