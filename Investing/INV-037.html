Tax-Loss and Tax-Gain Harvesting

In a taxable account, you choose when to realize gains and losses. Selling an investment that has fallen can create a tax deduction while you stay invested; selling one that has risen in a low-income year can reset its cost basis at a 0% tax rate. Both are legal, both have rules, and both are worth less than their marketing suggests unless you understand them.

The question this module answersWhen should I sell on purpose to lower my taxes, and what rules must I follow?

Start here

The tax code shares your losses — if you let it

When an investment in a taxable account rises above its cost basis, you owe no tax until you sell. When it falls, you get no deduction until you sell. You control the timing. Harvesting means using that control on purpose.

Most investors, most of the time, need do nothing. Inside a 401(k) or IRA there are no gains or losses to harvest: nothing inside is taxed until withdrawal. And in a taxable account, simply holding broad index funds for decades is already tax-efficient. Harvesting adds value in two narrower situations: after a market fall, when some holdings sit below what you paid, and in years when your income is low enough that gains are taxed at 0%.

Falls are not rare. Figure 37.1 shows the calendar-year return of the S&P 500 since 2000. In 26 of the 98 years from 1928 to 2025 the index lost money, and even in good years some funds and many individual stocks fall.

The netting rules

Gains first, then $3,000 of income, then the future

Realized capital loss
What it is
The amount by which the sale price of an investment falls short of your cost basis (INV-035). A loss exists on paper while you hold; it is "realized" only when you sell.
Used for
Offsetting capital gains, then a limited amount of ordinary income such as wages.
What it means for you
A fallen investment you still believe in can be sold, replaced with a similar one, and turned into a tax deduction.

The IRS sorts gains and losses by how long you held the asset: more than one year is long-term, one year or less is short-term. On Schedule D, losses first offset gains of the same kind, and any net loss of one kind then offsets net gains of the other kind. If you still have a net loss, the rule in Internal Revenue Code section 1211(b) applies:

Deduction against ordinary income = lesser of $3,000 ($1,500 married filing separately) or net capital loss

This $3,000 limit is written into the statute as a flat dollar amount and is not adjusted for inflation. Anything beyond it becomes a capital loss carryover: it carries forward to the next year, keeps its short- or long-term character, and repeats the same process, with no time limit (IRS Topic 409).

Going furtherA worked netting example

The Harpers realize $12,000 of long-term gains this year and harvest $30,000 of long-term losses on three technology stocks. Net: −$18,000. They deduct $3,000 against their wages and carry $15,000 forward. At a 15% long-term rate and a 24% ordinary rate, this year's federal saving is $12,000 × 15% + $3,000 × 24% = $2,520. The $15,000 carryover can offset future gains or $3,000 a year of ordinary income for five more years.

Practitioner depthCarryover character and joint returns

The Capital Loss Carryover Worksheet in Publication 550 uses short-term losses first when applying the $3,000 deduction, preserving long-term losses. Married couples filing jointly combine their gains and losses; the $3,000 limit is per return, not per person. If a couple who filed jointly later file separately, a carryover from the joint return can be deducted only on the return of the spouse who actually had the loss. Brokers report sales on Form 1099-B; you report them on Form 8949 and Schedule D.

Measure it

What a harvested loss is really worth

A harvested loss is partly a permanent saving and partly a loan from the government. When you sell at a loss and buy a similar investment, the new investment's cost basis is lower. If you later sell it, the gain is larger by the same amount, so part of today's saving is paid back. The calculator shows both sides.

Harvesting creates lasting value in four ways: when the loss offsets income taxed at a higher rate than the gain you will later pay back (a rate difference); when the payback is years away and the saving is invested meanwhile (time value); when the replacement is never sold, because you donate it (INV-039) or hold it until death and heirs receive a step-up in basis (INV-090); and when the payback happens in a year with a 0% gains rate.

Going furtherThe research on "tax alpha"

Chaudhuri, Burnham and Lo (2020, Financial Analysts Journal) simulated harvesting across the 500 largest US stocks in the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP) database from 1926 to 2018, assuming long-term and short-term gains rates of 15% and 35%. They found a before-transaction-cost "tax alpha" of 1.08% a year, falling to 0.82% a year when the wash-sale rule was respected. Two cautions: the strategy harvested individual stocks, which produce far more losses than a single index fund, and the benefit depends on high short-term rates and on how gains are eventually taxed. Studies like this measure the value of deferral and rate differences, not free money.

Practitioner depthDirect indexing

Direct indexing means owning the individual stocks of an index in a separately managed account rather than a fund, so that losing stocks can be harvested even in a year when the index rises. It creates more harvestable losses, which is why the research above found large figures. Over time the portfolio's basis falls and harvesting opportunities shrink, a pattern practitioners call "lock-in." Fees, tracking differences from the index, and the eventual gain must be weighed against the tax saved.

The rule that catches most people

Sell, but do not buy it back too soon

You cannot sell an investment at a loss, claim the deduction, and immediately buy the same thing back. This is the wash-sale rule. Internal Revenue Code section 1091 disallows a loss if, within 30 days before or after the sale, you acquire substantially identical stock or securities. IRS Publication 550 lists four ways to trigger it: you buy substantially identical stock or securities; acquire them in a fully taxable trade; acquire a contract or option to buy them; or acquire substantially identical stock for your IRA or Roth IRA. A purchase by your spouse, or by a corporation you control, also counts.

What happens to a disallowed loss

Usually the loss is postponed, not lost. Publication 550 says to add the disallowed loss to the cost of the new shares, and the new shares' holding period includes the old ones'. Its own example: buy 100 shares for $1,000, sell them for $750, and within 30 days buy 100 again for $800. The $250 loss is disallowed, and the new basis is $800 + $250 = $1,050. The exception is a purchase inside an IRA or Roth IRA: there is no basis to adjust, so that loss is gone for good.

If you buy back fewer shares than you sold, only that number of shares is caught. Publication 550's example: sell 100 shares at a $1,000 loss after buying 75 substantially identical shares in the 30 days before; the loss on 75 shares ($750) is disallowed and the loss on the other 25 ($250) is allowed.

Going furtherWhat counts as "substantially identical"?

Publication 550 says to "consider all the facts and circumstances" and that stocks or securities of one corporation are ordinarily not substantially identical to those of another. Selling one company's stock and buying a competitor's is therefore not a wash sale. For funds, the IRS has not published a test. Selling one S&P 500 fund and buying another S&P 500 fund from a different sponsor is a gray area; selling an S&P 500 fund and buying a total-market or large-cap fund that tracks a different index is the conservative choice most investors make.

Practitioner depthWhere wash sales hide

Watch for dividend reinvestment in the same fund in any account; 401(k) contributions buying the same fund you sold in taxable (Publication 550 names IRAs and Roth IRAs; many practitioners treat other retirement accounts the same way to be safe); employee stock purchase plan purchases of an employer stock you sold at a loss; and a spouse's accounts. A broker cannot see your other accounts or your spouse's, so a wash sale created across accounts may not appear on your Form 1099-B. You are responsible for reporting it.

The other direction

Sell winners on purpose in low-income years

Tax-gain harvesting means selling an investment that has risen, in a year when the gain is taxed at 0%, and immediately buying it back. Because the wash-sale rule applies only to losses, you can rebuy the same fund the same day. You pay no tax, stay fully invested, and your cost basis rises to today's price, so a future sale will have a smaller taxable gain.

The 0% capital-gains rate2026 thresholds
What it is
Long-term capital gains and qualified dividends are taxed at 0% to the extent they fall under a taxable-income threshold. For 2026 the maximum 0% amounts are $49,450 (single), $98,900 (married filing jointly) and $66,200 (head of household); the 15% rate then applies up to $545,500, $613,700 and $579,600 (Rev. Proc. 2025-32).
Used for
Taxing gains lightly for households with modest taxable income, including many early retirees.
What it means for you
Gains sit on top of your other taxable income. Only the space between your ordinary taxable income and the threshold is taxed at 0%.
0% room = 0% threshold − taxable income before the gain (never less than zero)

The defaults show a married couple with $60,000 of ordinary income: after the $32,200 standard deduction their taxable income is $29,800, leaving room for the whole $40,000 gain at 0%. The Harpers' first retirement year shows how large the opportunity can be: with $20,000 of interest and $4,000 of qualified dividends and no wages, they could realize about $107,100 of long-term gains at 0% in 2026. But every dollar of Roth conversion they do in the same year (INV-038) uses a dollar of that room: with a $50,000 conversion, the tax-free gain falls to about $57,100.

⚠️

"0%" is not always zero. Realizing gains raises your adjusted gross income. That can make more Social Security taxable, reduce the $6,000 senior deduction (phased out above $75,000 of modified AGI, $150,000 joint, for 2025–2028), raise Medicare premiums two years later (INV-038), reduce health-insurance subsidies, and add state tax. For Ruth, each $1,000 of "0%" gain would cost about $102 of federal tax because it pulls $850 of Social Security into taxable income at 12%.

Before you click "sell"

Carryforwards, deaths, states and fees

  1. Carryovers die with you. Publication 550: a decedent's capital loss "can be deducted only on the final income tax return," and the estate "cannot deduct any of the loss or carry it over." Late in life, a large carryover may never be used, while unrealized gains on held assets are erased by the step-up in basis.
  2. States differ. Most states start from federal income, but not all follow the federal loss rules. Pennsylvania, where Ruth lives, "has no provisions for the carryover of losses from one tax year to another" and "does not allow an offset of loss against gain from one class of income to another or between two taxpayers (i.e., spouses)" (PA Department of Revenue, Personal Income Tax Guide).
  3. Harvesting does nothing inside retirement accounts. Only taxable accounts have realized gains and losses.
  4. Short-term gains on the replacement. If the replacement rises and you sell it within a year, the gain is short-term and taxed at ordinary rates.
  5. Costs and drift. Trading costs, fund minimums and a replacement that differs from the original can eat the benefit. Keep your target allocation.
  6. Record keeping. Use specific lot identification (INV-035) so you sell the lots with the biggest losses, and keep records of wash-sale basis adjustments across accounts.
Going furtherHarvesting gains before a move or a higher bracket

The same timing logic runs in reverse. If you expect a higher tax rate later — a move to a state with a higher income tax, a promotion, or the end of a low-income year — realizing gains now at a lower rate can make sense. If you expect a lower rate later, such as moving from a high-tax state to one without an income tax, waiting may be better. INV-041 covers state residency rules.

Real numbers and real rules

Three harvesting stories

Case study 12008: a $36,550 loss with the recovery intact (illustration on real returns)
What happened
The S&P 500 returned −36.55% in 2008. $100,000 invested at the start of the year was worth about $63,450 at the end. An investor who sold in December and immediately bought a fund tracking a different index realized a $36,550 loss while staying invested for the 25.94% rebound of 2009.
What it teaches
The loss could offset gains for years — for example on selling a concentrated stock or a rental property — or $3,000 a year of ordinary income. The replacement fund's lower basis means part of the benefit is deferral, but the timing and rate advantages were real.
Source
Returns: Damodaran, A. (2026), Historical returns on stocks, bonds and bills, NYU Stern. Rules: IRC §§1091, 1211, 1212.
Case study 2Measuring harvesting over 93 years of stock data
What happened
Chaudhuri, Burnham and Lo tested a harvesting strategy on the 500 largest US stocks from 1926 to 2018. With 15% long-term and 35% short-term rates, it produced a tax alpha of 1.08% a year before trading costs, or 0.82% when constrained by the wash-sale rule.
What it teaches
The wash-sale rule removes about a quarter of the benefit in their test. Most of the value comes from harvesting individual stocks and from the gap between short- and long-term rates; a buy-and-hold index-fund investor will see much less.
Source
Chaudhuri, S. E., Burnham, T. C., & Lo, A. W. (2020). An empirical evaluation of tax-loss-harvesting alpha. Financial Analysts Journal, 76(3), 99–108.
Case study 3The law on buying back inside an IRA
What happened
Publication 550 lists acquiring "substantially identical stock for your individual retirement arrangement (IRA) or Roth IRA" among the actions that create a wash sale, and excludes that case from the usual basis adjustment.
What it teaches
Selling a fund at a loss in a taxable account and buying the same fund in your IRA within 30 days does not just postpone the loss; it destroys it. Coordinate every account, including a spouse's.
Source
Internal Revenue Service (2026). Publication 550: Investment income and expenses, "Wash Sales."

Decisions

Harvest or hold?

Scenario 1 · Maya

Suppose Maya's broad US stock index fund falls from $100 to $70 this fall. Her newest lot in the taxable account, 80 shares bought at $92 (INV-035), would show a $1,760 loss. She has also set up an automatic $300 purchase of the same fund in her Roth IRA each month, and the next one is 12 days away. She wants to harvest the loss. What should she do?

Sell now regardlessThe Roth IRA purchase 12 days later is an acquisition of the same fund within 30 days. The loss on as many shares as it buys (about 4 shares for $300 at $70, roughly $94 of the loss) is disallowed, and because the purchase is in an IRA it is not added to any basis: that part is gone for good.
Swap funds and redirect the automatic buyRight. The whole $1,760 loss is kept and she stays invested. It offsets gains, or up to $3,000 a year of her salary at 12% federal plus 2.75% Ohio, and any excess carries forward.
Never sell at a lossWaiting to "get back to even" is the disposition effect (INV-060). The tax code rewards selling losers, and swapping to a similar fund keeps her market exposure unchanged.
The principle: before harvesting, list every purchase of the same security in every account in the 30 days before and after.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers' first retirement year

In the year after they retire, the Harpers will have about $20,000 of interest and $4,000 of qualified dividends, no wages, and large gains in their taxable technology stocks. They also want to start Roth conversions. How should they use the year?

Gains onlyResets basis on about $107,100 of gains with no federal tax (North Carolina would tax the gain at 3.99%), but gives up a year of low-bracket conversions that could shrink future required distributions.
Conversions onlyUses the low brackets for conversions taxed at 10% to 12%, but the gains in the tech stocks stay locked, and so does their concentration risk.
Model them togetherRight. Conversions are ordinary income and fill the brackets first; gains stack on top. A $50,000 conversion cuts the 0% room from about $107,100 to $57,100. The best split depends on how much of the tax-deferred balance they want to convert over the whole window (INV-038).
The principle: gain harvesting and Roth conversions compete for the same low-income years; plan them as one budget.

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

After netting all gains, you have a $12,000 net capital loss and file jointly. How much carries forward to next year, in dollars?

$3,000 offsets ordinary income this year; $9,000 carries forward.
Exercise 2

You buy 100 shares for $1,000, sell them for $750, and 10 days later buy 100 shares of the same stock for $800. What is your basis in the new shares, in dollars?

The $250 loss is disallowed and added to the new cost: $800 + $250 = $1,050 (Publication 550's own example).
Exercise 3

A single filer has $40,000 of taxable income in 2026 before any capital gains. How much long-term gain can they realize at the 0% rate, in dollars?

0% room = $49,450 − $40,000 = $9,450, assuming no Social Security or phase-outs are affected.
Exercise 4

With no future capital gains, how many tax years does it take to use up a $20,000 capital loss carryover at $3,000 a year?

Six years use $18,000; the last $2,000 is used in the seventh: 7 years.
