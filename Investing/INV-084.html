Core Estate Documents

Six short documents decide who raises your children, who pays your bills if you are in a hospital bed, who can talk to your doctors, and who inherits what the rest of your plan does not cover. This module explains each one, shows what state law does when they are missing, and walks each household through its own gaps.

The question this module answersIf I died or could not speak for myself tomorrow, who would be in charge, and would they know what I wanted?

Start here

The paperwork that speaks when you cannot

Most people think of estate planning as something for the rich, or for the old. For the ordinary case that instinct is half right: if you are single, have no children and own little, the state's default rules may send your few belongings roughly where you would want them anyway.

But estate documents do two jobs, and only one of them is about money after death. The other is about you, alive, but unable to act: after a car accident, a stroke, or during advanced dementia. In that situation your spouse or adult child does not automatically have the legal right to sign your checks, sell your house, or even read your medical chart. And if you have children under 18, no amount of money replaces a written statement of who should raise them.

A 2021 Gallup survey found that only 46% of US adults had a will, a share that had stayed between 44% and 51% in Gallup polls going back to 1990. Among adults aged 30 to 49 — the age of most parents of young children — it was 36%. Figure 84.1 sorts the six core documents by the situation each one covers.

The document everyone has heard of

What a will does, and what it cannot do

A will is the oldest estate document, and still the foundation. It is also more limited than most people think. It speaks only after death, only after a court accepts it, and only about property that is in your name alone with no other arrangement for who receives it.

Willalso "last will and testament"
What it is
A signed written document, valid under your state's law, that says who receives your property at death, names the person who will settle your affairs, and, if you have minor children, nominates their guardian.
Used for
Directing probate property: things titled in your name alone with no beneficiary, joint owner or trust. It also names an executor (the person who settles the estate) and can create trusts for heirs.
What it means for you
A will does not control your 401(k), IRA, life insurance, jointly owned house or transfer-on-death accounts. Those pass by their own paperwork, which is why INV-085 on beneficiaries matters as much as this tab.

What makes a will valid

Each state sets its own formalities. Pennsylvania's are among the simplest: any person 18 or older and of sound mind may make a will, and it must be in writing and signed by the person at the end (20 Pa.C.S. §§ 2501–2502). Witnesses are required only if the person signs by mark or has someone else sign at their direction. Many other states require two witnesses in every case, and when a will is presented to the court someone must usually prove the signature, which is why lawyers routinely add witnesses and a notarized self-proving affidavit even where the law does not demand it.

A will also names the executor (in some states called the personal representative), who collects the assets, pays debts and taxes, and distributes what is left. Choosing an organized, trustworthy person who lives reasonably close matters more than choosing the eldest child.

Going furtherDivorce, marriage and old wills

Life events can change a will automatically. In Pennsylvania, a provision in favor of a spouse is ineffective if the person later divorces, or dies during divorce proceedings once grounds are established, unless the will shows it was meant to survive the divorce (20 Pa.C.S. § 2507(2)). Many states have similar rules, but relying on them is risky: the rule may not reach every document, and federal law can override it for employer plans (INV-085). The safer habit is to review every document after any marriage, divorce, birth, death or move to a new state.

Practitioner depthHandwritten and "do-it-yourself" wills

A holographic (handwritten) will is accepted in some states and not others, and states that accept it differ on how much must be in the person's handwriting. Pennsylvania's rule that a will need only be written and signed at the end means a handwritten note can qualify there; the same note might fail in a state that requires witnesses. Online will services can produce valid documents for simple situations, but common failures are in execution (wrong number of witnesses, no signature at the end) and in coordination (a will that leaves "everything to my children equally" while the IRA names only one child). If you use one, follow your state's signing instructions exactly.

If you have children under 18

Who raises the children, and who manages their money

For parents of young children, naming a guardian is the most important sentence in the whole estate plan. If both parents die and no guardian is named, a court decides who raises the children, choosing among relatives and friends who may disagree, without hearing from the only people who knew what the children needed.

Guardian of a minornominated in a will
What it is
The adult with legal authority to raise a child under 18 whose parents have died or cannot care for them. A parent nominates the guardian in a will; the appointment takes effect under state procedures.
Used for
Day-to-day custody and care: where the child lives, school, medical consent.
What it means for you
The guardian who raises your children does not have to be the person who manages their inheritance. Many parents deliberately split the two roles.

Arizona, where the Riveras live, is typical. A parent "may appoint by will a guardian of an unmarried minor," and the appointment becomes effective when the guardian files an acceptance in the court where the will is probated, if both parents are dead or the surviving parent has been judged incapacitated (A.R.S. § 14-5202). A child of 14 or older can block or end the appointment by filing a written objection (A.R.S. § 14-5203). In practice that means a 14-year-old's voice counts, which is another reason to talk with the person you choose and, at the right age, with the children.

Why money left "to the kids" causes trouble

A child under 18 cannot legally own and manage a large inheritance. If life insurance or a 401(k) names minor children directly, or the will leaves them property outright, a court typically has to appoint someone to hold it under court supervision, with annual reports, until the child reaches adulthood — at which point the child receives everything at once. Three better options: a trust for the children in the will or a separate trust (INV-087); a custodian under your state's version of the Uniform Transfers to Minors Act, named on the form or in the will; or naming the surviving spouse first and the trust second.

Going furtherChoosing a guardian

Planners suggest weighing the candidate's age and health, values and parenting style, where they live (moving children away from school and friends after losing their parents is hard), the stability of their household, and whether they actually want the job. Name at least one alternate. Couples should name the same guardians in both wills; conflicting nominations invite a court fight. Money should not decide the choice: if the best guardian cannot afford two more children, leave enough life insurance in trust to make it possible (INV-023 covers how much).

Practitioner depthStandby guardians and single parents

Some states offer a standby or temporary guardianship that a parent can sign during a serious illness so the designated adult can act without waiting for a court. A single parent's nomination is especially important because the other parent, if living and fit, usually has priority; a will cannot simply cut off a living parent's rights. Where there is a real concern about the other parent, the plan should document it and, above all, control the money through a trust with an independent trustee.

Ohio, where Monica lives, lets a parent nominate a guardian of the person, the estate, or both, for "minor or incompetent adult children" in a writing signed before two witnesses or acknowledged before a notary, and the writing can be filed with the probate court as a standby guardian nomination; the court follows the most recent nomination except for good cause (Ohio Rev. Code §2111.121). For a parent whose child will need support as an adult, the nomination should say so, because a will's guardian for a minor stops mattering at 18. Whether an adult child needs a guardian at all, or a less restrictive arrangement such as supported decision-making, is a separate question for his 18th birthday (INV-102).

If you cannot manage your money

The durable financial power of attorney

Imagine Ruth has a stroke and spends three months in rehabilitation. Her Social Security still arrives, but her property tax is due, her IRA's required distribution will need to be taken once she reaches 73, and a furnace breaks. Anna is her only child, but that alone gives Anna no authority to sign for her mother. Without a power of attorney, the usual path is a court guardianship of an adult, which is public, slow, expensive and supervised.

Durable power of attorney
What it is
A signed document in which you (the principal) authorize someone (your agent, sometimes called attorney-in-fact) to act for you in financial matters, and which stays in force if you become incapacitated. That last feature is what "durable" means.
Used for
Paying bills, managing bank and investment accounts, filing taxes, dealing with Social Security and insurers, selling property, and planning for long-term care.
What it means for you
It is the document that avoids a court guardianship. It ends at your death; after that, the executor takes over. See also power of attorney in the glossary.

Pennsylvania's rules, as an example

Pennsylvania presumes every power of attorney is durable unless it says otherwise (20 Pa.C.S. § 5601.1). For documents signed under the current law, the principal's signature must be both acknowledged before a notary and witnessed by two adults, neither of whom may be the agent (§ 5601(b)(3)). The document must begin with a notice, in capital letters, signed by the principal (§ 5601(c)). A document that is valid in one state is usually honored in another, but banks are more comfortable with forms that match local rules.

Immediate or "springing"?

A power of attorney can take effect when signed, or "spring" into effect on a later event such as your incapacity (Pennsylvania allows both, § 5604(a)). Springing documents feel safer, but someone must prove the incapacity — often with a physician's letter — before a bank will accept the agent, and that delay arrives at the worst moment. Many attorneys prefer an immediate document given to a trusted agent, kept with the attorney or in a safe place until needed.

Going furtherThe agent's duties

An agent is a fiduciary: they must act in the principal's interest, keep the principal's money separate from their own, keep records, and avoid conflicts of interest. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau publishes free guides, Managing someone else's money, that explain these duties in plain language, including how to spot and report financial exploitation. Choosing an agent is therefore a judgment about honesty and organization. Naming two agents who must act jointly adds a check but can slow things down; naming a successor agent is almost always wise.

Practitioner depthRetirement accounts and the agent

An agent can usually take distributions from an IRA, but changing its beneficiary is a hot power. For Ruth, whose IRA is her largest asset, the document should say clearly whether Anna may, for example, convert part of the IRA to a Roth IRA (INV-038) or name a trust as beneficiary. Many custodians also insist on their own acceptance procedures; asking the IRA custodian to review the document in advance avoids surprises.

If you cannot speak for yourself medically

Health care powers, living wills and HIPAA

The National Institute on Aging describes the two most common advance directives as the living will and the durable power of attorney for health care. Together with a HIPAA authorization they make sure the right person can talk to your doctors and knows what you would want.

Health care power of attorneynames a health care proxy or agent
What it is
A legal document naming the person who makes medical decisions for you if you cannot communicate them yourself. It is one form of health care directive.
Used for
Consenting to or refusing treatment, choosing providers and facilities, and deciding about life-sustaining care in line with your wishes.
What it means for you
Without one, doctors turn to whomever your state's law ranks as your surrogate — often a spouse, then adult children — who may not be the person you would choose, and siblings may disagree.
Living will
What it is
Your written instructions about medical treatment if you cannot decide for yourself: which common treatments you would want or avoid, and under what conditions.
Used for
Guiding your health care agent and your doctors, particularly near the end of life.
What it means for you
It spares your family from guessing. It is not the same as a Do Not Resuscitate (DNR) order or a POLST (physician orders for life-sustaining treatment) form, which are medical orders signed by a clinician, usually near the end of life.
HIPAA authorizationalso "HIPAA release"
What it is
A signed form allowing health care providers to share your health information with the people you name. Federal privacy rules say a provider may not disclose protected health information without an authorization unless another rule permits it (Code of Federal Regulations, 45 CFR § 164.508).
Used for
Letting family members, or a financial agent who needs medical proof of incapacity, get information before or without a formal proxy role.
What it means for you
Once a child turns 18, parents are no longer automatically entitled to their records. For a young adult like Jordan, a HIPAA authorization and health care power of attorney are the two documents that matter most.

HIPAA itself already treats a properly appointed health care agent as your personal representative: if "a person has authority to act on behalf of an individual who is an adult... in making decisions related to health care," the provider must treat that person as the individual for privacy purposes (45 CFR § 164.502(g)(2)). The separate authorization is still useful because it can name people who are not your agent and can work before you are incapacitated.

The letter of instruction

A letter of instruction is not a legal document, and that is its strength: you can update it any evening. It lists where the will and other originals are, every account and who to call, digital accounts and how to reach them, funeral and burial wishes, and who should receive personal items of sentimental value. The National Institute on Aging's "Getting your affairs in order" checklist is a good template. Keep it with your documents, and tell your executor and agents where it is.

Parents of a child with a disability often write a longer companion, a letter of intent, for whoever cares for the child next: the child's routines, diagnoses, doctors and medicines, school and therapy history, benefits and caseworkers, what calms or upsets them, and the parent's hopes for where and how the child will live. Like the letter of instruction it is not legally binding, and its value is in the detail. Monica's letter about Eli would guide his guardian and the trustee of his trust; she should update it every year.

Going furtherDo you need a lawyer for advance directives?

Not always. The National Institute on Aging notes that you can set up advance directives for little or no cost and that many states have their own forms you can complete for free. A lawyer is most useful when your situation is complicated. What matters is using a form valid in your state, signing it with the required witnesses or notary, and giving copies to your agent and doctors. People who live in two states may want to complete each state's form.

The state's default plan

What happens when there is no will

Dying without a will is called dying intestate. Every state has an intestacy statute that acts as a default will: it lists who inherits, in what shares, and in what order. The statute cannot know about a stepchild you raised, a partner you never married, a favorite charity, or a child who needs money managed for them. It also cannot name a guardian.

Pennsylvania spouse's share, when all descendants are also the spouse's: $30,000 + ½ × (intestate estate − $30,000)

Pennsylvania's rules (20 Pa.C.S. §§ 2102–2103) and Arizona's (A.R.S. §§ 14-2102–2103) show how much states differ. In Arizona, if every child is also the surviving spouse's child, the spouse takes everything. In Pennsylvania, the spouse takes the first $30,000 plus half of the rest, and the children split the other half. For a $400,000 intestate estate, a Pennsylvania spouse receives $215,000 and the children $185,000; an Arizona spouse receives all $400,000.

Going furtherPennsylvania's 2025 change for people with no relatives

If no relatives qualify, property traditionally "escheats" to the state. Act 50 of 2025 amended 20 Pa.C.S. § 2103, effective January 16, 2026: such estates now pass first to an endowed community fund in the decedent's municipality, then school district, then county, and only then to the Commonwealth. The Pennsylvania Department of Revenue describes the change on its inheritance tax page.

Practitioner depthWhy a will still matters when intestacy "gets it right"

For Ruth, intestacy would send everything to Anna, her only child, which is what she wants. A will still adds three things: it names Anna as executor (without a will, the Register of Wills appoints an administrator under a statutory priority list, and a bond may be required); it names a contingent heir if Anna dies first; and it can include specific gifts and a trust if Anna's circumstances change. Intestacy also requires heirs to survive by five days in Pennsylvania (20 Pa.C.S. § 2104(10)); a will can set a longer survival period.

Real history and law

What missing documents cost

Case study 1Prince, 2016–2022: a fortune and no will
What happened
The musician Prince died in Minnesota in April 2016. His sister filed to open a probate case days later, stating that no will had been found. He was unmarried and had no surviving children, so under Minnesota intestacy law his siblings, including half-siblings, were the heirs. A bank was appointed to administer the estate while hundreds of people claimed to be relatives; in May 2017 a judge ruled that his six siblings were the heirs. CNN reported in August 2022 that the estate, about $156 million, had been settled six years after his death.
What it teaches
Even when intestacy law produces a sensible list of heirs, the absence of a will means no chosen executor, no instructions, no privacy and years of court supervision. A will or trust would also have let him leave money to people or causes the statute ignores.
Source
Rothman, M. (2017, May 19). Prince's 6 siblings are heirs to his estate, judge rules. ABC News; Garvey, M. (2022, August 3). Prince's estate is finally settled after a 6-year battle. CNN.
Case study 2Pennsylvania rewrites the last step of its default will (2025)
What happened
Pennsylvania's Act 50 of 2025 amended the order of intestate succession, effective January 16, 2026. When no relative qualifies to inherit, the estate now goes to an endowed community fund in the decedent's municipality, school district or county before it can pass to the Commonwealth.
What it teaches
The "default will" is written by legislators and can change without your knowledge. Only your own will fixes your choices in place, and only until you change it.
Source
20 Pa.C.S. § 2103(a)(6), as amended by Act of Nov. 24, 2025, P.L. 308, No. 50; Pennsylvania Department of Revenue, Inheritance tax.
IllustrationA single parent's missing nomination
The setup
A hypothetical single parent dies leaving a 15-year-old and a $300,000 life insurance policy that names the child directly. There is no will.
The result
A court must appoint a guardian for the child, and, separately, someone to hold the insurance money under court supervision. When the child reaches adulthood, the remaining money is typically paid to them outright.
What it teaches
Two short documents — a will naming a guardian and a trust or custodian named as the policy's beneficiary — would have avoided both court proceedings. This is Monica's situation today, with a sharper edge: Eli, 13, has a lifelong disability, so money paid to him outright would count against the $2,000 resource limit of Supplemental Security Income (SSI) in 2026 and could cost him SSI and the Medicaid that goes with it. For her, the beneficiary should be a supplemental needs trust for Eli (INV-087), not a custodian and never Eli himself.

Decisions

Three households, three gaps

Scenario 1 · The Riveras

Marcus and Elena want Elena's sister in Tucson to raise Sofia and Leo if they both die, but she is poor with money. Marcus's brother is a careful accountant but lives in Chicago. What should their wills say?

Brother as guardianPossible, but it moves the children to another city, away from Elena's family, for a financial reason that a trustee could solve instead.
Split the rolesRight. Each person does what they are good at. The children stay near family; their life insurance and 401(k) money is managed by a careful trustee, with instructions about school, health and when they receive it. Both wills should name the same people.
No willsArizona intestacy would send each parent's estate to the survivor, and then to the children, but a court would choose the guardian and supervise the money until each child turns 18.
The principle: separate "who raises them" from "who manages the money," and write both down.
Scenario 2 · Ruth

Ruth is healthy, but a friend's long guardianship case frightened her. She wants Anna to be able to help, but worries about "handing over control." What kind of power of attorney fits?

Springing powerIt protects control, but proving incapacity can delay access for weeks at the moment Ruth needs bills paid. Some institutions hesitate to accept springing documents.
Immediate, carefully draftedA strong choice. Anna's authority is ready when needed, Ruth decides exactly which hot powers Anna has, and physical control of the document limits premature use. Ruth keeps full control of her own accounts.
Joint ownershipIt gives Anna access but also makes her a co-owner: the accounts pass to her at death outside the will, may be exposed to her creditors, and raise Pennsylvania inheritance-tax questions of their own (INV-089).
The principle: a power of attorney lends authority; joint ownership gives property away.
Scenario 3 · Jordan

Jordan, 24, lives in Denver; her parents are in another state. She owns little and assumes estate documents are for later. Which would you do first?

NothingHer assets are small, but a serious accident is the risk she actually faces. Without a health care agent, providers follow Colorado's default rules for choosing a surrogate decision-maker, and her parents have no automatic right to her records.
Health documents and beneficiariesRight. These cost little or nothing, often use a free state form, and cover the situations most likely at her age. A simple will can follow.
The principle: at 24, the incapacity documents matter more than the will.

Six things people say about estate documents

Apply it

What these documents mean for each household

Health first. A health care power of attorney and HIPAA authorization, using Colorado's forms, and confirming the beneficiary on her 401(k) and any group life insurance. A simple will can come later, when she has more to leave.
The urgent case. Matching wills naming a guardian for Sofia and Leo, with a trust for the children and a trustee; durable and health care powers of attorney for each other. Arizona's intestacy law would treat the survivor well, but it cannot name a guardian.
The most urgent case in the course. Monica has none of these documents, and Eli's father is not involved. She needs a will naming a guardian and an alternate for Eli, with an Ohio nomination that also covers him as an adult; a letter of intent about his care; a supplemental needs trust named as beneficiary of her $290,000 401(k), $40,000 Roth IRA and $92,000 group life policy, never Eli directly (INV-085, INV-087); and durable and health care powers of attorney naming a trusted adult, because no spouse can step in.
Update, don't start over. Their wills likely date from when Ethan was young. With Ethan now 20, the guardian clause is moot, but executor and trustee choices, powers of attorney and health care directives deserve review before retirement.
Incapacity is the bigger risk. Living alone at 68, Ruth most needs a durable power of attorney and health care power naming Anna (with a successor), a living will, and a letter of instruction. Her will should name Anna as executor and a contingent heir.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A Pennsylvania resident dies without a will, leaving a spouse and two children who are also the spouse's children. The intestate estate is $400,000. How much does the spouse receive, in dollars?

Under 20 Pa.C.S. § 2102(3): $30,000 + ½ × ($400,000 − $30,000) = $30,000 + $185,000 = $215,000. The two children split the remaining $185,000, $92,500 each.
Exercise 2

A Pennsylvania resident dies without a will, leaving a spouse and a mother but no descendants. The intestate estate is $250,000. How much does the spouse receive, in dollars?

Under § 2102(2): $30,000 + ½ × $220,000 = $140,000. The mother receives $110,000. In Arizona the spouse would receive all $250,000.
Exercise 3

An Arizona resident dies without a will, survived by a spouse and a child from an earlier marriage. The intestate estate is $400,000, of which 60% is the decedent's half of community property and 40% is separate property. How much of it does the spouse receive, in dollars?

Separate property: 40% × $400,000 = $160,000. Under A.R.S. § 14-2102(2) the spouse takes one-half of it, $80,000, and none of the decedent's $240,000 half of community property, which goes to the child. (The spouse already owns their own half of the community property.)
Exercise 4

In Gallup's 2021 survey, 20% of adults 18–29, 36% of adults 30–49, 53% of adults 50–64 and 76% of adults 65 and older had a will. In the age group that includes the Riveras, what percentage had a will?

Marcus is 35 and Elena 34, in the 30–49 group: 36%, so roughly two in three parents of that age had no will.
