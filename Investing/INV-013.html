Commodities and Gold

Gold, oil, wheat and copper are real things you can hold, and they sometimes rise just when stocks and bonds fall. They also pay no interest, no dividends and no rent. This module explains what gold and commodities have actually done, why a commodity fund can trail the commodity it tracks, and what the evidence says about using them against inflation.

The question this module answersShould gold or commodities have a place in my portfolio, and if so, how much and in what form?

Start here

Things you could drop on your foot

Most of this course is about claims on businesses and borrowers: stocks give you a share of profits, bonds pay you interest. Gold and other commodities are different. They are physical goods. Owning them gives you no share of anyone's earnings; your return is simply what the next buyer will pay.

That is not automatically a flaw. In some decades, the 1970s above all, raw materials soared while stocks and bonds lost money after inflation. The ordinary case for owning a little gold or a commodity fund is exactly this: it behaves differently.

The catch is that "behaves differently" cuts both ways. Figure 13.1 shows 98 years of data. A dollar in US stocks with dividends reinvested grew to about $11,570; a dollar in gold grew to about $210; prices in general rose about 19-fold. Gold beat inflation over the whole span, but by far less than stocks, and much of its gain came in a few bursts separated by decades of losses.

What this module covers

  1. Gold's history, including the decades when its price was fixed by law, and what that means for the data.
  2. Gold as an investment since 1972: returns, volatility, and very long droughts.
  3. Commodity futures: how commodity funds actually hold oil, grain and metals.
  4. Roll yield and contango: why a fund can lose money when the commodity does not.
  5. The evidence on inflation hedging, taxes on gold and futures, and how much to hold.

Before you read the data

When the price of gold was set by law

For most of American history gold was not an investment in the modern sense; it was money, or the thing money was promised in. Knowing that history matters because it changes how you should read any long chart of gold's "returns."

Gold standardmoney convertible into gold at a fixed price
What it is
A system in which the currency can be exchanged for a fixed weight of gold. From 1900 the US dollar was defined as a fixed amount of gold, equal to $20.67 per ounce.
Used for
Anchoring the value of money. Central banks had to hold gold reserves and honor conversions, which limited how much money they could create.
What it means for you
While the price was fixed, gold could not rise or fall much in dollars. The "returns" in a data set for those years mostly reflect government decisions, not investors' buying and selling.

The Federal Reserve History essays tell the story in four steps. In 1933, during the banking crisis, President Roosevelt suspended the gold standard, and the Treasury was given the power to compel the surrender of gold coins and certificates. The Gold Reserve Act, signed on January 30, 1934, transferred monetary gold to the Treasury and set the price at $35 an ounce, which cut the dollar's gold value to 59% of its 1900 level. The 1944 Bretton Woods agreement kept the dollar fixed to gold at $35. On August 15, 1971, President Nixon closed the "gold window," so foreign governments could no longer exchange dollars for gold. Americans have been able to own gold freely and legally since 1974, when President Ford signed an act of Congress permitting it.

⚠️

Dataset caveat. The course's historical data carry a gold return for every year from 1928, and long-run averages such as "gold 5.6% a year since 1928" include the official-price era. For questions about how gold behaves as a freely traded asset, this module uses 1972–2025, the first full calendar years after convertibility ended. Even that choice matters: 1972 was the eve of gold's best decade, so results starting then flatter gold.

Going furtherWhy a fixed price did not mean a stable economy

A fixed gold price made the dollar stable in gold, not in what it could buy: consumer prices fell 6–10% a year in 1930–1932 and rose 18% in 1946 with gold unchanged at $35 (course data).

Practitioner depthReading pre-1972 gold data

A long gold series splices an administered price onto a market price; the course data show larger moves from 1968 to 1970, when a two-tier system separated the private market from official settlements. When a gold statistic begins before 1971, ask whether those years were treated as market data.

Since 1972

High volatility, no income, long droughts

With the market era isolated, gold's record looks like this. From 1972 through 2025 gold returned 8.9% a year, or 4.8% after inflation. Stocks returned 11.1% a year, or 6.9% after inflation. But gold's typical yearly swing, measured by the standard deviation of annual returns, was 27.3%, against 17.0% for stocks. Gold was the riskier asset and the lower-returning one.

Store of value
What it is
Something expected to keep its purchasing power over long periods, so wealth can be carried from today into the future.
Used for
The main argument for gold: it cannot be printed, has been valued for millennia, and is not anyone's promise to pay.
What it means for you
"Over long periods" can mean longer than you have. Someone who bought gold at the end of 1980 waited 31 years to get back to even after inflation.

Going furtherHow gold is taxed

The Internal Revenue Service (IRS) treats gold, silver and platinum bullion, and coins, as collectibles. Net long-term gains on collectibles are taxed at a maximum rate of 28% rather than the 0%, 15% or 20% rates that apply to stocks held more than a year (IRS Tax Topic 409). IRS Publication 550 adds that collectibles gain includes gain from selling an interest in a trust due to unrealized appreciation of collectibles, which is why shares of physically backed gold trusts are generally taxed this way. Check the tax section of any fund's prospectus. If your ordinary tax rate is below 28%, the lower rate applies.

Practitioner depthGold in an individual retirement account (IRA)

Under Internal Revenue Code section 408(m), an IRA that buys a collectible is treated as having distributed the money. The statute makes exceptions for certain US-minted gold, silver and platinum coins, coins issued under state law, and gold, silver, platinum and palladium bullion of at least the fineness required for futures delivery, if the bullion is in the physical possession of the IRA's trustee. Keeping an IRA's bullion at home does not meet that test and risks the purchase being treated as a taxable distribution. Buying shares of a gold fund inside an IRA generally avoids the issue, and sidesteps the 28% collectibles rate while the shares stay in the account.

How funds hold oil and wheat

You cannot keep barrels in a brokerage account

Gold is compact and does not spoil, so a fund can simply store bars in a vault. Oil, natural gas, corn and cattle are bulky, costly to store or perishable. Almost every fund that offers "commodities" therefore holds futures contracts instead of the goods themselves.

Futures contractCommodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) definition
What it is
An agreement to buy or sell a commodity for delivery in the future at a price set today, which obligates both sides and may be satisfied by delivery or by an offsetting trade.
Used for
Farmers, airlines and refiners use futures to lock in prices; investors and funds use them to gain price exposure without storing the goods.
What it means for you
A commodity fund's return is the return on a series of contracts, not on the commodity. Those two can differ by several percentage points a year.

A futures position requires only a deposit, called margin, which the CFTC describes as a performance bond rather than a partial payment. Gains and losses are settled in cash every day ("marked to market"). That is leverage: a small deposit controls a large amount of the commodity. A commodity fund for ordinary investors is usually fully collateralized: it holds futures with a face value equal to its assets and keeps the rest of the money in Treasury bills.

Contango and backwardation
What they are
Contango: futures for later delivery cost more than nearer ones. Backwardation: later ones cost less. Contango is common when storage is plentiful and costly; backwardation when the commodity is scarce right now.
Used for
Reading what the market expects and, for a fund, estimating the cost or benefit of rolling its contracts forward.
What it means for you
A fund that keeps buying the next month's contract in steep contango bleeds value even if the spot price never moves. The tool on the next tab shows how much.
Futures fund return ≈ change in spot price + roll yield + interest on collateral − expenses

Gary Gorton and Geert Rouwenhorst, studying an equally weighted index of fully collateralized commodity futures from 1959 to 2004, found that commodity futures had offered about the same return and Sharpe ratio (return per unit of risk) as stocks, while being negatively correlated with stock and bond returns and positively correlated with inflation. Because the index was fully collateralized, its return included the interest on the collateral and the effect of rolling, not just changes in the price of the goods.

Going furtherProtections that do not apply

SIPC protects customers' securities and cash at failed brokerage firms. SIPC states that it does not protect commodity futures contracts (unless they are held in a special portfolio margining account) or cash held in connection with a commodities trade. Shares of an exchange-traded commodity fund held at a brokerage are securities; the futures inside the fund are not your personal account's assets.

Practitioner depthMargin, marked to market

The CFTC glossary distinguishes initial margin, required when a position is opened, from maintenance margin, the level below which the broker demands more money, and describes mark-to-market as the daily cash flow system that adds each day's gains to, and subtracts each day's losses from, every account. In an illustration, a contract for 1,000 barrels at $70 controls $70,000 of oil. If the initial margin were $7,000, a $7 fall in price would erase the deposit, and the broker would call for more money the same day. Fully collateralized funds avoid margin calls by holding the full face value in Treasury bills.

The hidden cost or bonus

Selling low, buying high, every month

Futures expire. A fund that wants to keep exposure must, before each expiration, sell the contract that is about to expire and buy a later one. That trade is called the roll. If the later contract costs more (contango), the same dollars buy fewer contracts after each roll. If the spot price never moves, the fund still shrinks.

Roll yield
What it is
The gain or loss from replacing expiring futures with later ones. Negative in contango, positive in backwardation.
Used for
Explaining the gap between a commodity's spot price and the return of funds that hold its futures.
What it means for you
Before buying a single-commodity futures fund, look at the shape of the curve and the fund's long-run tracking of spot, not just the headline price of oil or gas.

Try the defaults: a spot price rising 2% a year, a 4% annual roll cost, 3.5% interest on collateral and a 0.75% expense ratio. The fund earns about 0.6% a year while the commodity rose 2%. Now set the roll yield to +3% (backwardation): the fund beats the spot price by a wide margin. Neither case is a forecast; the shape of the curve changes over time and differs across commodities.

Going furtherWhy funds hold more than one month

Some funds spread holdings across several months or choose contracts by the shape of the curve, which can reduce roll costs but changes what the fund tracks. The prospectus explains the method; the long-run record against the spot price shows the result.

Practitioner depthHow futures are taxed

Regulated futures contracts are section 1256 contracts. IRS Publication 550 explains that any you hold at year-end are treated as sold at fair market value on the last business day of the year (marked to market), and that 60% of the gain or loss is treated as long-term and 40% as short-term, regardless of how long you held the contract. Commodity funds organized differently from ordinary mutual funds can produce different tax reporting; read the tax section of the prospectus before buying in a taxable account.

The evidence

A hedge over centuries, unreliable over years

"Gold protects against inflation" is the most common reason people buy it. The evidence is mixed in a way that matters. Claude Erb and Campbell Harvey, in "The Golden Dilemma" (Financial Analysts Journal, 2013), concluded that gold may be an effective hedge if the investment horizon is measured in centuries, but that over practical investment horizons gold is an unreliable inflation hedge.

Inflation hedge
What it is
An asset whose value tends to rise when inflation rises, so its real value holds up.
Used for
Protecting the purchasing power of savings, especially for retirees and anyone with long-dated spending in today's dollars.
What it means for you
Ask how reliable the link is, and over what horizon. For Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) it is written into the bond; for gold and commodities it is a loose historical tendency.

The US data agree. Since 1972 the correlation between gold's yearly return and the year's inflation rate was 0.41: positive but loose. In the 2021–2022 inflation surge, gold lost 15.1% of its purchasing power over the two years, worse than stocks (−7.6%). Then in 2023–2025 it more than doubled after inflation (+117%). Anyone who bought gold "for inflation" in 2021 was hurt during the inflation and rewarded after it.

💡

If inflation protection is the goal, there is a direct tool. TIPS and I bonds adjust their principal or interest with the Consumer Price Index by law. Their link to inflation is contractual; gold's is a tendency. INV-008 and INV-010 cover them.

Starting in 1972, gold raised both return and smoothness. Starting in 1981, just after gold's peak, it lowered return but still smoothed the ride and softened 2022. The consistent effect is less volatility; the effect on return depends on when you started. This course treats 0 to about 10% as the range the evidence can support for investors who value the diversification, and none for investors who would abandon it after a decade of lagging.

Going furtherBroad commodity funds versus gold

A broad commodity index fund holds futures on energy, metals and agriculture. Its link to inflation is tighter in principle, because energy and food prices are part of the Consumer Price Index, and Gorton and Rouwenhorst found commodity futures positively correlated with unexpected inflation. But it inherits roll yield, which can be strongly negative for years, and energy often dominates the index. Gold has no roll cost when held physically, but a looser link to consumer prices.

Practitioner depthWhy rebalancing matters more for volatile, zero-income assets

An asset with 27% volatility drifts far from its target weight. Rebalancing sells it after surges (1979, 2025) and buys after collapses (1981, 2013), so part of the benefit in the table comes from that discipline rather than from gold's own return.

Real history

Two episodes worth knowing

Case study 1When Americans could not own gold, 1933–1974
What happened
In 1933 the Treasury was given the power to compel the surrender of gold coins and certificates, and the gold standard was suspended. The Gold Reserve Act of January 30, 1934 transferred all monetary gold, including coins and bullion held by individuals, to the Treasury, with holders paid in currency at $35 an ounce. Private ownership of gold did not become freely legal again until 1974.
What it teaches
A "crisis asset" can be regulated in a crisis. Gold held as an asset outside the financial system still depends on laws, and those laws have changed before.
Source
Federal Reserve History: "Roosevelt's Gold Program" and "Gold Reserve Act of 1934."
Case study 2Oil below zero: April 20, 2020
What happened
The May 2020 West Texas Intermediate (WTI) crude oil futures contract fell from $17.73 a barrel to settle at −$37.63 on April 20, the day before it expired: the first negative price since the contract was listed 37 years earlier. The CFTC's staff report cites an oversupplied market, a collapse in demand from COVID-19 and worries about storage; the Energy Information Administration explains that holders at expiration must take physical delivery in Cushing, Oklahoma. During the turmoil, the futures broker of United States Oil Fund (USO) restricted it from adding new oil futures positions. In November 2021 the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and the CFTC settled charges that the fund and its general partner made misleading statements about that limitation; without admitting or denying the SEC's findings, they agreed to pay $2.5 million in penalties.
What it teaches
A futures price is not the price of oil in a tank; it is the price of an obligation. An "oil ETF" (exchange-traded fund) can behave very differently from what an investor expects, and even a fund's ability to follow its own strategy can be limited by its brokers.
Source
CFTC, Interim staff report on trading in NYMEX WTI crude oil futures (November 2020); EIA, Today in Energy (April 2020); SEC press release 2021-229 (November 8, 2021).

Decisions

Two households, two gold questions

Scenario 1 · Ruth

A radio advertiser tells Ruth that "paper money is doomed" and suggests moving 25% of her $780,000 IRA, about $195,000, into gold coins kept at home, through a company that will "set up everything." What should she do?

Do itGold lost 77% of its real value from 1980 to 2001. Metals an IRA owner keeps at home risk failing section 408(m), which requires an IRA's bullion to be in the trustee's possession; the purchase could then be treated as a distribution, with income tax due on $195,000. Dealer markups also take an immediate cut.
A small fund position, if anyA reasonable approach. Ruth's Social Security of $2,900 a month already rises with inflation each year, and TIPS are indexed to prices by law. A few percent in a gold fund can diversify without betting her retirement on one metal.
Coins in taxable savingsAvoids the IRA rule but not the markups, storage, theft risk or collectibles tax rates. Her $60,000 of cash is her safety reserve, not money for a volatile asset.
The principle: buy inflation protection that is contractual first; treat gold as a small diversifier, never as a retirement plan.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers

Worried about inflation before they retire, the Harpers want to put $50,000 of their taxable account into a single-commodity natural gas futures fund they saw rising on the news. What should they check first?

Assume it tracks gasSingle-commodity futures funds can lag their commodity badly in contango, as the roll tool shows. A price rise in the news may already be in the futures curve.
Check how it actually behavesRight. Their real risk is concentration: $220,000 in 25 technology stocks. Diversifying that, and adding TIPS to their bond holdings, addresses inflation and concentration together (INV-010, INV-035).
The principle: know what the fund holds, not just what its name says.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A futures curve is in contango: each next-month contract costs 1% more than the one expiring. The spot price stays flat and you ignore collateral interest and fees. After 12 monthly rolls, what is the fund's return, in percent?

Each roll turns $1 of contracts into 1 ÷ 1.01 = 0.9901 as many. Twelve rolls: 0.9901¹² = 0.8874, a return of −11.3%. In practice the fund also earns interest on its collateral, which offsets part of this.
Exercise 2

In 2026, an investor in the 32% federal bracket sells shares of a physically backed gold trust, held more than a year in a taxable account, for a $20,000 gain. Their long-term rate on stock gains would be 15%. How many more dollars of federal tax do they owe than on a $20,000 stock gain?

Collectibles: $20,000 × 28% = $5,600. Stock: $20,000 × 15% = $3,000. Difference: $2,600. (State tax and the net investment income tax are ignored here.)
Exercise 3

In 2022 stocks returned −18.04%, 10-year Treasuries −17.83% and gold +0.55%. What did a 55% stock / 35% Treasury / 10% gold mix return, in percent?

0.55 × (−18.04) = −9.92; 0.35 × (−17.83) = −6.24; 0.10 × 0.55 = +0.06. Total −16.1%, against −18.0% for 60/40.
Exercise 4

From 1972 to 2025 gold compounded at 8.89% a year and consumer prices at 3.90% a year. What was gold's real return per year, in percent?

Real return = (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1 = 1.0889 ÷ 1.0390 − 1 = 4.8% a year. The shortcut 8.89 − 3.90 = 4.99 slightly overstates it.
