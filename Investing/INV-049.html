Asset Allocation Models

A handful of famous recipes — 60/40, the three-fund portfolio, the Permanent Portfolio, All Weather, the Golden Butterfly, all stocks, and "your age in bonds" — compete for the same job: deciding how much of your money sits in growth assets and how much in shock absorbers. This module tests each one on 98 years of US data and shows you how to read those results without fooling yourself.

The question this module answersWhich ready-made portfolio recipe fits me, and how much should I trust the backtest that makes it look good?

Start here

Every portfolio is a recipe

If you have ever followed a recipe, you already understand an allocation model. A recipe does not tell you which brand of flour to buy; it tells you how much flour, how much sugar and how much butter. An allocation model does the same for money: so much in stocks, so much in bonds, so much in cash, perhaps some gold.

No single recipe is right for every household. A broad stock fund and a broad bond fund in a proportion you can hold is a practical baseline, but reasonable schools disagree about whether bonds improve a lifetime plan, whether age should drive the mix, and whether separate goals need separate portfolios. The famous recipes are competing arguments about what can go wrong — inflation, deflation, a lost decade for stocks, or running out of money — and each builds its mix around a different risk. Seeing them side by side shows what you are really choosing: which failure you most need to prevent and which bad decade you can live through.

What this module covers

  1. What an allocation model is, and the three decisions inside every one.
  2. The stock–bond family: 100% stocks, 80/20, 60/40, 40/60 and the three-fund portfolio.
  3. Age-based rules such as "110 minus your age."
  4. The "all-season" portfolios: Permanent Portfolio, All Weather and Golden Butterfly.
  5. A comparison tool that backtests all of them on 1928–2025 data.
  6. How to read a backtest honestly, and three historical episodes that broke the rankings.

The building blocks

Three decisions hide inside every model

Before comparing recipes, name the parts. Every allocation model answers three questions, whether or not it says so out loud.

Asset allocationthe mix, not the picks
What it is
The division of a portfolio among broad asset classes — stocks, bonds, cash, and sometimes real assets such as gold — expressed as percentages.
Used for
Setting how much a portfolio can grow and how far it can fall. The mix, far more than the individual funds chosen, decides how a diversified portfolio behaves in a crash.
What it means for you
Choosing between an S&P 500 fund and a total-market fund is a small decision. Choosing between 90% and 50% in stocks is a big one.
Allocation modela named, rule-based mix
What it is
A fixed recipe or rule for the mix, such as "60% stocks and 40% bonds" or "25% each in four assets," usually with a rule to restore the targets periodically.
Used for
Replacing moment-to-moment judgment with a plan you can follow when markets are frightening.
What it means for you
A model is only useful if you can hold it through its worst year. Pick one whose bad years you have looked at in advance.

The arithmetic of a mix is simple. Over one year, a rebalanced portfolio's return is the weighted average of its parts:

rportfolio = w1r1 + w2r2 + … + wnrn, with w1 + … + wn = 100%

In 2008, for example, the S&P 500 returned −36.55% and 10-year Treasuries +20.10% (Damodaran data). A 60/40 mix therefore returned 0.6 × (−36.55%) + 0.4 × 20.10% = −13.9%. Over many years, though, the growth rate of the mix is not the average of the parts' growth rates, because rebalancing sells what rose and buys what fell, and because volatility itself drags down compound growth (INV-003). That is why models must be tested on real sequences of returns, which is what the rest of this module does.

Going furtherWhy the mix matters more than the picks

For a portfolio built from broad index funds, almost all the year-to-year variation comes from the asset mix, because each fund already holds thousands of securities. The picks start to matter more when a portfolio is concentrated — the Harpers' 25 technology stocks behave much more like one large bet than like "the stock market" (INV-004). Every model in this module assumes the stock and bond pieces are broad, low-cost funds.

Practitioner depthHow this module measures each model

Every result uses calendar-year total returns from Damodaran's dataset (1928–2025): the S&P 500 with dividends for stocks, the 10-year Treasury bond for bonds, 3-month Treasury bills for cash, and the gold price. Each model is rebalanced to its targets at every year-end. Growth per year is the compound annual growth rate, (ending value ÷ starting value)1/n − 1. Real growth deflates each year by CPI-U inflation: (1 + r) ÷ (1 + i) − 1. Deepest fall is the largest decline from a previous year-end peak to a later year-end low; falls within a year were deeper. Longest under water counts consecutive year-ends spent below a previous peak. No fees or taxes are deducted.

The mainstream recipes

100% stocks, 60/40 and the three-fund portfolio

The most widely used models hold only two ingredients: stocks and high-quality bonds. They differ only in proportion.

The 60/40 portfolio
What it is
60% stocks and 40% high-quality bonds, rebalanced periodically. Its cousins are 80/20 (more growth) and 40/60 (more stability).
Used for
A middle-of-the-road default: a long-standing benchmark for "balanced" funds and pension portfolios.
What it means for you
Over 1928–2025 it grew 8.3% a year versus 10.0% for all stocks, with a worst year of −27.3% instead of −43.8%. You trade some growth for a much shallower bottom.
The three-fund portfolio
What it is
A total US stock market fund, a total international stock fund and a total US bond fund, in proportions you choose. It is a 60/40-style portfolio with the stock half spread across the world.
Used for
Owning nearly every listed company and investment-grade bond with three low-cost funds.
What it means for you
It is the do-it-yourself twin of a target-date fund (INV-050) and was popularized by the Bogleheads community (INV-044). The international question is covered in INV-054.

Two patterns show up in every period you test. First, each step from 40/60 to 100% stocks added roughly a percentage point of growth a year over the long run. Second, the price of that point was a bigger worst case: the all-stock portfolio's year-end value fell 65% from 1928 to 1932. The question "how much in stocks?" is therefore really "how deep a hole can I sit in without selling?" INV-025 on risk tolerance and capacity is the place to answer it honestly.

Going furtherThe three-fund portfolio cannot be tested back to 1928 here

This course's long dataset has no international stock series. Kenneth French's data library does have value-weighted returns for developed markets outside the US from 1991 onward, so the comparison tool runs a three-fund model (36% US, 24% international, 40% Treasuries) from 1991. Over 1991–2025 it grew 8.2% a year against 9.1% for a US-only 60/40 — because this happened to be one of the strongest stretches in US history. INV-054 shows the decades when the opposite was true.

Rules of thumb

"Your age in bonds" and its descendants

The oldest shortcut ties the mix to your age: hold your age in bonds and the rest in stocks. A 30-year-old would hold 70% stocks; a 70-year-old, 30%. As people began living longer and bond yields fell, variations appeared that hold more stocks: 110 minus your age, or 120 minus your age.

Age-based allocation rule
What it is
A formula that sets the stock share from your age, such as stocks = 110 − age (in percent). It automatically becomes more conservative every year.
Used for
A simple, self-adjusting starting point; it is the idea behind target-date glide paths (INV-050).
What it means for you
The rule knows your age and nothing else. It ignores your pension, Social Security, job stability and how much you have saved — all of which change how much risk you can carry.
Stocks (%) = 110 − age   ⇒   at 58: 110 − 58 = 52% stocks, 48% bonds

The straight line is the rule's weakness as much as its strength. Economists think of your future paychecks as a bond-like asset called human capital. At 25 you own decades of future earnings and very little in investments, so your total wealth is mostly "bond" already; you can afford a stock-heavy portfolio. At 64 your paychecks are almost used up. That logic supports a mix that stays heavy in stocks for a long time and then de-risks in the last decade or two before retirement — the shape of the Vanguard line in Figure 49.4 — rather than a steady one-point-a-year decline from your twenties.

Tested on history, the 110-minus-age rule for someone who was 40 at the start of 1972 grew 8.1% a year through 2025, against 9.4% for a fixed 60/40 — not because the rule is bad, but because it held under 50% stocks from age 61 onward, during the strong markets of the 1990s and 2010s. Its reward came in calm: by 2010, at 78, it held 32% stocks, a mix many retirees find easy to live with.

Assessment, not a universal verdict. Start with required cash flows and loss capacity, then select the simplest mix that funds them. Going furtherWhen one age does not describe the money

Age rules can err in both directions, and Maya shows both at once. At 58 the rule gives her 52% stocks. For the money she will spend in her own retirement, starting at about 67, that may be close to right: she has a single income, no second earner to fall back on, and the years just before and after retirement are when a large loss does the most damage (sequence risk, INV-077). But part of her savings may one day fund a supplemental needs trust for her son Eli, who is 13 and may need support for 50 years or more (INV-102). For that money the horizon is far longer than her age implies. A rule keyed to one person's age cannot see a second person's lifetime; it is a starting point to adjust for human capital and for whom the money is really for, not a replacement for looking at the whole balance sheet.

The all-weather family

Permanent Portfolio, All Weather and Golden Butterfly

A different school starts from economic scenarios instead of from stocks. The idea: no one knows whether the next decade brings growth or recession, inflation or deflation, so hold something that does well in each and balance them. Figure 49.5 shows the logic.

Permanent PortfolioHarry Browne
What it is
25% each in stocks, long-term Treasury bonds, cash (Treasury bills) and gold, rebalanced periodically. Browne introduced the concept in Inflation-Proofing Your Investments (1981, with Terry Coxon); his last investment book was Fail-Safe Investing (1999).
Used for
Balancing four conditions equally: prosperity (stocks), deflation (long bonds), inflation (gold) and recession (cash).
What it means for you
It has had remarkably shallow drawdowns since the 1970s, and markedly lower growth than stock-heavy portfolios whenever stocks did well.
All WeatherBridgewater Associates, 1996
What it is
A strategy Bridgewater launched in 1996, originally for Ray Dalio's trust assets, that puts equal risk on four growth-and-inflation scenarios. The institutional version uses leverage and inflation-linked bonds; retail versions are simplified, unleveraged approximations.
Used for
Holding a portfolio designed "to be indifferent to shifts in discounted economic conditions," in Bridgewater's words; it is the origin of the "risk parity" movement.
What it means for you
You cannot buy the real thing in a 401(k). The version tested here (30% stocks, 55% 10-year Treasuries, 15% gold) is this module's own stand-in, not Bridgewater's portfolio.
Golden ButterflyPortfolio Charts
What it is
20% each in US large-cap stocks, US small-cap value stocks, long-term Treasuries, short-term Treasuries and gold, created by the author of the Portfolio Charts website as a variation on the Permanent Portfolio tilted toward prosperity.
Used for
Keeping the Permanent Portfolio's resilience while adding growth.
What it means for you
It was designed with the historical record in view, so its backtests are partly the data it was built from (see "Reading a backtest"). This module's data lack a small-cap value series, so the test uses 40% S&P 500 in place of both stock slices.

⚠️

Gold's history splits in two. Until 1933 the dollar was convertible into gold at $20.67 an ounce; the Roosevelt administration suspended the gold standard in 1933 and, with the Gold Reserve Act of 1934, reset the link. Under the postwar Bretton Woods system dollars were convertible into gold at $35 an ounce, until President Nixon closed the "gold window" on August 15, 1971 (Federal Reserve History). So gold's pre-1971 "returns" in any dataset describe a controlled price, not a market; the dataset used here even shows yearly moves in the fixed-price era that a fixed official price would not produce. Treat gold-heavy results before the 1970s as unreliable, and notice how much gold's post-1971 history does to the rankings.

The comparison tool

Backtest every model yourself

Choose a period and a model to highlight. The table ranks all of them on the same five measures; the chart plots the highlighted model against 100% stocks and 60/40. The default period starts in 1972, after gold began trading freely.

With the default settings (1972–2025) you should see a surprising table. The Permanent Portfolio grew 8.5% a year, the Golden Butterfly stand-in 9.2% and 60/40 9.4%, while their worst years were −8.3%, −10.3% and −18.0% respectively (all in 2022). 100% stocks grew 11.1% a year, with a worst year of −36.5% (2008). On these numbers the gold portfolios look like a free lunch: almost 60/40's growth with half the pain.

Now move the first year to 1981. The Permanent Portfolio drops to 7.3% a year against 60/40's 10.1%, and the gap in ending wealth becomes enormous. What changed? Nothing about the models — only the start date. Gold rose from $35 to a record in 1980, then fell for two decades: the dataset's gold series lost 53.5% from 1981 through 2000. Any test that starts in 1972 includes gold's best decade in history; any test that starts in 1981 includes its worst. The next tab turns this into rules for reading every backtest you will ever see.

Practitioner depthWhat the tool does and does not include

Rebalancing to exact targets every December 31 is an idealization; real investors rebalance with bands, cash flows and taxes (INV-056). Year-end values understate drawdowns: a market can fall steeply within a year and recover before December 31, and the tool never sees it. The 10-year Treasury stands in for "long-term Treasuries," which were more volatile, so the Permanent Portfolio's real historical swings were larger than shown. No fund fees, no trading costs and no taxes. Taxes can differ by ingredient: gold coins are collectibles, and net gains on collectibles are taxed at a maximum 28% rate (IRS Topic 409), so ask how any gold product is taxed before holding it in a taxable account (INV-030).

Honest caveats

Six questions to ask of any backtest

Backtest
What it is
A simulation of how a rule or portfolio would have performed on past data, as if someone had followed it.
Used for
Checking whether an idea survived real history, and seeing its worst periods before you live through them.
What it means for you
A backtest shows one path the world took. It is evidence about how a model behaves, not a forecast of what it will earn.
  1. Where does it start and end? Starting in 1972 flatters gold; starting in 1981 punishes it; ending in 2025 includes gold's 66% gain that year. Always test several start dates.
  2. Was the model designed after seeing the data? A recipe built by trying combinations on past returns will look good on those returns. That is look-ahead bias and overfitting. The Permanent Portfolio has decades of results after its 1981 publication; newer models have far less out-of-sample history.
  3. Are the ingredients real? Our All Weather and Golden Butterfly stand-ins substitute available series for missing ones. Many online backtests do the same without saying so.
  4. How coarse is the data? Year-end data miss drops inside a year; monthly data miss drops inside a month. Real drawdowns were always at least as deep as shown.
  5. Which country? This is US data, from the country whose stock market did best in the 20th century. Survivorship matters (INV-054).
  6. After costs, taxes and behavior? A model you abandon in its worst year has no long-run return at all. The behavior gap is covered in INV-061.
Real return = (1 + nominal return) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1    e.g. (1.094 ÷ 1.039) − 1 = 5.3%

That formula explains the "after inflation" column. Inflation averaged 3.9% a year from 1972 to 2025 (CPI-U), so 60/40's 9.4% nominal growth is 5.3% in real terms — the number that tells you what your money could actually buy.

Going furtherWhy a longer backtest is not automatically a better one

It is tempting to trust 98 years over 54. But the longer test mixes regimes that may never recur: a gold standard, bond yields held down by the Federal Reserve during and after World War II, and markets without index funds or cheap trading. A long test is best used to see the range of things that happened, not to pin down a single "expected" return.

History that broke the rankings

Three episodes every model must survive

Case study 1The inflation decade, 1972–1980
What happened
Consumer prices rose 12.3% in 1974 and 12.5% in 1980. Over 1972–1980 the S&P 500 grew 7.9% a year before inflation but lost 0.7% a year after it; a 60/40 mix lost 2.1% a year after inflation. Gold, freed from its official price, rose about 33.6% a year. The Permanent Portfolio mix gained 5.6% a year after inflation.
What it teaches
Stocks and nominal bonds can both fail against inflation for a decade. The all-weather school was built on memories of exactly this period; if it recurs, a small allocation to real assets such as TIPS or gold earns its keep.
Source
Computed from Damodaran, NYU Stern, historical returns 1928–2025 (S&P 500, 10-year Treasury, T-bill, gold, CPI).
Case study 2Gold's long slide, 1981–2000
What happened
After peaking in 1980, gold lost 53.5% over 1981–2000 in this dataset, while stocks enjoyed one of their best two decades. From 1981 through 2018 the Permanent Portfolio grew 6.7% a year and 60/40 10.0% a year: after 38 years, 60/40 ended with about three times as much wealth.
What it teaches
Insurance has a premium. A portfolio that holds 25% in an asset with no cash flows will lag badly whenever that asset is out of favor, and the lag can last longer than most investors' patience.
Source
Computed from Damodaran, NYU Stern.
Case study 32022: the year 60/40 had nowhere to hide
What happened
With inflation at 6.5% and interest rates rising fast, the S&P 500 returned −18.0% and 10-year Treasuries −17.8%. A 60/40 mix lost 18.0%, its third-worst year in the dataset, after 1931 and 1937. The Permanent Portfolio mix lost 8.3%, cushioned by T-bills (+2.1%) and gold (+0.6%).
What it teaches
Bonds protect best against recessions and deflation, not against inflation shocks. That is the core argument for holding more than one kind of shock absorber (INV-004 shows how the stock–bond correlation changes).
Source
Computed from Damodaran, NYU Stern.

Decisions

Choosing a recipe for a real household

Scenario 1 · The Riveras

Marcus saw a Golden Butterfly backtest online and wants to move their $62,000 of 401(k)s into it. Their plans offer index funds for US stocks, international stocks and bonds, a money market fund and target-date funds — no gold and no small-cap value fund. They are 35 and 34. What should they do?

Build it anywayPossible, but it means a model that holds 40% in bonds and cash plus 20% gold for a couple with 30 years of paychecks ahead of them — human capital already provides their ballast. It also adds an account, a tax wrinkle (collectibles rates on gold ETF gains) and the temptation to tinker.
Simple and stock-heavyA defensible choice. The backtest they saw is not a reason to hold 60% outside stocks at 35. They should also consolidate their six overlapping funds (INV-004) and make sure they capture the full employer match.
Wait in cashIndecision has a cost. Vanguard's research found money held in cash usually lagged invested money (INV-051). They can pick a sensible default now and refine it later.
The principle: the best model is one you can build from the funds you have and hold for decades.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers

Tom and Rachel are 57 and 55 and plan to retire around 62–63. Their $1.1 million is roughly 80% in stocks, including $220,000 in 25 technology stocks. Their advisor proposes moving to a 60/40 mix over the next two years. Rachel asks whether 100% stocks would "make up for lost time."

100% stocksThe historical worst year was −43.8% (1931) and −36.5% as recently as 2008. A loss like that two years before retirement can force a later retirement or lower spending: sequence risk (INV-078).
Toward 60/40Sensible. Near retirement, a mix whose worst year was about −27% (1931) and −18% since 1972 is easier to hold. Selling concentrated winners in the taxable account should be staged to manage capital gains (INV-035, INV-037).
Permanent PortfolioIt would have limited losses since 1972 but grown far less in the long stretches when stocks led; with perhaps 30 years of retirement ahead, too little growth is also a risk.
The principle: near retirement, reduce the chance of a devastating loss first; chasing extra return comes last.
Scenario 3 · Ruth

Ruth, 68, has $780,000 in her IRA and $60,000 in CDs and savings. A newsletter says she should hold "your age in bonds": 68% bonds. Her Social Security survivor benefit of $2,900 a month covers most of her basic bills. What should she think about?

Follow the ruleNot wrong, and very safe. But she may live another 25 years, and her IRA mostly funds extras and emergencies; a lower stock share means less growth against inflation.
Adjust for Social SecurityRight. A reliable, inflation-adjusted income stream lets many retirees hold somewhat more in stocks than an age rule suggests — as long as they can stomach the swings. Her required minimum distributions from 2031 will need cash available too (INV-081).
The principle: count guaranteed income as part of your safe assets before applying any rule of thumb.

Six things people say about allocation models

Apply it

What this module means for each household

The rule says 86%; a target-date fund says about 90%. At 24, with $3,000 saved and 40 years of earnings ahead, a single stock-heavy fund in her 401(k) is the simplest sound model. Her first priorities are the employer match and her 6.8% loans (INV-020).
Pick one model and consolidate. Six overlapping large-cap funds are not a model. A three-fund split or a target-date fund, at roughly 80–90% stocks, fits their horizon; their $41,000 idle in checking is the more urgent fix.
The rule says 52%, but her money has two horizons. Across her $370,000 of invested savings, 52% stocks is $192,400. For her own retirement nine years out, a 50–60% stock model that she will hold through a crash fits a single earner facing sequence risk (INV-077); money likely to end up in a trust for Eli has a much longer horizon. Her $30,000 of cash is her emergency fund, $4,200 short of its $34,200 target (INV-019), not part of the model.
Moving from about 80% toward 60% makes sense before retiring. Five or six years out, the Harpers are in the window where a model's worst year matters most. They should compare their advisor's proposal with a target-date fund for the same year (INV-050).
Count Social Security as her bond. The rule says 42% stocks for Ruth. Her survivor benefit covers most fixed costs, so a balanced model with a cash bucket for two to three years of withdrawals may suit her better than a rigid formula.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

In 2008 stocks returned −36.55% and 10-year Treasuries +20.10%. What did a 60/40 portfolio return, in percent (one decimal)?

0.6 × (−36.55%) = −21.93%; 0.4 × 20.10% = +8.04%; total −13.9%.
Exercise 2

In 2022: stocks −18.04%, 10-year Treasuries −17.83%, T-bills +2.09%, gold +0.55%. What did a Permanent Portfolio (25% each) return, in percent?

Equal weights, so take the simple average: (−18.04 − 17.83 + 2.09 + 0.55) ÷ 4 = −33.23 ÷ 4 = −8.3%.
Exercise 3

A portfolio grew 9.4% a year while inflation averaged 3.9% a year. What was its real growth rate, in percent?

(1 + 0.094) ÷ (1 + 0.039) − 1 = 1.0529 − 1 = 5.3%. Simply subtracting (9.4 − 3.9 = 5.5%) slightly overstates it.
Exercise 4

Maya is 58 with $370,000 invested (her $400,000 of savings minus $30,000 of emergency cash). Under the 110-minus-age rule, how many dollars would be in stocks?

110 − 58 = 52% stocks; 0.52 × $370,000 = $192,400. The other 48%, $177,600, would be in bonds.
