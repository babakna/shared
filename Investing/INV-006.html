Reading a Company: Statements and Valuation

A share is a slice of a real business. This module walks through one fictional company's three financial statements, the handful of ratios that matter, and what a stock's price quietly assumes about its future. Then it steps back to the whole market, with 145 years of valuation data.

The question this module answersHow do I tell what a company earns, what it owns and owes, and whether its price makes sense?

Start here

Would you buy the coffee shop on the corner?

Imagine a friend offers to sell you a share of the coffee shop down the street. Before you answered, you would ask four questions: How much does it sell? How much of that is profit? What does it own and what does it owe? And what are you being asked to pay?

Those are exactly the questions investors ask of a public company, just at a larger scale. Public US companies must answer them in standardized financial statements filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), which anyone can read for free. This module uses a fictional company, Lakeside Coffee Co. (ticker LKSD), with round numbers so the arithmetic stays visible.

💡

Why this module matters even for index investors. You may never pick a stock. But market-wide valuation, dividend yields and earnings are in the news every day, and understanding them protects you from both hype and panic. Tab 6, "The whole market," applies all of this to the market as a whole.

What it sells and earns

From revenue to earnings per share

The income statement starts with revenue (everything customers paid) and subtracts costs in layers until only profit is left. Each layer answers a different question about the business.

Earnings per share (EPS)
What it is
Net income divided by the number of shares outstanding: the profit attributable to one share over a period.
Used for
Comparing profits across time and companies of different sizes, and computing the P/E ratio.
What it means for you
Growth in EPS over many years is, in the end, what drives a stock's long-run value. Watch for "adjusted" EPS that leaves out costs the company would rather you ignore.
Going furtherGAAP versus "adjusted" earnings

US companies report under Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP). Many also report "adjusted" or non-GAAP figures that exclude items such as restructuring charges or stock-based pay. Some adjustments are reasonable; others turn recurring costs into "one-time" items every year. SEC rules require companies to reconcile non-GAAP measures to the nearest GAAP figure, so you can always see the difference.

What it owns and owes

Assets = liabilities + equity

The balance sheet is a snapshot on one day. Everything the company owns (assets) was paid for either with borrowed money (liabilities) or with owners' money (equity). The two sides always balance — hence the name.

Book value and return on equity
What it is
Book value is shareholders' equity on the balance sheet. Return on equity (ROE) is net income ÷ equity — how much profit the business earns on the owners' capital. Lakeside: $120M ÷ $600M = 20%.
Used for
Judging how productively a company uses its capital, and valuing asset-heavy businesses such as banks.
What it means for you
Book value is an accounting number, not a market value. Brands, software and know-how are often barely recorded, which is why many excellent companies trade far above book value.
Going furtherDebt: helpful until it is not

Borrowing lets a company grow faster and raises ROE when things go well, but interest must be paid in bad years too. Two quick checks: debt-to-equity (Lakeside 0.83) and interest coverage, operating income ÷ interest ($180M ÷ $28M ≈ 6.4 times). Coverage below about 2–3 times leaves little room if profits fall. Lenders are paid before owners, so high debt makes the shares riskier.

Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact

Does the profit turn into cash?

Accounting profit includes estimates and timing choices. The cash flow statement shows the actual cash that came in and went out, in three groups: operating (running the business), investing (buying equipment, acquisitions) and financing (borrowing, repaying, dividends, buybacks).

Free cash flow (FCF)
What it is
Operating cash flow minus capital expenditures: cash available for dividends, buybacks, debt repayment or growth.
Used for
Testing the quality of earnings, and valuing businesses by the cash they generate.
What it means for you
A company reporting rising profits but persistently weak or negative free cash flow deserves a closer look. It is the pattern behind many accounting problems.

Lakeside paid $48M of its $90M free cash flow as dividends ($0.96 a share) and used $20M to buy back shares. A company that pays out more than its free cash flow for long has to borrow or sell assets to keep doing it.

What am I paying?

Price is what you pay; value is what you get

Lakeside's shares trade at $48. With 50 million shares, the whole company is valued at $2.4 billion — its market capitalization. Ratios put that price in context. Change the inputs below to see how each ratio moves.

Price-to-earnings ratio (P/E)
What it is
The share price divided by earnings per share, usually over the last twelve months ("trailing") or the next twelve ("forward").
Used for
A quick gauge of how much optimism is in a price, and comparisons within an industry.
What it means for you
A high P/E is not "expensive" by itself — it may reflect fast expected growth — and a low P/E is not "cheap" by itself; it may reflect a shrinking business. The ratio tells you what the market expects, not whether it is right.
Practitioner depthWhat a price implies: the Gordon growth model

If a dividend grows at a constant rate g forever and investors require return r, the price is P = D1 ÷ (r − g) (Gordon, 1959). Rearranged, g = r − D1/P: expected return ≈ dividend yield + growth. At $48, a 2% yield and a 9% required return imply about 7% growth, forever. Reverse-engineering the growth a price requires is often more useful than forecasting growth yourself: it tells you what has to go right. The model breaks down when g approaches r and ignores buybacks, which is why analysts also discount free cash flow.

Valuation at the market level

145 years of the Shiller CAPE

Economist Robert Shiller popularized a market-wide valuation measure: the cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio (CAPE), the S&P Composite's price divided by the average of the last ten years of inflation-adjusted earnings. Averaging over a decade smooths out booms and recessions.

⚠️

CAPE is a thermometer, not a clock. CAPE was above its long-run average for most of 1990–2026. Anyone who sold whenever it looked "high" would have missed much of that period's growth. Its main practical use is setting realistic long-term return expectations for planning, which Stages 10 and 11 use.

Real history

When price and value parted ways

Case study 1The dot-com bubble, 1995–2002
What happened
Excitement about the internet pushed technology shares to extreme valuations. The Nasdaq Composite closed at a record 5,048.62 on March 10, 2000, then fell about 78% to 1,114.11 by October 9, 2002. It did not close above its 2000 record again until April 23, 2015. The market-wide CAPE reached 44.2 in December 1999, its highest level on record.
What it teaches
Many companies of that era were real and important; the internet did change the world. The problem was the price. When a valuation already assumes decades of spectacular growth, even a good outcome can disappoint. The S&P 500 itself lost money in 2000, 2001 and 2002.
Source
Kiplinger, "3 lessons for investors from the tech bubble" (2015); NPR, "15 years after the dot-com bust, a Nasdaq record" (2015); Shiller, ie_data.xls; Damodaran, NYU Stern.
Case study 21982: the cheapest market in two generations
What happened
After a decade of high inflation and weak markets, the CAPE fell to 6.6 in July 1982, and many investors had given up on stocks. Stocks bought in January 1982 went on to return about 13.5% a year after inflation over the next ten years.
What it teaches
The best long-term buying opportunities tend to arrive when sentiment is worst. Few investors could act on that in 1982, which is exactly why prices were low.
Source
Shiller, ie_data.xls (CAPE and real total return).

Decisions

Judge the price, not just the story

Scenario 1 · A dividend that looks too good

Monica, thinking about the income she will need once she stops working at about 67, finds a stock with a 9% dividend yield and thinks it would be a safe source of income. The company's payout ratio is 140% and its free cash flow has fallen for three years. What should Monica conclude?

A bargainA yield far above the market's usually means investors expect a cut. Paying out 140% of earnings means the company is funding dividends from debt or reserves.
A warning signRight. This is often called a "yield trap." If the dividend is cut, the price usually falls further, and the income disappears with it. Diversified dividend funds (INV-047) spread this risk.
The principle: an unusually high yield is a question, not an answer.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers' favorite tech stock

The Harpers own a fast-growing tech company trading at 60 times earnings. It has grown profits 25% a year for five years. Should they buy more?

YesPast growth is known; future growth is not. Few companies sustain 25% growth for a decade, and at 60 times earnings the price has little room for disappointment.
Maybe, with questionsRight. It could still be a fine business and a reasonable holding. But the Harpers' 25-stock portfolio is already all technology (INV-004); adding to their largest bet increases concentration at the point in life when a large loss hurts most.
The principle: a great company is not automatically a great investment; price matters.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A stock trades at $54 and earned $3.00 per share over the last year. What is its P/E ratio?

P/E = 54 ÷ 3.00 = 18. Its earnings yield is 1 ÷ 18 = 5.6%.
Exercise 2

The same stock pays $1.35 a year in dividends. What is its dividend yield, in percent?

1.35 ÷ 54 = 0.025 = 2.5%. Its payout ratio is 1.35 ÷ 3.00 = 45%.
Exercise 3

If you require an 8.5% return from this stock, what constant dividend growth rate does its price imply, in percent? (Use g ≈ r − dividend yield.)

g ≈ 8.5% − 2.5% = 6% a year, indefinitely.
Exercise 4

Lakeside earned $120M of net income on $600M of shareholders' equity. What is its return on equity, in percent?

ROE = 120 ÷ 600 = 20%.
