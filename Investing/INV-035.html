Taxable Accounts and Cost Basis

An ordinary brokerage account has no contribution limits and no withdrawal rules, and every sale in it is a tax event. What you owe depends on a number most investors never look at: the cost basis of the particular shares you sell. This module shows how basis is built, how the lots you choose change your tax, the special rules for employee stock purchase plan shares, and the records that let you prove it all.

The question this module answersWhen I sell, which shares am I selling, and how do I keep the tax as low as the law allows?

Start here

Selling "100 shares" is not one decision but several

When you sell shares, your broker shows a gain or loss and a Form 1099-B arrives the next winter. Most people accept that number without asking where it came from, and in a simple account that is fine.

But if you bought the same stock or fund at different times and prices, each purchase is a separate tax lot with its own cost basis and its own holding period. Selling "160 shares" out of 400 means choosing which 160. The choice can move your gain from long-term to short-term, from thousands of dollars to a few hundred, or from a gain to a loss, without changing a cent of what you receive.

Monica Walker has $40,000 in a taxable brokerage account, all in one broad US stock index exchange-traded fund (ETF). She bought it in four purchases over 14 years and takes the dividends in cash, so at today's price of $100 she holds 400 shares in four lots (Figure 35.1). She wants to sell 160 shares, $16,000, toward replacing her car in 2029 (INV-024); her emergency-fund gap is closed from take-home pay instead (INV-019). This module is built around that sale.

The starting number

Cost basis, and what changes it

Adjusted basis
What it is
What you paid for an investment, including commissions and fees, then adjusted: increased by reinvested dividends (each is a new purchase), by ordinary income already taxed on employee shares, and by disallowed wash-sale losses; decreased by returns of capital; divided across shares after a split.
Used for
Gain or loss = amount realized − adjusted basis. Every dollar of basis you can document is a dollar that is not taxed again.
What it means for you
Forgetting reinvested dividends, or the ESPP income already on your W-2, overstates your gain and means paying tax twice on the same money.
Covered security
What it is
A security for which your broker must report cost basis to you and the IRS on Form 1099-B. Generally, stock acquired after 2010 is covered; other securities, such as fund shares and many bonds, were phased in over the following years.
Used for
Pre-filling Form 8949 and Schedule D with the basis the broker has on file.
What it means for you
For older ("noncovered") shares, or shares transferred between brokers without their history, the basis is your responsibility. The broker's figure can also be wrong, most often for ESPP and stock option shares, as the next tabs show.

Sources: IRS Publication 550 (2025), "Generally, a covered security is a security you acquired after 2010, with certain exceptions"; IRS Publication 550, average basis for mutual fund shares acquired after 2011 in dividend reinvestment plans.

Which shares did you sell?

First in, first out; specific identification; average cost

MethodHow it worksAllowed for
First in, first out (FIFO)The shares you bought first are treated as sold first.The legal default for stocks and bonds when you do not adequately identify the shares.
Specific identificationYou name the lots sold, by date or price, at the time of the sale, and receive written confirmation from the broker.Stocks, bonds, funds. "Highest cost first" and "lowest tax" are broker menu versions of it.
Average costBasis per share is the average across all shares held in the account.Mutual fund shares and shares acquired after 2011 through a dividend reinvestment plan held with a custodian, not ordinary individual stock.

Source: IRS Publication 550 (2025), "Identifying stock or bonds sold", "Identification not possible" and "Choosing average basis for mutual fund shares". Once you use average basis for a fund, you generally must keep using it for that fund unless you revoke the election under the rules in Publication 550.

Specific identification
What it is
For shares held at a broker, an adequate identification requires two things: you tell the broker which shares to sell at the time of the sale, and the broker confirms it in writing within a reasonable time (IRS Publication 550). Most brokers let you set a standing method or pick lots on the order screen.
Used for
Choosing the lots that produce the gain, loss or holding period you want.
What it means for you
You cannot pick lots after the fact at tax time. Set your account's default method before you sell, and check the trade confirmation.

Try it

Which of Monica's shares should go?

The calculator applies the 2026 federal rates to each method, stacking the gains on Monica's estimated $56,810 of taxable income as head of household: her $92,000 salary, minus $11,040 of 401(k) deferrals and the $24,150 standard deduction for 2026 (INV-029, INV-030).

At the defaults, FIFO costs $151.50 of federal tax, highest-cost-first $76.80 (its 80 short-term shares of lot D are taxed at 12%), and the lowest-tax specific identification, all 100 shares of lot C and 60 of lot B, costs nothing: its $5,800 of long-term gain fits inside the $9,390 of room Monica's taxable income leaves under the 0% long-term ceiling, $66,200 of taxable income for head of household in 2026. The lowest bill is not automatically the best choice, though. FIFO realizes $10,400 of gain, $9,390 of it at 0% and only $1,010 at 15%, so the shares Monica keeps carry $7,520 less unrealized gain than under highest-cost-first and $4,600 less than under the lowest-tax choice. If those gains are later taxed at 15%, that is $690 of future tax avoided for $151.50 more today. A hand-picked mix can do better than all three: 60 shares of lot A and 100 of lot B realize $9,200 of long-term gain, all of it at 0%. The general lesson: when you have room at 0%, realizing long-term gains is close to free, and tax-gain harvesting becomes the goal (INV-037).

Practitioner depthHow the calculator computes the tax

For FIFO it sells the oldest lots first and for highest cost the lots with the highest basis first; for "lowest tax" it tries every mix of lots in 20-share steps and keeps the cheapest (ties go to the mix that realizes the least gain). For each choice it splits the gain into short- and long-term, adds short-term gain to ordinary income and runs the 2026 ordinary and 0/15/20% schedules on the total, then subtracts the tax Monica would owe with no sale. It ignores state tax (Ohio taxes gains as ordinary income at 2.75% above $26,050 for 2026) and the net investment income tax (NIIT), which Monica is far below.

Short or long, and the ESPP twist

Holding periods, and how ESPP shares are taxed

As INV-030 explained, a lot held more than one year is long-term; count from the day after you acquired it to the day you sell, inclusive. Every reinvested dividend and every ESPP purchase starts its own clock. Two rules extend a holding period: shares received as a gift can take on the donor's holding period, and inherited property is always treated as long-term (IRS Publication 550).

Employee stock purchase plan (ESPP)section 423 plans
What it is
A plan that lets employees buy company stock through payroll deductions, often at a discount of up to 15% (the price may not be below 85% of market value) and sometimes at the lower of the price at the start or end of the offering period. Rights to buy are limited to $25,000 of stock a year, valued at the grant date.
Used for
Buying employer shares at a discount. Nothing is taxed when you buy; tax arrives when you sell.
What it means for you
How the sale is taxed depends on whether it is a qualifying or disqualifying disposition.
Qualifying dispositionDisqualifying disposition

⚠️

The Form 1099-B trap. For options granted on or after January 1, 2014, including ESPP rights, the basis your broker reports on Form 1099-B does not include the ordinary income already reported on your W-2. If you use the 1099-B number as is, you pay tax on that income twice. IRS Publication 525 says it is your responsibility to correct the basis on Form 8949.

A preview

The wash-sale rule, and why ESPP buyers trip over it

Selling at a loss can reduce your taxes, up to $3,000 a year against ordinary income plus unlimited offsets against gains (INV-030). The wash-sale rule stops you from claiming that loss while keeping the position.

Wash sale
What it is
A sale at a loss where, within 30 days before or after, you buy substantially identical stock or securities, acquire them in a fully taxable trade, acquire an option to buy them, or buy them in your individual retirement account (IRA) or Roth IRA. A purchase by your spouse or a corporation you control also counts.
Used for
Disallowing a loss that is not a real change in your investment.
What it means for you
The loss is not lost, only postponed: it is added to the basis of the replacement shares, and their holding period includes the old shares'. Except when the replacement is bought in an IRA: then the loss is gone for good.

The practical rules are simple. An employee selling ESPP shares at a loss should do it more than 30 days away from the plan's purchase dates. For Monica, if lot D ever shows a loss worth harvesting, she must not buy the same fund within 30 days either side of the sale, including through automatic monthly purchases, dividend reinvestment, or her Roth IRA; taking dividends in cash, as she does, removes one trap. INV-037 covers tax-loss harvesting in full, including what "substantially identical" means for funds.

Proving it

Record keeping, and basis for gifts and inherited assets

What to keep

  • Trade confirmations and year-end statements showing each purchase date, quantity and cost.
  • Records of reinvested dividends and return-of-capital distributions.
  • ESPP Forms 3922 and stock option Forms 3921, and the W-2s showing the ordinary income.
  • For gifts: the donor's basis and purchase date, and the value on the date of the gift. For inheritances: the value at the date of death (Schedule A of Form 8971 if the estate filed an estate tax return, or an appraisal).
  • Form 8606 for any nondeductible IRA contributions (INV-032), and records of wash-sale adjustments.

Step-up in basispreview of INV-090
What it is
When someone dies, the basis of most assets they owned outside retirement accounts resets to fair market value at death, so gains that built up during their life are never taxed as income.
Used for
Estate planning: highly appreciated assets are often better held until death, or given to charity, than sold or given to children during life, who would take the old basis.
What it means for you
It does not apply to IRAs or 401(k)s, which have no basis step-up; and it does not apply to appreciated property you gave the decedent within one year before death that comes back to you (IRS Publication 551).

Real rules and IRS examples

Three cases

Case study 1Broker basis reporting, from 2011
What happened
For stock acquired after 2010, and in stages for mutual funds, dividend-reinvestment shares and bonds, brokers must report cost basis to the IRS on Form 1099-B. Before that, the IRS saw only sale proceeds, and taxpayers alone supplied the basis.
What it teaches
Reporting reduced errors, but it also created two classes of shares: covered, where the broker's number is usually right, and noncovered, where it may be blank. Many older portfolios, and positions moved between brokers, still depend on the owner's records.
Source
IRS Publication 550 (2025), "covered security".
Case study 2The IRS's own ESPP arithmetic
What happened
In Publication 525's Example 10, an employee buys 100 ESPP shares for $20 when they were worth $22 at grant, and sells them 14 months later for $30: $200 of wages and $800 of capital gain. In Example 11 the same shares are sold 6 months after purchase: $300 of wages (the $23 purchase-date value minus $20), and $700 of capital gain.
What it teaches
Holding ESPP shares longer can convert some ordinary income into capital gain, but for a concentrated position that tax saving is usually small next to the risk of holding (INV-004). Many employees sell soon after each purchase and accept the ordinary income.
Source
IRS Publication 525 (2025), Examples 10 and 11.
Case study 32010: the one year without a step-up
What happened
For people who died in 2010, the federal estate tax briefly did not apply, and executors could elect a system under which heirs generally took the decedent's basis, with limited increases, by filing Form 8939. Heirs of such estates may still hold property with that carryover basis.
What it teaches
Basis rules for inherited property are set by Congress and can change. Anyone who inherited in 2010 should check which basis applies before selling.
Source
IRS Publication 550 (2025), "Inherited property" (Form 8939).

Decisions

Three basis decisions

Scenario 1 · Monica

Monica will sell 160 shares of her index fund at $100. The broker's default is FIFO. What should she do?

Accept the defaultIt happens to work well this year, but by luck. In a year when Monica has no 0% room, FIFO would cost 15% on the full $10,400.
Compare, then chooseRight. The choice must be made at the time of sale, with written confirmation. Selling 60 shares of lot A and 100 of lot B realizes $9,200 of long-term gain at no federal tax, which raises the basis of what she owns for the retirement years when she will draw on this account.
Always highest costIt realizes the least gain, but here it sells the newest shares, which are short-term and taxed at 12%, so it is not even this year's cheapest choice (100 shares of lot C and 60 of lot B cost nothing), and it leaves large gains for later years when rates may be higher.
The principle: lot selection is a choice about which years pay the tax, not just about this year's bill.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers

The Harpers give $5,000 to charity each year by check and take the standard deduction. Their technology stocks include lots with very large gains. Is there a better way?

All cashSimple. From 2026, P.L. 119-21 lets non-itemizers deduct up to $2,000 of cash gifts on a joint return, worth $440 to the Harpers at 22%, but the other $3,000 of cash gives no federal benefit.
Split cash and sharesRight. The new non-itemizer deduction applies only to cash (not stock, and not gifts to donor-advised funds), so the first $2,000 is best given in cash. Giving long-term shares for the rest avoids their capital gain: on $3,000 of shares with $2,400 of gain, $360 of tax at 15%. Donors who itemize can deduct gifts of appreciated stock held more than a year at fair market value (IRS Publication 526); INV-039 covers bunching.
The principle: give your lowest-basis shares to charity and keep, or sell, your highest-basis ones; use cash where the tax law rewards cash.
Scenario 3 · Ruth

Ruth wants to give Anna 200 shares of a stock Walter bought decades ago, now worth $30,000. Should she give them now or leave them to Anna?

Same either wayNot necessarily. A gift passes on Ruth's basis; an inheritance generally resets it to value at death.
Establish the basis firstRight. If the shares came to Ruth when Walter died, their basis is generally their value in 2023 for the part inherited, so the gain since then may be small. INV-090 covers step-up in detail and INV-085 joint ownership.
The principle: before gifting appreciated assets, compare carryover basis with a future step-up.

Apply it

Basis questions for each household

Start with good habits. When Jordan opens a taxable account, she can set her broker's default to specific identification and keep her funds simple, so basis is easy to track.
No basis questions yet. Their $41,000 of cash has no gain. If they invest it in a taxable account, broad index funds or exchange-traded funds (ETFs) with low turnover keep annual taxable distributions small (INV-012).
Name lots in writing, and use the 0% room every year she works. At her income about $9,390 of long-term gain a year can be realized at no federal tax. Selling and immediately buying back is allowed for gains (the wash-sale rule applies only to losses), so she can raise her basis year by year before she starts drawing on the account in retirement.
Map every lot. With 25 stocks and about $120,000 of gain, a lot-by-lot list lets them sell high-basis lots first, give low-basis lots to charity, and spread the rest over years to stay under the NIIT threshold (INV-030).
Document the basis of anything inherited from Walter. Values at his death in 2023 set the starting point for assets that passed to her; her IRA has no basis to track unless nondeductible contributions were made.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

Monica sells 160 shares at $100 using FIFO. What is the total gain, in dollars?

All 120 shares of lot A: 120 × ($100 − $30) = $8,400. Then 40 of lot B: 40 × ($100 − $50) = $2,000. Total $10,400, all long-term.
Exercise 2

You buy 100 shares for $1,000, sell them for $750, and within 30 days buy 100 shares of the same stock for $800. What is your basis in the new shares, in dollars?

The $250 loss is disallowed and added to the new cost: $800 + $250 = $1,050 (IRS Publication 550, Example 1).
Exercise 3

ESPP shares: option price $20, value $22 at grant, $23 at purchase. 100 shares are sold 6 months after purchase for $30. How much is ordinary (wage) income, in dollars?

A disqualifying disposition: wages equal the spread at purchase, ($23 − $20) × 100 = $300; the other $700 is capital gain (Publication 525, Example 11).
Exercise 4

You own 100 fund shares bought at $20 and 100 at $30, and use average cost. You sell 50 shares at $28. What is your gain, in dollars?

Average basis ($2,000 + $3,000) ÷ 200 = $25. Gain 50 × ($28 − $25) = $150.
