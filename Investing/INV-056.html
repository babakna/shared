Rebalancing

A portfolio built at 60% stocks does not stay at 60%. Markets move the mix every day, usually toward more stocks and more risk. Rebalancing is the routine that brings it back. This module shows how far portfolios drift, compares the main rebalancing rules on 98 years of data, and shows how to rebalance with new money and without needless tax.

The question this module answersHow often should I bring my portfolio back to its target, and how do I do it without paying unnecessary tax?

Start here

Doing nothing is a decision to take more risk

After you build a portfolio (INV-055), the easiest thing is to leave it alone. For the individual funds, that is exactly right. For the mix, it is not: because stocks usually grow faster than bonds, a portfolio left alone slowly turns into a stock portfolio.

Here is how far that goes. Put $10,000 into a 60% stock / 40% bond portfolio at the start of 1928 and never touch it. By the end of 2025 stocks would make up 99.6% of it. Vanguard's researchers found the same pattern in more recent data: a 60/40 portfolio at the end of 1989, never rebalanced, would have been 80% equities by the end of 2021 (Zhang et al., 2022). Nobody chose that risk. It simply arrived.

Rebalancing means periodically selling some of what has grown beyond its target and buying what has fallen below it, so the portfolio keeps the risk you chose. It is the maintenance half of portfolio construction.

The mechanics

Different returns change the weights

Driftthe gap between your target and your actual mix
What it is
The change in a portfolio's asset weights caused by the assets earning different returns. If stocks rise 20% and bonds 2% in a year, a 60/40 portfolio ends the year at about 64/36.
Used for
Deciding when to rebalance: many rules trade only when drift passes a limit.
What it means for you
Drift is not a mistake; it is arithmetic. Checking your actual mix once or twice a year tells you whether your risk is still the one you chose.
New stock weight = w(1 + rs) ÷ [ w(1 + rs) + (1 − w)(1 + rb) ]
e.g. 0.6 × 1.20 ÷ (0.6 × 1.20 + 0.4 × 1.02) = 0.72 ÷ 1.128 = 63.8%

Drift runs in both directions. In a crash it runs the other way: a 60/40 portfolio at the start of 2008 ended that year at 44.2% stocks, because stocks fell 36.6% while 10-year Treasuries rose 20.1%. A portfolio that was "moderate" in January was "conservative" by December — just as stocks became cheap.

💡

Rebalancing is risk control, not a return booster. Vanguard's 2010 study put it this way: "the goal of portfolio rebalancing is to minimize risk (tracking error) relative to a target asset allocation, rather than to maximize returns" (Jaconetti, Kinniry and Zilbering, 2010). Over periods when stocks beat bonds by a wide margin, a portfolio that is allowed to drift toward stocks will usually end with more money. That is not evidence against rebalancing; it is evidence that more stocks meant more return and more risk.

Going furtherIs there a "rebalancing bonus"?

You will read that rebalancing "buys low and sells high" and so adds return. Sometimes it does: between assets with similar long-run returns that take turns leading, rebalancing can add a little. Between stocks and bonds, whose long-run returns differ a lot, the main effect is the opposite — rebalancing repeatedly sells the higher-returning asset. In the 1928–2025 data the annually rebalanced 60/40 portfolio grew 8.34% a year and the drifting one 9.45%. Over 2000–2025, when bonds did relatively well, rebalancing came out ahead: 6.94% versus 6.77% a year. Choose rebalancing for the risk it controls; treat any extra return as luck.

Practitioner depthDrift compounds with time

After n years without trading, the stock weight is w·Gs ÷ [w·Gs + (1 − w)·Gb], where Gs and Gb are the cumulative growth factors of the two assets. Because Gs/Gb itself grows roughly geometrically, drift accelerates: from 1928 through 2025, 10-year Treasuries grew about 77-fold while the S&P 500 grew about 11,600-fold — 150 times as much — which is why the drifting weight approaches 100%. Over any single year the drift is small; over a working lifetime it is decisive.

Choosing a rule

Rebalance on a schedule, or when drift crosses a line

Calendar rebalancing
What it is
Returning to the target on a fixed schedule — every year, every quarter, or every other year — whatever the drift.
Used for
Simplicity: pick a date (your birthday, the first week of January) and do it every time.
What it means for you
Easy to remember and to automate. It sometimes trades when drift is trivial and waits while drift is large.
Threshold (band) rebalancing
What it is
Checking the portfolio regularly but trading only when an asset's weight moves outside a band around its target — for example, when stocks move more than 5 percentage points from 60%.
Used for
Trading only when it matters, which cuts the number of trades and their costs and taxes.
What it means for you
Look at your mix on a schedule, act only if a band is breached. In calm years you may do nothing at all.

Start with the default: a 5-point band checked at each year-end. Then switch the rule to "every year" and to "never," and change the period to 2000 onward. Three patterns stand out. Every disciplined rule produced very similar growth and risk. The band rules traded far less often. And "never" always ended with the most stocks.

What the research says

  • No single best rule. Using US stock and bond data from 1926 through 2009, Vanguard found that "there is no optimal frequency or threshold," that risk-adjusted returns were "not meaningfully different" whether portfolios were rebalanced monthly, quarterly or annually, but that the number of trades and costs rose sharply with frequency. It concluded that annual or semiannual monitoring with rebalancing at 5% thresholds was a reasonable balance for most investors (Jaconetti, Kinniry and Zilbering, 2010).
  • Not too often, not too rarely. A 2022 Vanguard paper that simulated returns and trading costs found optimal methods were "neither too frequent, such as monthly or quarterly calendar-based methods, nor too infrequent, such as rebalancing every two years," and that annual rebalancing was optimal for investors who do not harvest tax losses (Zhang et al., 2022).
  • Bands can beat calendars on cost. A 2024 Vanguard paper on target-date funds found that a threshold policy — trade when drift exceeds 2 percentage points, and trade back to 1.75 points from target rather than all the way — was expected to beat monthly rebalancing by 15–22 basis points a year during accumulation, mainly through lower transaction costs (Zhang et al., 2024).
Going furtherAbsolute bands, relative bands, and small assets

An absolute band of 5 points works well for big slices such as 60% stocks. For a small slice — say a 10% allocation to TIPS — a 5-point band would let it swing from 5% to 15%, a relative change of 50%. Many investors therefore use a relative band for small slices (for example, a quarter of the target: 7.5% to 12.5% for a 10% slice). Whatever you choose, write it in your investment policy statement (INV-028) so the decision is made before markets move.

Practitioner depthTrading back to target or to the band edge

When a band is breached you can trade all the way back to target or only back inside the band. Trading to the edge moves less money each time, so it costs less, but it leaves the portfolio near the edge and may trigger again sooner. In the annual 1928–2025 data, trading to the edge of a 5-point band produced more trades (54 versus 35) but smaller ones, and kept the portfolio a little more stock-heavy on average. Vanguard's 200/175 policy is a middle ground: trigger at 2 points, stop at 1.75. With calendar-year data these differences are small; with monthly data and real trading costs they matter more.

The cheapest trade is the one you do not make

Let new money and withdrawals do the rebalancing

Cash-flow rebalancing
What it is
Steering money that is already moving — new contributions, dividends, interest, withdrawals and required minimum distributions — toward whatever is underweight, or away from whatever is overweight, instead of selling one asset to buy another.
Used for
Rebalancing with no sales, and therefore no realized gains, no tax and little trading cost.
What it means for you
While you are saving, you may rarely need to sell anything. In retirement, deciding which fund each withdrawal comes from does the same job.

With the default numbers — $70,000 in stocks, $30,000 in bonds, a 60/40 target and $10,000 of new money — sending all $10,000 to bonds brings stocks from 70.0% to 63.6%. To reach 60% exactly would still mean selling $4,000 of stocks. In a taxable account with 40% of the value as gain, that sale realizes $1,600 of gain and about $240 of tax at a 15% rate. Inside an IRA it costs nothing. Many investors would simply accept 63.6% — well inside a 5-point band — and let next year's contributions finish the job.

How much new money does it take?

For the portfolio to reach its target without selling, the new money must at least equal the overweight asset's excess over its target after the new money arrives. For a stock-heavy portfolio:

New money needed (all to bonds) = S ÷ s − (S + B)    e.g. 70,000 ÷ 0.6 − 100,000 = $16,667

That is why cash-flow rebalancing works best for savers whose contributions are large relative to the portfolio — Denise and the Riveras — and needs help from sales for large portfolios like the Harpers'.

In retirement, withdrawals are the rebalancing tool

Ruth withdraws money from her IRA every year, and from 2031 she must take required minimum distributions. Each withdrawal can come from whichever fund is overweight. After a strong year for stocks, she takes her spending money from the stock fund; after a crash, from the bond fund, leaving stocks to recover. Her $60,000 cash reserve adds flexibility: in a bad year she can spend from cash and sell nothing at all.

Keep the tax bill small

Where you trade matters as much as what you trade

Selling a fund at a gain in a taxable account creates a taxable capital gain. Selling the same fund inside a 401(k), 403(b) or IRA creates none. Because your allocation is measured across all accounts together (INV-055), you can usually make the trade where it is free.

A tax-aware sequence

  1. Use cash flows first (previous tab): contributions, dividends, withdrawals.
  2. Trade inside tax-advantaged accounts. Exchange stock funds for bond funds, or the reverse, inside the 401(k) or IRA. No tax, no reporting.
  3. In taxable accounts, sell losses before gains. Selling shares worth less than you paid realizes a loss that offsets gains and up to $3,000 a year of ordinary income (INV-037). This is tax-loss harvesting.
  4. If you must sell gains, choose the lots. With specific identification you can sell the shares with the highest cost basis, and prefer shares held more than one year, which qualify for long-term rates.
  5. Consider giving instead of selling. Donating appreciated shares held more than a year to charity, or to a donor-advised fund, removes the overweight without realizing the gain (INV-039).

The 2026 long-term capital gains brackets

Long-term gains (on investments held more than one year) are taxed at 0%, 15% or 20%, depending on taxable income. The 0% rate is worth knowing about: a household in a low-income year can sometimes rebalance a taxable account for no federal tax at all.

Wash-sale rulethe trap in tax-loss harvesting
What it is
You cannot deduct a loss if, within 30 days before or after the sale, you buy substantially identical stock or securities — including a purchase in your IRA or Roth IRA (IRS Publication 550).
Used for
Preventing people from claiming a loss while keeping the same position.
What it means for you
When you harvest a loss while rebalancing, buy a similar but not identical fund, and check that no automatic purchase of the same fund lands in any of your accounts within the 61-day window.
Going furtherWhy the Harpers rebalance in their 401(k)s

The Harpers hold all their bonds in pre-tax 401(k)s and their technology stocks in the taxable account (INV-055). When stocks surge, the portfolio becomes stock-heavy, and it is tempting to sell the winners in the taxable account. The cheaper trade is inside the 401(k)s: exchange some of the US stock index fund for the bond fund there. The overall allocation returns to 60/40 and the taxable account's gains stay unrealized. The taxable account still has its own project — diversifying away from technology — which they manage with loss harvesting and charitable gifts over several years.

A routine you can keep

A 20-minute rebalancing checklist

When someone else rebalances for you

  • Target-date and balanced funds rebalance internally. If one fund is your whole portfolio in an account, you do not need to rebalance it — but do not surround it with other funds, or the combined mix drifts again.
  • 401(k) automatic rebalancing. Many plans offer a setting that resets your chosen fund mix quarterly or annually. It works inside that plan only; it cannot see your IRA or brokerage account.
  • Robo-advisors and managed accounts monitor drift automatically, often with bands, and may combine rebalancing with tax-loss harvesting. Compare the fee to the value of your time (INV-104).

The emotional part

The hardest rebalancing trades are the most useful ones: buying stocks after a crash. For a 60/40 portfolio with a 5% threshold, Vanguard counted only seven occasions since 1926 when a rebalancing opportunity into equities occurred — 1930, 1931, 1937, 1974, 2000, 2002 and 2008, each after very poor trailing returns — and noted that during the 2008 crisis "poor investment performance coupled with considerable uncertainty about the future made it seem counterintuitive" to rebalance into stocks (Jaconetti, Kinniry and Zilbering, 2010). A written rule made in calm times is what makes those trades possible.

⚠️

Rebalancing is not market timing. A rebalancing rule responds only to your own portfolio's drift, by a fixed amount, at fixed intervals. Selling because you expect a fall, or buying because a fund "has momentum," is a forecast; INV-052 covers why those rarely pay.

History, year by year

Three episodes that show what rebalancing does

Case study 1The 1990s boom and the 2000–2002 bust
What happened
From 1990 through 1999 the S&P 500 returned more than 15% in six of ten years. A $100,000 60/40 portfolio left alone grew to $396,853 by the end of 1999, but with 79.5% in stocks. Over 2000–2002 the S&P 500 fell 9.0%, 11.9% and 22.0% while Treasuries gained. The drifting portfolio fell to $312,869 (−21.2%). The annually rebalanced one went from $368,688 to $329,728 (−10.6%).
What it teaches
By 1999 the drifting investor held a portfolio they never chose, at the worst possible moment. Rebalancing gave up about $28,000 of boom-time gains and got it all back, and more, in the bust.
Source
Computed from Damodaran, A. (2026), Historical returns on stocks, bonds and bills: 1928–2025, NYU Stern.
Case study 22008–2013: buying stocks when it felt worst
What happened
A $100,000 60/40 portfolio at the start of 2008 ended the year at $86,110 with only 44.2% in stocks. Rebalancing meant buying about $13,600 of stocks in January 2009. By the end of 2013 the rebalanced portfolio was worth $148,121; the one left alone, $136,379.
What it teaches
The rebalancing trade was the one nobody wanted to make. A rule made it automatic, and it added about $11,700 over five years.
Source
Computed from Damodaran, NYU Stern (2008: S&P 500 −36.55%, 10-year Treasury +20.10%). Vanguard lists 2008 among its seven major rebalancing opportunities since 1926 (Jaconetti et al., 2010).
Case study 32022: when there was nothing to rebalance
What happened
In 2022 the S&P 500 returned −18.0% and 10-year Treasuries −17.8%. A 60/40 portfolio lost about 18% and ended the year at 59.9% stocks.
What it teaches
Rebalancing helps only when assets diverge. When stocks and bonds fall together, the mix barely moves and there is no trade to make. Rebalancing controls drift; it cannot protect against a year when everything falls (INV-004).
Source
Computed from Damodaran, NYU Stern.

Decisions

Three rebalancing choices

Scenario 1 · The Harpers after a strong year

After a strong year for stocks the Harpers' $1.1 million portfolio is at 66% stocks against a 60% target, outside their 5-point band. Moving back means shifting about $66,000 from stocks to bonds. Where should they trade?

Sell in taxableWith large embedded gains, this could realize tens of thousands of dollars of gain. If 60% of the value were gain, $39,600 of gain at 15% is about $5,940 of federal tax for a trade they could make for free.
Exchange in the 401(k)sRight. Same allocation result, no tax. They continue diversifying the taxable account separately and patiently.
Do nothingFive or six years from retirement, drifting toward more stock risk is the opposite of their glide path (INV-058). Their band exists for exactly this moment.
The principle: rebalance the portfolio as one, but place the trade in the account where it is cheapest.
Scenario 2 · Ruth after a crash

Stocks have fallen 30% this year. Ruth's IRA has dropped from 45% to about 36% stocks, and she needs $30,000 for next year's spending. How should she take it?

Sell stocksThat locks in the loss and pushes her further from her plan. It is a market-timing decision, not a rebalancing one.
Spend from bonds or cash; rebalance inside the IRARight. Her bonds and cash reserve exist to fund spending while stocks recover. Trades inside the IRA carry no tax.
The principle: in retirement, withdrawals are rebalancing trades; take them from what is overweight.
Scenario 3 · The Riveras' contributions

The Riveras contribute about $1,500 a month to their 401(k)s. Their 80/20 portfolio has drifted to 84% stocks. What is the simplest fix?

Sell and buyInside a 401(k) this is tax-free and perfectly fine. It is just more work than necessary for a 4-point drift inside a 5-point band.
Redirect contributionsRight, and it is free. Many plans also offer an automatic rebalancing setting that would do this for them.
Target-date fundA reasonable choice that ends the rebalancing question inside the 401(k)s — but their Roth IRAs would still need their own mix, and the combined portfolio would need checking.
The principle: for savers, the paycheck is the rebalancing tool.

Apply it

A rebalancing rule for each household

Nothing to do. A single target-date fund rebalances itself. If she uses three funds instead, pointing each paycheck at the underweight fund is enough for years.
Contributions first, annual check. A January check across both 401(k)s (and any Roth IRAs they open); redirect contributions if drift exceeds 5 points; turn on the plans' automatic rebalancing if offered.
All the trading happens in the 401(k). Every bond she owns is there, so a single exchange in the 401(k) restores 60/40 with no tax; the Roth IRA and taxable account never need to sell. Her $14,720 a year of contributions is only about 4% of the portfolio, too little to fix a big drift alone. A yearly check with a 5-point band, and her $30,000 reserve kept out of the calculation.
Trade inside the 401(k)s. Twice-yearly checks with a 5-point band; exchanges happen in the pre-tax accounts, while the taxable account is diversified separately with loss harvesting.
Withdrawals do the work. Each month's or year's withdrawal comes from the overweight fund; a bad year is funded from cash and bonds. From 2031, RMDs follow the same rule.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A 60/40 portfolio has a year in which stocks return +20% and bonds +2%. What is the stock share at year-end, in percent?

Stocks 0.6 × 1.20 = 0.720; bonds 0.4 × 1.02 = 0.408; total 1.128. Stock share 0.720 ÷ 1.128 = 63.8%.
Exercise 2

A portfolio holds $70,000 of stocks and $30,000 of bonds; the target is 60% stocks. How much new money, all sent to bonds, would bring it back to target without selling?

The $70,000 of stocks must be 60% of the new total: 70,000 ÷ 0.6 = $116,667. The portfolio is $100,000 now, so it needs $16,667 of new money in bonds.
Exercise 3

You sell $50,000 of a stock fund in a taxable account. 60% of the value is long-term gain, and your rate on long-term gains is 15%. What is the federal tax, in dollars?

Gain 50,000 × 0.6 = $30,000; tax 30,000 × 0.15 = $4,500. The same exchange inside a 401(k) would cost $0.
Exercise 4

The Harpers' $1,100,000 portfolio is at 66% stocks with a 60% target. How many dollars must move from stocks to bonds?

Stocks are 0.66 × 1,100,000 = $726,000; the target is 0.60 × 1,100,000 = $660,000. Move $66,000.
