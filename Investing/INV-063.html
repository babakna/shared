Rent vs Buy

Buying a home is the largest financial decision most households make, and the one most often made on slogans. This module adds up everything owning really costs, sets it against renting and investing the difference, and shows why the answer depends on price-to-rent, interest rates and how long you stay.

The question this module answersIs it cheaper for me to own or to rent the home I want, and over what time frame?

Start here

"Renting is throwing money away" — is it?

You have probably heard it from a parent or a friend: rent disappears every month, while a mortgage payment builds something you own. There is truth in it. Owning a home you stay in for decades has been a good deal for millions of American families, and a paid-off house is a large part of many retirees' wealth.

But the slogan compares the wrong things. A renter's check buys a place to live and nothing else. An owner's check is split: part of it, the principal, repays the loan and becomes the owner's savings. The rest — mortgage interest, property tax, insurance, maintenance, and the return the down payment could have earned elsewhere — is gone just as surely as rent. Owners also pay thousands of dollars to buy and to sell. The fair comparison is between the renter's rent and the owner's unrecoverable costs, with the renter investing whatever they save.

Add it all up

Seven costs of owning, not one

When people say a home "costs $2,000 a month," they usually mean the mortgage payment. That is only the start. Here is each cost, defined before we use it.

Principal and interestthe mortgage payment
What it is
A mortgage payment has two parts. Interest is the lender's charge for the money, computed on the balance still owed. Principal is the part that reduces the balance. Early in a 30-year loan, most of each payment is interest (INV-064 shows the schedule).
Used for
Interest is a cost of borrowing; principal is a transfer from your checking account into your home equity.
What it means for you
At 7% on a $292,500 loan, the first payment of $1,946 is $1,706 of interest and only $240 of principal.
Taxes, insurance, maintenance, duesthe costs that never end
What it is
Property tax is levied by local governments on assessed value. Homeowners insurance covers the building, contents and liability. Maintenance covers everything that wears out, from paint to roofs. Condominiums and many newer neighborhoods add homeowners association (HOA) dues.
Used for
Keeping the house legal, insured and in good repair. A landlord pays all of these for a renter and builds them into the rent.
What it means for you
They continue after the mortgage is paid off. Many planners assume 1% to 2% of the home's value a year for maintenance, a rule of thumb rather than a measured average; older homes and harsh climates need more. Use actual tax bills and insurance quotes for a specific house.
Transaction costs and opportunity costthe two people forget
What it is
Closing costs are paid when you buy (lender fees, title insurance, appraisal, recording). Selling costs include agent commissions, which are negotiable, transfer taxes in some states, and concessions to the buyer. The opportunity cost of the down payment is the return that money would have earned if it had been invested instead.
Used for
Transaction costs are paid once each way, so they matter most for short stays. Opportunity cost matters most when the down payment is large and expected investment returns are high.
What it means for you
On a $325,000 home, 3% to buy and 6% to sell three years later is about $31,000 — money a renter never spends.
Going furtherDoes the mortgage interest deduction change the math?

Mortgage interest and property taxes are deductible only if you itemize deductions instead of taking the standard deduction. For tax year 2026, the standard deduction is $16,100 for single filers, $24,150 for heads of household and $32,200 for married couples filing jointly (IRS, Rev. Proc. 2025-32). Itemizing helps only by the amount your deductions exceed that figure.

Take a head of household who buys the illustrative home above. First-year interest is about $20,400 and property tax about $4,900. Add state and local income tax and charitable gifts, and suppose the total reaches $29,000 — $4,850 more than the $24,150 standard deduction. In a 12% bracket that saves about $580 a year; in a 22% bracket, about $1,070, or $89 a month. Real, but far smaller than the $1,000-a-month gap in Figure 63.2. For many married couples the deduction is worth nothing at all, because $32,200 is hard to exceed.

Monica, also a head of household, shows the other end. On her $95,000 balance at 3.4%, interest is about $3,230 a year, and property tax at the same illustrative 1.5% of her home's $310,000 value is about $4,650. Even with state and local income tax added she stays far below $24,150, so she takes the standard deduction and her mortgage gives her no tax benefit at all. That matters in INV-064, when she weighs paying it off early.

Practitioner depthThe 2026 limits on housing deductions

Mortgage interest: deductible on up to $750,000 of acquisition debt ($375,000 if married filing separately) for loans taken out after December 15, 2017; $1 million for older loans (IRS Publication 936). Home-equity interest is deductible only to the extent the money was used to buy, build or substantially improve the home. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21) made the $750,000 limit permanent.

State and local taxes (SALT): for 2026 the deduction for state and local income, sales and property taxes is capped at $40,400 ($20,200 married filing separately), reduced for modified adjusted gross income above $505,000 ($252,500) but not below $10,000 ($5,000) (IRS correction notice to the 2026 Form 1040-ES). The cap is scheduled to rise 1% a year through 2029 and return to $10,000 in 2030 (Public Law 119-21, section 70120).

Mortgage insurance premiums are deductible again. The deduction had expired (Publication 936 for 2025 says it can no longer be claimed), but Public Law 119-21, section 70108, permanently restored the treatment of these premiums as deductible mortgage interest for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025. It helps only if you itemize, and it shrinks by 10% for each $1,000 of adjusted gross income above $100,000 (each $500 above $50,000 if married filing separately), so it is gone entirely above about $109,000 (26 U.S.C. 163(h)(3)(E)). Selling: gain on a main home is excluded up to $250,000 ($500,000 married filing jointly) if you owned and lived in it for two of the last five years (IRS Publication 523). INV-068 covers the tax rules for rentals.

A fast, fair shortcut

Compare rent with what an owner never gets back

You can compare renting and owning in two ways. The long way, in the next tab, simulates both households' wealth month by month. The short way asks one question: what does it cost each year to live in this home, counting only money that does not come back?

Unrecoverable cost of owning
What it is
Property tax + maintenance + insurance + mortgage interest + the return the down payment gives up, minus the price growth you expect. Principal is excluded because it is your own money moving into home equity.
Used for
A like-for-like comparison with rent: if a comparable home rents for less than this, renting is the cheaper way to live there.
What it means for you
It turns a confusing decision into one number per year. A simplified version is sometimes called the "5% rule": roughly 1% for property tax, 1% for maintenance and 3% for the cost of capital, times the price.
Unrecoverable cost ≈ P×(tax% + maintenance%) + insurance + loan×rate + down payment×expected return − P×expected growth

With the default inputs — an illustrative $325,000 home in Columbus, Ohio, where Monica lives, 10% down, a 7% mortgage, a 6% expected return elsewhere and 3% expected price growth — the unrecoverable cost is about $22,600 a year, or $1,883 a month: 6.95% of the price. Comparable rent is $1,850. On this snapshot, owning and renting cost about the same to live in. The tie breaks against buying once transaction costs are added, unless the buyer stays a long time.

Going furtherWhy interest rates dominate the answer

Interest is usually the largest unrecoverable cost. At 7%, interest on a $292,500 loan is about $20,500 a year; at 4% it would be about $11,700. That $8,800 difference is almost five months of the $1,850 rent. The same house can be a sensible purchase at one rate and an expensive one at another, which is why rent-versus-buy conclusions from 2021, when 30-year rates averaged 2.96%, do not carry over to late September 2026, when the weekly average was 7.03% (Freddie Mac, September 24, 2026).

Practitioner depthWhat the shortcut leaves out

It is a first-year snapshot. It ignores buying and selling costs, private mortgage insurance, the fact that rent usually rises while a fixed mortgage payment does not, and taxes on investment returns. The expected return and price growth should be on the same basis: if you use nominal returns (before inflation) for one, use nominal for both. Treat the result as a first screen, then run the full simulation.

The full simulation

Two households, one home, thirty years

This calculator follows two otherwise identical households. One buys the home. The other rents a comparable home and invests the down payment and closing costs on day one. Each month, whichever household has lower housing costs invests the difference at the same return. At the end of each year it compares the owner's wealth — home value minus loan balance minus the cost of selling, plus investments — with the renter's investments.

With the defaults, owning costs about $2,900 a month in year 1 against about $1,870 for renting, and the renter invests the $1,000 difference every month. After 10 years the renter has about $220,000 and the owner about $160,000; buying does not pull ahead within 30 years. That is not a law of nature. It is the result of a 7% mortgage rate, a 6% investment return and 3% price growth. Change any one and the answer moves, as Figure 63.3 shows.

Going furtherThe discipline assumption

The renter wins in many scenarios only if they actually invest the difference every month for decades. Many people do not. A mortgage is a forced savings plan: the principal goes into the house whether or not you feel like saving. If you know you would spend the difference, owning's automatic saving is worth something the calculator cannot see. If you are a disciplined saver who automates investing (INV-018), renting keeps that advantage without the concentration in one house.

Practitioner depthHow the model works, and what it ignores

Monthly steps. Owner cost = principal and interest (fixed) + value × tax% ÷ 12 + insurance ÷ 12 + value × maintenance% ÷ 12 + HOA + PMI while the balance exceeds 80% of the price. Renter cost = rent + renters insurance ÷ 12. Rent, insurance and HOA dues rise once a year at the inflation input; the home's value compounds monthly at the growth input; investments compound monthly at the return input. It ignores income taxes (the deduction in the previous tab, capital-gains tax on the renter's portfolio, the home sale exclusion), refinancing, and the non-financial value of stability or flexibility. One ratio to start with

How many years of rent does the house cost?

Price-to-rent ratio
What it is
The price of a home divided by one year's rent for a comparable home. The Columbus example: $325,000 ÷ ($1,850 × 12) = 14.6.
Used for
Comparing how expensive owning is relative to renting across cities and across time. Its inverse is the gross rental yield: 1 ÷ 14.6 = 6.8% of the price earned in rent each year, before any costs.
What it means for you
The higher the ratio, the more house price you pay for each dollar of rent you avoid, and the more likely renting and investing is the cheaper path. Analysts often describe ratios below about 15 as favoring buying and above about 20 as favoring renting; these are loose conventions, not rules, and they shift with interest rates.
Price-to-rent = Price ÷ (monthly rent × 12)     Gross rental yield = 1 ÷ price-to-rent

Figure 63.4 is a national index, so it shows the direction of the ratio, not its level in any city. From 2000 to 2025, national home prices rose about 3.1-fold while rents rose about 2.4-fold. When prices outrun rents for years, buyers are paying more for the same housing service, which has sometimes preceded falling prices (2006–2012) and sometimes simply years of slow price growth while rents catch up.

Going furtherGetting a fair ratio for one house

Compare like with like: the same neighborhood, size, age and condition. Rental listings skew toward apartments and sale listings toward houses, so a naive city-wide ratio can mislead. Use two or three truly comparable rentals, and note that asking rents on listings can exceed what tenants actually pay after concessions.

Practitioner depthPrice-to-rent and interest rates

A price-to-rent ratio is the housing version of a price-to-earnings ratio (INV-006), and like a P/E it should be lower when interest rates are higher. At a 3% mortgage rate, a gross rental yield of 5% (ratio 20) covers the interest easily; at 7% it does not. This is why the ratio conventions in the definition above are loose: the ratio that favors buying depends on the rate you borrow at and the return you could earn instead.

⚠️

This is not a fair race, in either direction. The stock figures include dividends; the home figures leave out the home's "dividend," the imputed rent of living there, worth several percent of the price each year. But they also leave out property tax, insurance and maintenance, which eat most or all of that dividend. And most owners use a mortgage, which magnifies price changes on their equity (INV-066). The honest summary: a home is mainly a place to live that has, over long periods, roughly held its real value and forced its owners to save.

Going furtherA house is a concentrated, illiquid, leveraged asset

A total-market index fund owns thousands of companies; a house is one building in one neighborhood, tied to one local job market. It cannot be sold in pieces, costs several percent to sell, and is usually bought with a loan several times the owner's equity. None of this makes owning bad. It means your home should be judged mainly as housing, and your retirement plan should not depend on its price rising faster than inflation.

Real history and real rules

Three cases that changed how people think about owning

Case study 1The 2006–2012 housing bust
What happened
The S&P Cotality Case-Shiller U.S. National Home Price Index peaked at 184.6 in July 2006 and fell to 134.0 in February 2012, a decline of 27%. In Phoenix, home of the Riveras, the city index fell from 227.4 in June 2006 to 100.2 in September 2011 — 56%. On an annual-average basis the national index did not pass its 2006 level again until 2017.
What it teaches
Home prices can fall for years, nationally and much more locally. A household that bought in 2006 and had to sell in 2010 could owe more than the house was worth. Owners who could keep paying and stay put recovered; those forced to move did not. The ability to stay is part of the rent-versus-buy decision.
Source
S&P Dow Jones Indices, via FRED, series CSUSHPINSA and PHXRNSA (2026).
Case study 22021–2026: the same house at two interest rates
What happened
Freddie Mac's 30-year fixed rate averaged 2.96% in 2021. By September 24, 2026 it was 7.03%, up from 6.30% a year earlier. National home prices rose about 26% from 2021 to 2025 (annual averages), so the payment on a newly purchased home rose far faster than the price.
What it teaches
On a $300,000 loan, principal and interest are about $1,258 a month at 2.96% and $2,002 at 7.03%. Rent-versus-buy answers are time-stamped; any rule of thumb from the low-rate years needs rechecking at today's rates.
Source
Freddie Mac, Primary Mortgage Market Survey (release of September 24, 2026; annual averages via FRED series MORTGAGE30US).
Case study 3The home sale exclusion and a surviving spouse
The rule
A seller who owned and lived in a home for at least two of the last five years can exclude up to $250,000 of gain, or $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly. A surviving spouse who has not remarried can still use the $500,000 limit if the home is sold within two years of the spouse's death and the other conditions are met.
Applied (illustration)
Ruth was widowed in 2023. Had she sold the Pittsburgh house within two years of Walter's death, up to $500,000 of gain could have been excluded; selling now, her limit is $250,000. If her gain exceeds that — the house is worth about $380,000, so, before selling costs, that would take a cost basis under $130,000 — the rest is a taxable capital gain. A step-up in basis on Walter's share at his death (INV-090) may reduce the gain.
Source
IRS Publication 523 (2025), Selling your home.

Decisions

Three households, three housing questions

Scenario 1 · Monica

Monica's Columbus house is worth about $310,000, with $95,000 left on a 3.4% mortgage and 12 years to go. A coworker says she should sell, rent a similar house for about $1,850 a month, and invest the $215,000 of equity to "catch up" for retirement and for Eli. Eli, who has special needs, has lived in the house most of his life. What should she weigh?

Sell and rentThe equity is counted already: the 6% it could earn elsewhere is in her $16,380 unrecoverable cost, and that is still about $5,820 a year, or $485 a month, less than renting. Selling would also cost about $18,600 and give up a 3.4% loan in a market where new 30-year loans average about 7%. Moving could unsettle Eli, for whom routine matters.
Stay, revisit at retirementRight. The numbers and Eli's needs point the same way. At 67 the question changes: whether the house suits Eli as an adult, whether he might live in it with support, or whether downsizing would fund his trust. That is an estate and care decision (INV-087, INV-102) as much as a housing one.
The principle: an owner deciding whether to sell runs the same comparison as a renter deciding whether to buy, and counts the value of stability too.
Scenario 2 · Jordan

Jordan, 24, earns $62,000 in Denver and has $3,000 of savings and $28,000 of student loans at 6.8%. A coworker bought a condo with 5% down and says Jordan should too. In the calculator, a hypothetical $400,000 condo with $350 of HOA dues and 5% down costs about $4,000 a month against $1,800 of rent. What should she do?

Stretch and buyA $4,000 monthly cost is about 78% of her gross pay, far beyond what lenders or common sense allow. With $3,000 saved she also cannot cover closing costs or the first repair.
Rent and build the baseRight for now. Every step in her base (INV-019, INV-020) earns a sure return. Renting at 24 is a normal, sensible stage, not a failure.
The principle: a home is a purchase you should be able to afford with room to spare, not a way to start investing.
Scenario 3 · Ruth

Ruth's paid-off Pittsburgh house is worth about $380,000. Her daughter Anna suggests selling and renting an apartment near her. Ruth worries that renting at 68 is "going backward." What should she weigh?

Never sellA paid-off house is not free: tax, insurance and maintenance continue, and $380,000 of equity earns no income. It may still be the right place for her, but by choice, not by rule.
Weigh it fullyRight. Living near family has real value as she ages. Selling costs, the $250,000 exclusion, and her comfort matter. INV-075 on how much she needs and INV-083 on long-term care connect to this decision.
The principle: in retirement, a home is both housing and a large pool of capital; decide on both counts.

Six things people say about owning and renting

Apply it

What rent versus buy means for each household

Rent without guilt. At $62,000 of income and $3,000 of savings, any purchase would be a stretch that crowds out her emergency fund, 401(k) match and 6.8% loans. Renting is the right tool at her stage.
Their low rate changes the question. Owning at 3.1% is cheap housing: interest on their $310,000 balance is about $9,500 over the next year. Moving would mean a new loan at about 7%. The rent-versus-buy question for them is really "stay or move," and staying is favored unless their needs change.
Stay; the cheap loan and the familiar home both count. Her unrecoverable cost is about $16,380 a year against $22,200 of comparable rent, and her price-to-rent ratio is about 14.0. The real housing decision comes at retirement: where Eli will live as an adult, and whether the house, or the money from selling it, should one day pass to his special needs trust.
Plan the retirement house now. Downsizing, staying or relocating when they retire at 62–63 decides how much of their $560,000 of equity stays tied up. Their $500,000 joint home sale exclusion covers a large gain.
Run the numbers on selling. Her house's unrecoverable costs — tax, insurance, upkeep and the income $380,000 could produce — may approach rent near Anna. Her exclusion is now $250,000 as a single filer.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A home costs $480,000. A comparable home rents for $2,000 a month. What is the price-to-rent ratio?

Annual rent = $2,000 × 12 = $24,000. Ratio = $480,000 ÷ $24,000 = 20, a gross rental yield of 5%.
Exercise 2

A $400,000 home with 20% down and a 6% mortgage. Property tax 1.2%, maintenance 1%, insurance $1,400 a year, expected return on the down payment 5%, expected price growth 3%. What is the annual unrecoverable cost, in dollars?

Tax $4,800 + maintenance $4,000 + insurance $1,400 + interest ($320,000 × 6% = $19,200) + return given up ($80,000 × 5% = $4,000) = $33,400. Subtract expected growth ($400,000 × 3% = $12,000): $21,400 a year, or $1,783 a month to compare with rent.
Exercise 3

You buy a $325,000 home paying 3% closing costs and sell it three years later after 3% a year price growth, paying 6% to sell. Total transaction costs, in dollars?

Buying: $325,000 × 3% = $9,750. Value after 3 years: $325,000 × 1.033 = $355,136. Selling: 6% × $355,136 = $21,308. Total $31,058, about 9.6% of the purchase price.
Exercise 4

A married couple filing jointly for 2026 has $22,000 of mortgage interest, $9,000 of property tax, $2,800 of state income tax and $2,000 of charitable gifts. By how many dollars do their itemized deductions exceed the 2026 standard deduction?

Itemized: $22,000 + $9,000 + $2,800 + $2,000 = $35,800 (the $11,800 of state and local taxes is under the $40,400 SALT cap). Standard deduction for 2026, married filing jointly: $32,200. Excess: $3,600. At a 22% bracket, owning saves them about $792 of tax, not 22% of $22,000.
