Dividend and Income Investing

"Live off the income and never touch the principal" is one of the most comforting ideas in personal finance. This module explains what dividends really are, why a dividend is not extra return, when dividend strategies help and hurt, how dividends are taxed for 2026, and how a retiree can draw a steady income from the whole portfolio instead.

The question this module answersShould I build my portfolio around the income it pays, or around its total return?

Start here

The appeal of a paycheck from your investments

A dividend feels like rent from a property you own: cash arrives, and the property is still there. For someone like Ruth, who has stopped working, a check that shows up every quarter without selling anything is deeply reassuring.

That reassurance is real, and dividends are real cash paid out of real profits. But there is a catch that many investors never hear about: when a company pays a dividend, the company itself is worth that much less. The cash moved from one of your pockets to the other. A dividend is part of your return, not an addition to it. This module shows why, what that means for choosing investments, and why the idea of "never touching principal" can quietly cost retirees income, diversification and taxes.

💡

Why this matters now. At a yield near 1%, a broad US stock fund pays about $10,600 a year on $1 million (the iShares Core S&P 500 ETF's 12-month trailing yield was 1.06% on August 31, 2026). Living only on that would require either a very large portfolio or a portfolio tilted heavily toward the relatively few companies that pay high dividends.

Mechanics

Cash out of the company, into your account

Dividend
What it is
A payment of a company's earnings to its shareholders, usually in cash and usually quarterly in the US. The IRS describes dividends as "distributions of earnings and profits" a corporation pays to its shareholders.
Used for
Returning profits the company does not need to reinvest in its business.
What it means for you
You receive cash (or more shares, if you reinvest), and the company holds that much less cash. Your total wealth is unchanged at the moment of payment.
Ex-dividend date
What it is
The first trading day on which a buyer of the stock no longer receives the upcoming dividend. On that morning the share price typically opens lower by roughly the amount of the dividend.
Used for
Determining who is entitled to the dividend.
What it means for you
Buying a stock just before the ex-dividend date to "capture" the dividend does not create free money: you get the dividend and the price falls by about as much, and in a taxable account you now owe tax on it.
Total return = dividend yield + change in price      Dividend yield = dividends per share over the past year ÷ share price
Payout ratio
What it is
Dividends as a share of earnings. A company earning $4 a share and paying $2 has a 50% payout ratio.
Used for
Judging how much room a company has to keep paying, or raising, its dividend if earnings fall.
What it means for you
A payout ratio near or above 100% is a warning: the company is paying out more than it earns, often a sign of a coming cut (INV-006).

Miller and Modigliani, 1961

Why a dividend is not extra return

In 1961 Merton Miller and Franco Modigliani published "Dividend Policy, Growth, and the Valuation of Shares." Their argument can be told in plain words. What makes a company valuable is the profit its business earns and will earn. Whether it hands some of that profit to you as a dividend or keeps it and reinvests it changes the form of your return, not its size. If you want cash and the company does not pay a dividend, you can sell a few shares; if the company pays a dividend and you do not want cash, you can reinvest it.

Dividend irrelevance theorem
What it is
The proposition that, in a world without taxes and trading costs, a company's dividend policy does not affect its value or its shareholders' wealth; only its investment decisions do.
Used for
Separating the question "is this a good business at a good price?" from "does it pay a dividend?"
What it means for you
A higher yield is not a higher return. Choose investments on total return, diversification and cost; then decide how to get the cash you need.
Homemade dividend
What it is
Creating your own cash flow by selling a small part of your holdings, instead of relying on the dividends companies choose to pay.
Used for
Getting exactly the income you need from a diversified portfolio whatever its yield.
What it means for you
Selling 2% of a portfolio and receiving a 2% dividend leave you in the same position before taxes and costs. In a taxable account, selling is often taxed less.
Going furtherWhere the real world departs from the theorem

Miller and Modigliani's result assumes no taxes, no trading costs and no information in the dividend itself. Each assumption bends in practice. Taxes: in taxable accounts, dividends are taxed in full every year, while selling shares taxes only the gain portion, so dividends are usually less tax-efficient. Costs: selling shares once cost commissions; since 2019 most large US brokerages have charged none for online stock and ETF trades (INV-005), which makes homemade dividends nearly free. Signals: managers are reluctant to cut dividends, so a dividend increase can signal confidence and a cut can signal trouble. Discipline: some investors argue dividends stop managers wasting cash. None of these makes a high yield a higher expected return.

Practitioner depthDividends and share buybacks

Companies can also return cash by buying back their own shares. Economically a buyback is a dividend paid only to the shareholders who choose to sell, and remaining shareholders own a larger slice. In a taxable account, the buyback route lets each investor decide whether to realize gains. So comparing today's dividend yield near 1% with the much higher yields of the 1950s can understate how much cash companies return, because some of it now arrives through buybacks.

Yield trap
What it is
A stock whose high yield reflects a falling price, because the market expects the dividend to be cut or the business to shrink.
Used for
Warning against buying on yield alone.
What it means for you
A 9% yield is usually a forecast, by the market, that the 9% will not last. Check the payout ratio and the balance sheet first.

Strategies

Dividend growth funds, high-yield funds, and what they leave out

Dividend growth investing
What it is
Focusing on companies with a record of raising their dividends year after year, rather than on those with the highest current yield.
Used for
Seeking income that rises over time, from companies profitable enough to keep increasing payouts; it tends to tilt toward quality (INV-046).
What it means for you
A dividend growth fund is a reasonable core stock holding, but it is a subset of the market with its own style tilt, not a safer version of the market.

The high-yield funds tripled the S&P 500's yield by holding a small fraction of its companies. Those companies cluster in the industries that pay large dividends, and they omit most of the large, fast-growing companies that pay little or nothing. For a retiree, that concentration is the hidden cost of an income strategy: a portfolio chosen for yield is less diversified than the market (INV-004).

Dividends are not guaranteed

Unlike bond interest, a dividend is a decision of the company's board and can be cut at any time. In bad recessions many companies cut at once.

Taxes, 2026

Qualified, ordinary, and inside an IRA

Where a dividend is received matters as much as how much it is. In a taxable brokerage account, dividends are taxed in the year they are paid, even if you reinvest them. Inside a traditional IRA or 401(k), they are not taxed when paid; withdrawals are taxed later as ordinary income whatever the source. Inside a Roth IRA, qualified withdrawals are tax-free.

Qualified dividend
What it is
A dividend that qualifies for the lower long-term capital gains rates. Among the conditions, you must have held common stock for more than 60 days during the 121-day period that begins 60 days before the ex-dividend date (IRS Publication 550). The payer identifies qualified amounts on Form 1099-DIV; other ("ordinary") dividends are taxed as ordinary income.
Used for
Lowering the tax on dividends in taxable accounts.
What it means for you
Bond interest is not a dividend at all and is taxed at ordinary rates, and some distributions labeled "dividends" do not qualify. That is one reason to hold income-heavy investments in tax-deferred accounts (INV-036).

Going furtherWhy the 0% bracket matters for some retirees

For 2026, a married couple whose taxable income stays below $98,900 pays no federal tax on qualified dividends and long-term gains. Retirees who live partly on taxable-account investments, before large IRA withdrawals or Social Security push income up, can sometimes realize gains at 0% (tax-gain harvesting, INV-037). Ruth files as single, where the threshold is $49,450; her IRA withdrawals count toward taxable income, and part of her Social Security may too, and her holdings are mostly inside the IRA anyway.

For retirees

A paycheck you set, not one the market sets

The "live on the income" rule answers a real need: how much can I spend without running out? But it answers it indirectly. Your spending ends up being whatever the companies you own decide to pay, and it tempts you to buy higher-yielding, less diversified holdings to get enough income. The alternative, often called the total-return approach, picks a sustainable withdrawal amount first (INV-076), holds a diversified portfolio for the best risk-adjusted total return, and sells shares or bonds to make up the difference between the dividends received and the amount needed.

Total-return investing
What it is
Building a portfolio for its combined return (dividends, interest and price changes) and funding spending from any of them, rather than from income alone.
Used for
Retirement withdrawals that are steady, planned and independent of dividend policy.
What it means for you
"Touching principal" is not a failure; it is the plan. The question is whether the rate you withdraw is sustainable, not where each dollar came from.

With the calculator's defaults — Ruth's $780,000 IRA, a 4% first-year need of $31,200 rising 2.5% a year, a 6% total return, and a 3.3% yield for the income-only portfolio — dividends pay $25,740 in year one, short of the need in every one of the 25 years, a total shortfall of about $163,000. The income-only portfolio ends larger (about $1.52 million versus $1.04 million) only because Ruth would have spent less. The total-return approach delivers the income she planned, and still leaves more than she started with in this illustration. Neither result is guaranteed: returns vary, and sequence risk matters (INV-077).

Going furtherWhy the "income" mindset persists

Behavioral economists call it mental accounting: we treat money differently depending on the label we give it. "Income" feels spendable and "principal" feels untouchable, although a dollar of dividend and a dollar of share sale are the same dollar. The rule can be useful as a brake on overspending. Its costs are concentration in high-yield stocks, a paycheck that shrinks when companies cut, and, in taxable accounts, higher taxes. A written withdrawal plan provides the same brake without those costs (INV-028, INV-076).

Real history

Three episodes

Case study 1The Great Depression, 1930–1933
What happened
Twelve-month dividends on the S&P Composite fell 55% from December 1930 to December 1933. The dividend yield in December 1931 was 9.72%, the highest in the period, because prices had collapsed even faster.
What it teaches
A retiree living only on dividends in 1930 would have seen that income more than halved in three years. The high yield of 1931 was not a bargain for income seekers; it was a symptom.
Source
Shiller, R. J. Stock market data used in "Irrational Exuberance" (ie_data.xls).
Case study 2The 2008 financial crisis
What happened
Trailing S&P Composite dividends fell 24% from their September 2008 peak to March 2010. High-yield stocks did not escape the crash: the highest-yielding 30% of payers lost 33.0% in 2008, against 44.2% for the lowest-yielding payers and 41.4% for non-payers.
What it teaches
Dividend stocks can fall less than the market in a crash, but they still fall a lot, and their income can fall with them. They are stocks, not bonds.
Source
Shiller, ie_data.xls; Kenneth R. French Data Library, Portfolios Formed on D/P.
Case study 3When high yield helped: 2022
What happened
In 2022, as interest rates rose and expensive growth stocks fell, the highest-yielding 30% of US dividend payers gained 7.7% while the lowest-yielding payers lost 21.9%.
What it teaches
Dividend strategies have periods of real strength, largely when value stocks lead. That is the case for including them as part of a diversified portfolio, and the case against making them all of it.
Source
Kenneth R. French Data Library, Portfolios Formed on D/P.

Decisions

Income or total return?

Scenario 1 · Ruth

Ruth wants to "never touch principal." A friend suggests moving her $780,000 IRA into a high-dividend fund yielding about 3.3%, so she can live on the roughly $26,000 a year it pays. She receives $34,800 a year from Social Security and needs about $31,000 from the IRA. What should she weigh?

High-dividend fundIt would pay about $5,000 a year less than she needs, concentrate her stocks in fewer than 100 companies, and its income can be cut in a recession, as in 2008–2010. Inside an IRA it offers no tax advantage.
Total return, planned withdrawalRight. Inside her IRA, dividends and share sales are taxed identically as ordinary income when withdrawn. A written withdrawal rate (INV-076) and a cash or bond reserve for bad years give her the security she wants. Her required minimum distributions begin at 73, in 2031 (INV-081).
All CDsGuaranteed income, but at the cost of growth over what may be a 25-year retirement; inflation would steadily shrink what the income buys (INV-001, INV-008).
The principle: decide what you can safely spend, then fund it from the whole portfolio.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers

In their $220,000 taxable account the Harpers are considering replacing some technology stocks with high-dividend stocks yielding 3.5% "to diversify and get some income." They do not need the income; they reinvest it. What is the catch?

No catchOn a 3.5% yield, $100,000 would produce $3,500 of dividends and about $658 of federal tax a year at 18.8%, every year, for income they do not spend.
Mind the tax dragRight. For investors who reinvest, a lower-yield, broad fund in the taxable account, and higher-yield holdings inside their 401(k)s, is usually more tax-efficient (INV-036).
The principle: in a taxable account, unwanted income is a tax bill.
Scenario 3 · Jordan

Jordan's employer stock pays a 2.5% dividend, and Jordan says "the dividend is a reason to keep it." Is it?

Keep for the dividendA dividend does not reduce the risk of a single company. In a crisis, the dividend, the share price and the paycheck can all fall together.
The dividend is not a reasonRight. Jordan can get the same cash flow from a diversified fund, or by selling a few shares. The plan to reduce the concentration stands (INV-004, INV-040).
The principle: judge a holding by its risk and total return; the dividend is just one piece of the return.

Six claims about dividends

Apply it

Dividends and income for each household

Reinvest and ignore the yield. In her 401(k), dividends are reinvested automatically and untaxed. Maya should choose funds on cost and diversification; yield is irrelevant for decades.
The "dividend growth" fund is a style tilt. One of their six funds is a dividend growth fund. It overlaps heavily with their other large-cap funds; it is not a safety feature (INV-044).
The employer dividend is not protection. The cash flow can be replicated from a diversified fund; the concentration cannot be justified by it.
Watch the tax drag. With income above $250,000, every dollar of dividends in their taxable account is taxed at about 18.8% federally. Hold high-yield investments in their 401(k)s where possible.
Plan the paycheck, not the yield. Social Security covers $34,800 a year. For the rest, a planned withdrawal from a diversified IRA, with a cash and CD reserve, gives steadier income than hoping dividends suffice.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A stock trades at $100 and pays a $3 dividend. Ignoring any other price movement, at about what price should it open on the ex-dividend date, in dollars?

The company pays out $3 of cash per share, so each share represents $3 less value: about $97. You hold $97 of stock plus $3 of cash.
Exercise 2

In a taxable account you sell $10,000 of shares whose cost basis is $5,000, at a 15% long-term rate. How much federal tax do you owe, in dollars?

Only the gain is taxed: $10,000 − $5,000 = $5,000; × 0.15 = $750. The same $10,000 as qualified dividends would cost $1,500.
Exercise 3

Ruth's $780,000 is invested at a 3.34% yield. How much would she receive in dividends in a year, in dollars?

780,000 × 0.0334 = $26,052, about $5,100 short of a $31,200 need.
Exercise 4

How large a portfolio yielding 1.06% would you need to receive $40,000 a year in dividends, in dollars?

40,000 ÷ 0.0106 = $3,773,585. A 4% total-return withdrawal would need $1 million.
