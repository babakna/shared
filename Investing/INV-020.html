Debt vs Investing

Every extra dollar can pay down a debt or buy an investment. Paying a debt earns its interest rate with certainty; investing earns an uncertain return. This module compares the two by rate and tax, tests the avalanche and snowball payoff methods, explains federal student loans under the rules that took effect in July 2026, and ends with a sensible order of operations.

The question this module answersShould my next dollar pay off debt or be invested, and which debt first?

Start here

Paying off a debt is an investment with a guaranteed return

Most people have a feel for this already: a credit-card balance is an emergency, a low-rate mortgage is not. That instinct is right, and this module makes it precise. Every dollar that pays down a debt "earns" the interest you no longer pay, with certainty and without tax. Every dollar invested earns whatever the market delivers.

That is why an employer match and high-rate debt sit at the top of every sensible list. So the comparison is always between a certain return (the debt's after-tax interest rate) and an uncertain one (the investment's expected return). High-rate debt wins almost every time; very low-rate debt usually loses; in between, it is a judgment about risk, taxes and peace of mind.

APRannual percentage rate
What it is
The yearly cost of borrowing, including interest and some fees, stated as a percentage of the balance.
Used for
Comparing debts, and comparing a debt with what the money could earn elsewhere.
What it means for you
Repaying a card at 22% APR is like earning 22% risk-free. No mainstream investment reliably offers that.

Several debts

Highest rate first, or smallest balance first?

Debt avalanche and debt snowball
What it is
Both pay every minimum, then send all extra money to one target debt; when it is gone, its payment "rolls" to the next. The avalanche targets the highest interest rate first. The snowball targets the smallest balance first.
Used for
Deciding the order in which to attack several debts at once.
What it means for you
The avalanche always costs the least interest. The snowball gives earlier wins. The difference is often smaller than people expect; the method you stick with beats the one you abandon.

The calculator starts with four illustrative debts totaling $18,100: a $6,500 card at 24.9%, a $1,200 store card at 19.9%, a $600 interest-free medical payment plan, and a $9,800 car loan at 7.4%, with $250 a month beyond the minimums.

Going furtherWhat the research says about motivation

Kettle, Trudel, Blanchard and Häubl (2016) studied indebted consumers in a field study and three experiments. Concentrating repayments on one account, rather than spreading them, boosted people's motivation to get out of debt, "most pronounced when the repayments are concentrated into consumers' smallest accounts," because people judge progress by the share of a single balance they have repaid. That is the psychology behind the snowball. The interest cost is real, but so is the risk of quitting.

Practitioner depthHow the calculator works, and a hybrid

Each month: interest is added to each balance (APR ÷ 12), every minimum is paid, then the extra plus any freed minimums go to the target. Real card minimums fall as balances fall, which makes minimum-only paying slower still. A common hybrid clears any very small balance first for a quick win, then switches to the avalanche. When two debts' rates are close, choose the one whose payoff frees the largest monthly payment.

The core comparison

Compare the after-tax cost of the debt with what investing might earn

After-tax cost of debt = interest rate × (1 − tax rate at which the interest is deductible)

Most consumer debt (cards, car loans) is not deductible, so its after-tax cost is simply its rate. Student-loan interest is deductible up to a limit; mortgage interest only if you itemize. Then compare:

After-tax debt rateTypical debtsUsual answer (a heuristic)
Above about 8%Credit cards, many personal loans, some private student loansRepay aggressively after capturing any employer match; few investments reliably beat a certain 8%+
About 4–8%Many car loans, federal student loans, recent mortgagesJudgment call: split extra money, weigh risk tolerance, liquidity and tax-advantaged room
Below about 4%Older mortgages, some auto promotionsUsually invest (or hold safe cash, which can out-earn the debt) rather than prepay

Try Maya's student loan: $300 a month extra, 6.8% rate, deductible at her 12% bracket, versus investing at an assumed 6%.

Rules changed in 2025–2026

Federal student loans under the new rules

Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025 (which the Department of Education now calls the Working Families Tax Cuts Act), rewrote federal student-loan repayment. The Department's final regulations took effect July 1, 2026. What applies to you depends on when your loans were made.

Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP)
What it is
The new income-driven plan. Annual "base payment" = 1% of AGI for AGI of $10,000–$20,000, rising one point per $10,000 band to 10% above $100,000 ($120 a year at $10,000 or less). Monthly payment = base ÷ 12 − $50 per dependent, never below $10.
Used for
Keeping payments tied to income. For on-time payments, unpaid interest is waived, and if a payment reduces principal by less than $50 the government reduces it by up to $50 more. Any balance left after 360 qualifying payments (30 years) is forgiven; RAP payments also count toward Public Service Loan Forgiveness.
What it means for you
A lower payment is not a cheaper loan if your income is high enough to keep the balance falling slowly: you pay interest for longer.

Maya's choice. Her $28,000 at 6.8% was borrowed before July 2026, so the standard 10-year plan is available: $322.22 a month, about $10,667 of total interest. Her 2026 AGI is about $55,100, so RAP would ask about $230 a month ($55,100 × 5% ÷ 12 = $229.58). Her monthly interest starts at $158.67, so the balance still falls and the interest waiver and match do not apply; if her income never rose, the loan would take about 17 years and about $19,700 of interest. The lower payment costs about $9,000 more.

Going furtherRefinancing federal loans with a private lender

A private refinance can lower the rate for a borrower with high, stable income, but it converts a federal loan into a private one permanently. The borrower gives up income-driven repayment plans, federal deferment and forbearance options, and eligibility for Public Service Loan Forgiveness. For someone like Maya, early in her career, those protections are worth more than a modest rate cut.

Practitioner depthNew borrowing limits

The same law eliminated new Graduate PLUS loans, set a new lifetime borrowing cap ($257,500 for most borrowers), capped parent PLUS borrowing, and reduced annual limits for students enrolled less than full time. Borrowers with loans made on or after July 1, 2026 may choose only the Tiered Standard plan or RAP. Existing borrowers in PAYE or ICR must elect a new plan before July 1, 2028; those who do not are generally moved to RAP.

Rules that change the rate

Four tax rules to know for 2026

Worked example. Maya pays about $1,842 of student-loan interest in 2026. Her income is below the phase-out, so she can deduct all of it. In the 12% bracket that saves about $221 of federal tax, and her loan's after-tax rate is 6.8% × (1 − 0.12) ≈ 5.98%.

The Riveras' mortgage. Their interest is roughly 3.1% × $310,000 ≈ $9,600 a year, far below the $32,200 joint standard deduction, so they almost certainly take the standard deduction and get no tax benefit from the interest. Its after-tax cost is simply 3.1%, below what a 3-month Treasury bill yielded in September 2026 (4.08%). Prepaying it would earn less than safe cash.

Going furtherDebt you should never prepay with retirement money

Withdrawing from a 401(k) or IRA to pay off debt usually triggers income tax plus, before age 59½, a 10% additional tax unless an exception applies. Losing a quarter or more of the money to pay off a 7% loan is rarely a good trade. A 401(k) loan avoids the tax but becomes due quickly if you leave the job, and the borrowed money misses market growth (INV-031).

Putting it together

A financial order of operations

Several personal-finance educators publish a "financial order of operations," a sequence for the next dollar. The details differ; the logic is the same: take free money and certain high returns first, protect against disaster, then fill tax-advantaged accounts, then everything else. Treat the sequence below as a heuristic, not a rule.

Going furtherWhy the match comes before high-interest debt

A 50% match returns 50 cents on each dollar immediately, more than a year of interest on even a 29% card. The only reason to skip it is a genuine cash crisis in which minimum payments cannot be made. After the match, the high-rate debt takes priority over further retirement saving.

Real history and law

Three lessons from recent years

Case study 1Credit-card rates jump, 2021–2024
What happened
The average rate on credit-card accounts charged interest was 16.45% in 2021. As the Federal Reserve raised interest rates to fight inflation, it climbed to 22.15% in 2023 and 22.89% in 2024 (annual averages), and was 22.15% in the second quarter of 2026.
What it teaches
Card rates are variable. A balance that seemed manageable at 16% cost about 39% more in interest by 2024, with no change in the borrower's behavior.
Source
Federal Reserve, Consumer Credit – G.19; TERMCBCCINTNS via FRED.
Case study 2From SAVE to RAP: repayment rules can change under you
What happened
The SAVE income-driven plan was held unlawful by a federal appeals court (Missouri v. Biden, 8th Cir. 2024), and on March 10, 2026 a federal district court vacated most of the SAVE rule. Meanwhile Public Law 119-21 (July 4, 2025) created the Repayment Assistance Plan and the Tiered Standard plan from July 1, 2026, and phases out PAYE and ICR by July 1, 2028.
What it teaches
A plan that depends on one program's fine print lasting for 20 years carries policy risk. Borrowers who kept an emergency fund and chose a payment they could afford under any plan were least disrupted.
Source
U.S. Department of Education, final regulations, 91 FR 23768 (May 1, 2026); Dear Colleague Letter GEN-25-04 (July 18, 2025).
Case study 3Why the snowball works for some people
What happened
Kettle and colleagues found in a field study and three experiments that concentrating repayments on one account increased consumers' motivation to become debt-free, most strongly when they concentrated on their smallest accounts.
What it teaches
The mathematically best plan is only best if you follow it. For someone who has quit before, paying a little more interest for early wins can be the cheaper choice overall.
Source
Kettle, K. L., Trudel, R., Blanchard, S. J., & Häubl, G. (2016). Repayment concentration and consumer motivation to get out of debt. Journal of Consumer Research, 43(3), 460–477.

Decisions

Three debt decisions

Scenario 1 · Maya

Maya captures her full match and is building her emergency fund. Once it is full she will have $300 a month extra. Loan at 6.8%, or a Roth IRA?

All to the loanA certain 5.98% after tax, and she is debt-free sooner. Reasonable, especially if debt weighs on her. The cost: Roth IRA room she does not use this year is lost for good.
SplitA common answer in the 4–8% zone: part certain return, part long-run growth in a tax-free account. The calculator shows the two paths close together at her assumptions (repaying slightly ahead), so hedging makes sense.
All to the RothDefensible at 24 with decades ahead, but "stocks return more" is an expectation, not a promise. At her assumptions there is no expected edge: 5.98% charged monthly compounds to about 6.15% a year, slightly more than the 6% assumed return.
The principle: in the middle zone, there is no wrong answer, only a trade-off between certainty and growth.
Scenario 2 · The Riveras

Marcus wants to put the Riveras' extra $41,000 toward their 3.1% mortgage "to be debt-free." Elena wants to start 529 plans. What does the math say?

PrepayEarns a certain 3.1%, less than Treasury bills paid in September 2026. It also locks the cash in the house, where it cannot pay for a job loss or college.
Keep the mortgageRight on the numbers. Their low fixed rate is an asset. INV-034 shows how 529s add tax-free growth and, in many states, a state tax benefit; Arizona's rules are covered in INV-041.
The principle: do not prepay debt that costs less than safe investments earn.
Scenario 3 · The Harpers

The Harpers have $90,000 left on their mortgage (assume 4%, illustrative) and $220,000 in technology stocks in a taxable account. They plan to retire at 62. Should they sell stock to pay off the mortgage now?

All at onceSelling $90,000 of appreciated stock in a high-income year could add a large capital-gains bill. The goal is right; the timing is costly.
Plan itA good fit: a paid-off house lowers the income they must withdraw in retirement, reduces sequence risk (INV-077), and trimming 25 tech stocks reduces concentration (INV-004). Spreading sales, or selling in lower-income early-retirement years, manages the tax (INV-037).
Never pay it offAt 4%, keeping the loan is not unreasonable while they work. But in retirement a fixed payment must be funded by withdrawals in every market; many retirees value the lower fixed costs.
The principle: near retirement, lower fixed costs and lower concentration often matter more than a small expected return edge.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A student loan charges 6.8%, and the interest is fully deductible in the 12% bracket. What is its after-tax cost, in percent?

6.8% × (1 − 0.12) = 5.98%.
Exercise 2

Under RAP, a borrower has adjusted gross income of $45,000 and one dependent. What is the monthly payment, in dollars?

$45,000 is in the $40,000–$50,000 band: 4%. Base = $1,800 a year = $150 a month. Minus $50 for one dependent = $100.
Exercise 3

A $5,000 card balance carries a 24% APR. Roughly how much interest accrues in one month, in dollars?

24% ÷ 12 = 2% a month; $5,000 × 0.02 = $100.
Exercise 4

Maya's employer matches 50% of contributions up to 6% of her $62,000 salary. If she raises her contribution from 3% to 6%, how much more match does she receive per year, in dollars?

The extra 3% is $1,860; 50% of that is $930, an instant 50% return before any investment growth.
