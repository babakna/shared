Cash and Cash Equivalents

Every household needs money that will be there, in full, on a known date. Savings accounts, certificates of deposit (CDs), money market funds, Treasury bills and savings bonds all promise that, but they differ in who stands behind them, how easily you can get out, how they are taxed, and how much they pay. In September 2026 the gap between the worst and best of them was more than four percentage points.

The question this module answersWhere should money I will need in the next few years sit, so it is safe, available and not quietly losing ground?

Start here

The money you cannot afford to lose

You already know the ordinary case: keep some money in the bank for bills and emergencies, and invest the rest for the long run. That instinct is right. This module is about doing the first half well, because "cash" is not one thing, and the difference between a good and a careless choice is real money every year.

Think of three jobs for short-term money: paying this month's bills, absorbing a surprise such as a job loss or a car repair (the emergency fund, covered in INV-019), and holding money for a known expense a few years out, like a down payment or tuition. None of these jobs can wait for a stock market to recover. They need money whose dollar value does not fall. What they do not need is money that earns almost nothing.

The national average savings rate of 0.37% and a Treasury bill yield of 4.24% are both "safe," in the sense that you will get your dollars back. On $41,000 — the amount the Riveras keep idle in checking — that is the difference between about $152 and about $1,738 of interest a year. Nothing about the risk changed. Only the choice did.

Deposits

Savings accounts, and the insurance behind them

A bank deposit is a loan you make to a bank. The bank lends the money on, pays you interest, and promises to give it back on demand. What makes that promise safe for ordinary savers is not the bank itself; it is federal deposit insurance.

FDIC and NCUA insurancefederal deposit and share insurance
What it is
The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) insures deposits at member banks; the National Credit Union Administration (NCUA) insures credit union accounts through its Share Insurance Fund, which it describes as backed by the full faith and credit of the United States. Both cover $250,000 per depositor, per insured institution, for each account ownership category.
Used for
Making bank and credit union deposits safe even if the institution fails. Covered: checking, savings, money market deposit accounts and CDs. Not covered: stocks, bonds, mutual funds (including money market funds), annuities, life insurance, crypto assets, municipal securities, and Treasury bills, notes and bonds — even when a bank sells them.
What it means for you
Below $250,000 per category per bank, a bank's health is not your problem. Above it, spread money across banks or ownership categories, or use Treasury securities, which are backed directly by the US government.

Until 2020, federal rules limited "convenient" transfers from savings accounts to six a month. On April 24, 2020 the Federal Reserve removed that limit from Regulation D. Banks may still set their own limits, so read the account terms.

Going furtherWhy the average bank pays so little

Banks set deposit rates to attract the deposits they need, not to match the market. Customers who never move their money are cheap funding, so large banks can pay well below Treasury yields and keep the difference. Online banks, which have no branches and need to win customers, typically pay more. Nothing about a high-yield account at an FDIC-insured bank is riskier up to the insurance limit. The practical test: compare your rate with the three-month Treasury bill yield. If the gap is several points, you are paying for inertia.

Practitioner depthAPY versus interest rate

Truth in Savings rules require banks to disclose the annual percentage yield (APY), which includes compounding, so APYs are comparable across banks. A 4.00% rate compounded daily is an APY of (1 + 0.04/365)365 − 1 = 4.08%. Money market funds usually quote a seven-day yield instead: an annualized figure based on the fund's most recent week of income after expenses. Compare APY with APY and yield with yield; the differences are usually a few hundredths of a point.

Time deposits

Certificates of deposit: a better rate for a promise to wait

Certificate of deposit (CD)
What it is
A bank or credit union deposit held for a fixed term — three months to five years or more — usually at a fixed rate. It is insured like any other deposit.
Used for
Locking in a rate for money you will not need before a known date.
What it means for you
If you need the money early, you pay an early-withdrawal penalty. The penalty is set by the bank, not by law, so it is the first thing to read.

Federal law sets only a floor: under the Federal Reserve's Regulation D, a time deposit withdrawn within the first six days must carry a penalty of at least seven days' simple interest. Beyond that, each bank chooses its own penalty — commonly expressed as a number of months of interest — and the Truth in Savings rules (Regulation DD) require the bank to disclose how it is calculated before you open the account.

Penalty (simple interest) = amount × rate × days of penalty ÷ 365

Worked example (illustration). $20,000 in a one-year CD at 4.00% APY with a 90-day interest penalty. After six months the CD has earned 20,000 × (1.040.5 − 1) = $396.08. The penalty is 20,000 × 0.04 × 90 ÷ 365 = $197.26. Breaking the CD leaves $198.82 of interest — still positive, but half what was earned. Because each bank sets its own penalty, a penalty larger than the interest earned so far is possible, and the difference then comes out of principal.

Two CD variations to read carefully

  • Brokered CDs are bank CDs sold through a brokerage account. The Securities and Exchange Commission's (SEC) investor site notes that deposit brokers can sometimes negotiate higher rates, but that no state or federal agency licenses, examines or approves deposit brokers, so check who is selling. Before buying, ask how you would get out early: if the only way is to sell the CD to another investor, you receive the market price, which falls when interest rates rise.
  • Callable CDs let the bank end the CD early. Banks call when rates fall, which is exactly when you would want to keep the old rate. A callable five-year CD is not a five-year promise; it is a promise that lasts only as long as it suits the bank.
Going furtherCDs versus Treasuries of the same term

In September 2026 the FDIC's national average 12-month CD rate was 1.73%, while the one-year Treasury yielded 4.50% (September 25). Competitive CDs from online banks and credit unions narrow that gap, and a CD can beat a Treasury for someone in a state with no income tax. For everyone else, remember that Treasury interest is free of state and local income tax, and CD interest is not; compare after tax (the calculator in the Treasury bills tab does it).

Cash inside a brokerage account

Money market funds, and the sweep you may not have chosen

When you sell an investment or deposit money in a brokerage account, the cash has to sit somewhere until you invest it. Brokerages usually "sweep" it automatically into one of two very different places.

Money market funda mutual fund, not a bank account
What it is
A mutual fund that invests in liquid, short-term debt: Treasury bills, repurchase agreements, commercial paper. The SEC describes three categories: government funds (which must hold at least 99.5% of assets in cash, government securities and fully collateralized repurchase agreements, under SEC Rule 2a-7), tax-exempt (municipal) funds, and prime funds, which also buy corporate short-term debt.
Used for
Holding cash in brokerage accounts and as an alternative to bank savings. Most retail and government funds aim for a stable $1.00 share price and pay income that tracks short-term interest rates.
What it means for you
Money market funds are not FDIC-insured, and the SEC reminds investors you can lose money in one. In practice, government money market funds hold mostly the same Treasury bills you could buy yourself, and usually pay close to T-bill yields minus a small expense ratio.

A bank sweep program is the other option. The brokerage moves your cash into deposits at one or more partner banks, where it is FDIC-insured, and the brokerage or its affiliate sets the interest rate. That rate can be far below a money market fund's. In January 2025 the SEC settled charges against two Wells Fargo advisory firms and Merrill Lynch over their sweep programs, finding that the gap between the bank deposit sweep and other alternatives "at times grew to almost 4 percent" during the period of rising rates. The firms paid $60 million in penalties without admitting or denying the findings.

The 2023 rule changes

The SEC tightened money market fund rules again on July 12, 2023. It removed funds' ability to suspend redemptions temporarily (a "gate"), required institutional prime and institutional tax-exempt funds to charge a liquidity fee when daily net redemptions exceed 5% of net assets, allowed any non-government fund's board to impose a discretionary fee, and raised the minimum share of assets that must mature or be sellable within a day and a week (to 25% and 50%). Institutional prime and institutional tax-exempt funds must also float their share price, so they can be bought and sold slightly above or below $1.00 (Code of Federal Regulations, 17 CFR 270.2a-7).

Going furtherWhich money market fund for which account

Government funds are the default choice for most people: they carry essentially no credit risk and are exempt from the mandatory liquidity fees. In a taxable account, part of a government fund's income may come from Treasury securities and may be exempt from state income tax; ask the fund for its year-end percentage. Tax-exempt funds can make sense for high earners in high-tax states, using the tax-equivalent yield in INV-010. Check the seven-day yield and the expense ratio: they differ more than the risk does.

Practitioner depthWhat "breaking the buck" means

A stable-price fund values its holdings at amortized cost, and its board must promptly consider action if the market value per share deviates from that price by more than half a cent (1/2 of 1 percent). A fund whose share price is re-set below $1.00 has "broken the buck." It is rare, but it happened in September 2008 (see Case studies). Securities Investor Protection Corporation (SIPC) protection at a failed brokerage (INV-005) covers missing securities, including fund shares, but not a fall in a fund's value.

Lending to the US government

Treasury bills: the benchmark for safe short-term money

Treasury bill (T-bill)
What it is
Short-term debt of the US government, sold in terms of 4, 6, 8, 13, 17, 26 and 52 weeks. Bills pay no coupon: you buy at a discount and receive the face value at maturity; the difference is your interest. Minimum purchase $100, in $100 increments, at TreasuryDirect or through a brokerage.
Used for
Money needed within a year, and as the "risk-free rate" against which other investments are compared.
What it means for you
Backed by the US government, not a bank. Interest is taxed federally but is exempt from state and local income tax, which is worth real money in a high-tax state.
Return over the term = (face value − price) ÷ price     Annualized ≈ return × 365 ÷ days

Worked example. A 26-week (182-day) bill with a $10,000 face value costs $9,795. At maturity you receive $10,000: a gain of $205, or 205 ÷ 9,795 = 2.093% over 26 weeks, which annualizes to about 4.20%. Treasury's published "investment rate" uses a slightly more exact version of the same idea.

You can buy a bill once and let it mature, or set TreasuryDirect or your broker to reinvest ("roll") it automatically. Bills can be sold before maturity through a broker at the market price; because they are so short, that price rarely moves much.

Compare every option after tax

Enter your own quotes. The calculator applies federal tax to everything and state tax only where it applies, and it respects the I bond's one-year lock and early-redemption penalty.

Why the state exemption matters. Suppose a CD pays 4.40% and a T-bill 4.24%, and you are in the 22% federal bracket and a 5% state. The CD keeps 4.40 × (1 − 0.27) = 3.21%; the T-bill keeps 4.24 × (1 − 0.22) = 3.31%. The lower quoted rate wins.

TreasuryDirect

I bonds and EE bonds: the current rules

US savings bonds are sold only by the Treasury, only electronically (paper bonds through tax refunds ended on January 1, 2025), and only through a TreasuryDirect account. They cannot be sold to anyone else; you cash them in with the Treasury. Both types share these rules, as published by TreasuryDirect in September 2026:

I bondSeries I savings bond
What it is
A savings bond whose rate combines a fixed rate, set when you buy and never changing, and an inflation rate reset every May 1 and November 1 from the non-seasonally-adjusted Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U). The combined rate cannot fall below zero.
Used for
Inflation-protected savings for money you can leave alone for at least a year.
What it means for you
A near-perfect home for part of an emergency fund you are unlikely to touch in year one, or for money earmarked several years out. The $10,000 limit keeps it a supplement, not the whole plan.
Composite rate = fixed + (2 × semiannual inflation) + (fixed × semiannual inflation)

For May–October 2026: 0.0090 + (2 × 0.0167) + (0.0090 × 0.0167) = 0.04255, which rounds to 4.26%. The 0.90% fixed part is what you keep, above inflation, for 30 years; the 1.67% six-month inflation rate will be replaced on November 1, 2026.

EE bondSeries EE savings bond
What it is
A savings bond with a fixed rate (2.40% for May–October 2026) and a Treasury guarantee that its value will at least double after 20 years.
Used for
Money you are sure you can leave for exactly 20 years or more.
What it means for you
Doubling in 20 years is 21/20 − 1 = 3.53% a year. Cash in at year 19 and you get the 2.40% rate, not 3.53%. The EE bond rewards patience and punishes a change of plans.
Going furtherThe education exclusion

Interest on EE and I bonds can be excluded from federal income tax if you use the proceeds for qualified higher-education expenses, the bond's owner was at least 24 when it was issued (TreasuryDirect stresses that a bond registered to the child will not qualify), and your income is below a limit. For tax year 2026, IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-32 sets the phase-out at modified adjusted gross income above $101,800 (single and head of household, fully phased out at $116,800) and above $152,650 for joint returns (fully phased out at $182,650). The limit that matters is the one for the year you cash the bonds in. INV-034 compares this with 529 plans, which are usually more flexible for college.

Practitioner depthHow the six-month clock works

Each bond's rate changes every six months from its own issue month, not on May 1 and November 1. A bond issued in September moves to a new rate each March 1 and September 1. Interest accrues monthly and is added to principal every six months (semiannual compounding). Because bonds earn from the first day of the month of purchase, buying late in a month earns a full month's interest. TreasuryDirect's displayed values for bonds under five years old already exclude the three-month penalty.

98 years of data

Safe in dollars is not the same as safe in buying power

Treasury bills are as close as the market gets to a risk-free asset in dollars. But after inflation, the record is sobering. In the Damodaran data used throughout this course, three-month T-bills returned 3.37% a year from 1928 to 2025 while prices rose 3.04% a year. The real return was 0.33% a year. A dollar in T-bills at the start of 1928 bought about 1.38 dollars' worth of goods at the end of 2025. The same dollar in stocks bought hundreds of times more (INV-001).

Real events

Three times "safe" money was tested

Case study 1The Reserve Primary Fund breaks the buck, 2008
What happened
Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy on September 15, 2008. The Reserve Primary Fund, a large prime money market fund, held $785 million of Lehman-issued securities. According to the SEC, it became illiquid on September 15 when it could not meet investors' redemption requests, and on September 16 its net asset value fell below $1.00 per share — it "broke the buck." The SEC later charged the fund's operators with fraud for misleading investors and trustees during the crisis.
What it teaches
A money market fund is an investment, not an insured deposit. Prime funds took credit risk to earn a little more; government funds, which hold Treasury and agency debt, avoid that risk. Money market fund rules have been tightened several times since, most recently in 2023.
Source
U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (2009, May 5). SEC charges operators of Reserve Primary Fund with fraud (Press release 2009-104); SEC Litigation Release 21025.
Case study 2Silicon Valley Bank, 2023
What happened
The Federal Reserve's review found that about 94% of Silicon Valley Bank's deposits were uninsured at year-end 2022, and that the bank had invested heavily in long-dated securities that lost value as interest rates rose. After it announced a restructuring on March 8, 2023, depositors withdrew more than $40 billion on March 9, and management expected $100 billion more the next day. California regulators closed the bank on March 10.
What it teaches
Deposits above $250,000 per ownership category are exposed to the bank's own health. Businesses and wealthy individuals kept millions at one bank. Do not plan on a rescue above the limit. For individuals, the fix is simple: stay within the limits, or hold Treasury bills, which do not depend on any bank.
Source
Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System (2023, April 28). Review of the Federal Reserve's supervision and regulation of Silicon Valley Bank.
Case study 3Cash sweep programs, 2025
What happened
On January 17, 2025 the SEC announced settlements with Wells Fargo Clearing Services ($28 million), Wells Fargo Advisors Financial Network ($7 million) and Merrill Lynch ($25 million) — $60 million in total — for failing to adopt reasonable policies on cash sweep programs in advisory accounts. The firms offered bank deposit sweeps as the only sweep option for most advisory clients, and during rising rates the yield gap with other alternatives at times reached almost 4 percentage points. The firms neither admitted nor denied the findings.
What it teaches
Idle cash in an investment account is a choice, even when you did not make it. Look up your account's sweep rate and, if it is low, buy a money market fund or T-bills with the cash you intend to hold.
Source
U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (2025, January 17). SEC charges pair of Wells Fargo advisory firms and Merrill Lynch with compliance failures relating to cash sweep programs (Press release 2025-16).

Decisions

Three households, three cash decisions

Scenario 1 · The Riveras

The Riveras keep a $9,000 emergency fund and another $41,000 in checking that has built up over two years. Their checking pays almost nothing. What should they do with the $41,000?

Leave itIt is safe, but at 0.07% it earns about $29 a year; a government money market fund near T-bill yields would earn roughly $1,700, and inflation of 3% costs them about $1,230 of buying power a year either way.
Give each dollar a jobRight. With two incomes and a mortgage, they can size the emergency fund from their own essential expenses (INV-019 works through how many months). Money for a car or a roof in two years belongs in a ladder or T-bills. Money with no date belongs in their long-term plan.
All into stocksBetter than idle cash over decades, but if part of this money is really their safety net, a stock fall could force them to sell at the wrong moment. Decide the job first, then the vehicle.
The principle: cash is a tool with a purpose, not a default. Idle money should be either working as insurance or invested.
Scenario 2 · Jordan

Jordan has $20,000 in savings for Ava's college, which starts in three years. A coworker says to "put it in the market to catch up." Jordan's income is $88,000.

All stocksIn the 1928–2025 data, the S&P 500 lost money in 26 of 98 calendar years, and three-year stretches have fallen far more than a family budget can absorb. With a fixed deadline, the risk falls on Ava's first semester.
Match maturitiesRight. A ladder locks today's yields for known dates. I bonds can be cashed after 12 months; the education exclusion could make their interest federally tax-free if the bonds are in Jordan's name and Jordan's income is under the limit in the year of redemption (for 2026, the phase-out starts at $101,800 of modified adjusted gross income for head of household). The $10,000 annual limit means the rest goes in CDs or Treasuries. A 529 plan (INV-034) is also worth comparing.
Stay putSafe, but it gives away several hundred dollars a year compared with competitive rates. Safe and efficient are not in conflict here.
The principle: the closer the deadline, the more certain the money must be; then pick the best-paying certain option.
Scenario 3 · Ruth

Ruth has $60,000 in CDs and savings at her local bank. A five-year CD is maturing, and the bank offers to renew it automatically at 1.73% (an illustrative offer).

Auto-renewAt 1.73% on $60,000 she earns about $1,040 a year. At a competitive 4.00% (an illustrative quote) she would earn $2,400. Automatic renewal is where banks pay the least.
Shop and planRight. Pennsylvania taxes interest, so Treasuries' state exemption helps Ruth. She also has a $780,000 IRA; her cash reserve and her IRA's bond holdings should be planned together (INV-076 on withdrawals).
The principle: loyalty is expensive. Every maturity date is a chance to re-shop.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

An I bond has a fixed rate of 0.90% and a semiannual inflation rate of 1.67%. Using Treasury's formula, what is its composite rate, in percent (two decimals)?

0.0090 + (2 × 0.0167) + (0.0090 × 0.0167) = 0.0090 + 0.0334 + 0.00015 = 0.04255, which Treasury rounds to 4.26%.
Exercise 2

You pay $9,795 for a 182-day Treasury bill with a $10,000 face value. What is its simple annualized return, in percent?

Gain $205; 205 ÷ 9,795 = 2.093% over 182 days; × 365 ÷ 182 = 4.20%.
Exercise 3

You are in the 22% federal bracket and pay 5% state income tax. A T-bill yields 4.24%. What do you keep after tax, in percent? (A 4.40% CD keeps 3.21%.)

T-bill interest is not taxed by states: 4.24 × (1 − 0.22) = 3.31%. The CD: 4.40 × (1 − 0.22 − 0.05) = 3.21%.
Exercise 4

$20,000 in a one-year CD at 4.00% APY is broken after six months. The penalty is 90 days of simple interest at 4%. How many dollars of interest are left?

Earned: 20,000 × (1.040.5 − 1) = $396.08. Penalty: 20,000 × 0.04 × 90 ÷ 365 = $197.26. Left: $198.82.
