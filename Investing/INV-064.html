Mortgages

A mortgage is usually the largest loan of a lifetime and, for many households, the largest single cost in the budget. This module explains how a mortgage is priced and repaid, how to choose among fixed and adjustable rates and points, how to get rid of mortgage insurance, when refinancing pays, and whether extra money should go to the mortgage or to investments.

The question this module answersHow do I choose, manage and pay off a mortgage without overpaying for it?

Start here

A loan you repay in equal monthly slices

If you have had a car loan or a student loan, you already know the shape of a mortgage: you borrow a lump sum and repay it in equal monthly payments over a set term. The difference is size and length. A mortgage is often several times your annual income, and it runs 15 or 30 years.

Two facts about that equal payment surprise most first-time borrowers. First, early payments are mostly interest, because interest is charged on the balance still owed, and the balance is largest at the start. Second, over 30 years the total interest can exceed the amount borrowed. On a $300,000 loan at 7.03% — the average 30-year rate in Freddie Mac's survey on September 24, 2026 — the payment is $2,002 a month, and total interest over 30 years is about $420,700.

Choosing the rate

Certainty, or a lower rate for now?

Fixed-rate mortgage
What it is
A loan whose interest rate never changes, so principal and interest are the same every month for 15, 20 or 30 years.
Used for
Locking in housing costs. In the US, most borrowers can also repay early without penalty, so if rates fall they can refinance; if rates rise they keep their rate.
What it means for you
You buy protection against rising rates. That protection is why fixed rates are usually higher than an adjustable loan's starting rate.
Adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM)
What it is
A loan with a fixed rate for an initial period, often 5, 7 or 10 years, that then resets periodically. The new rate is a published market index plus a fixed margin set in the loan, limited by rate caps.
Used for
Borrowers who expect to sell or refinance before the first reset, or who can absorb higher payments later.
What it means for you
The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) describes three caps: an initial adjustment cap (commonly 2 or 5 percentage points), a subsequent cap on each later adjustment (commonly 1 or 2 points) and a lifetime cap (commonly 5 points). Compute the payment at the lifetime cap before you sign.
Going furtherWhen an ARM can make sense

If you are confident you will sell within the fixed period — a job posting of known length, a planned move — an ARM's lower starting rate can save money with little reset risk. The risk is that plans change, and that when you need to sell, prices or your income may have fallen at the same time rates rose. The foreclosure wave after 2006 (INV-066) showed how rate resets, falling prices and lost refinancing options can arrive together.

Practitioner depthReading the ARM disclosures

ARMs are described as "initial period / adjustment frequency," for example a 5/6-month or 7/1 ARM, with caps written as three numbers (initial / periodic / lifetime). The loan documents name the index, the margin, the first adjustment date, and the floor below which the rate cannot fall. The Loan Estimate a lender gives you shows how the payment can change over time; compare the highest possible payment across offers, not only the starting rate.

How the payment works

The formula, and what extra payments do

Amortization
What it is
Repaying a loan through equal payments calculated so that the last payment brings the balance exactly to zero. Each month, interest = balance × monthly rate; the rest of the payment reduces the balance.
Used for
Computing the payment for any loan amount, rate and term, and the balance at any date.
What it means for you
Any extra principal you pay skips ahead in the schedule. Every later payment then carries less interest and more principal, so the loan ends early.
M = P × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)−n)    P = amount borrowed, r = annual rate ÷ 12, n = number of monthly payments

The defaults are the Riveras' mortgage. They refinanced in fall 2021 into a 30-year fixed loan of about $348,000 at 3.1% (an illustrative detail consistent with their $310,000 balance today), so about 25 years remain and the payment is $1,486 of principal and interest. Scheduled payments will cost about $135,900 more in interest. Adding $500 a month would cut that to about $87,400, a saving of about $48,500, and end the loan 8 years and 3 months early.

Going furtherA 15-year loan, or a 30-year loan paid like one?

A 15-year loan has a lower rate (0.61 points lower in the September 24, 2026 survey), which is a real saving. A 30-year loan with voluntary extra payments keeps the flexibility to stop paying extra in a lean year. People who value that flexibility, or who would otherwise invest the difference at a higher expected return, often choose the 30-year loan. People who value certainty and a lower rate, and can comfortably afford the payment, often choose the 15-year.

Practitioner depthBalance after k payments
Bk = P(1 + r)k − M[(1 + r)k − 1] ÷ r

This is the future value of the loan minus the future value of the payments. It is how a lender computes a payoff figure (plus accrued interest for the partial month) and how you can check the date your loan-to-value ratio reaches 80%, the key date for removing PMI.

Two upfront choices

Discount points, and getting rid of mortgage insurance

Discount points
What it is
Fees paid at closing to lower the interest rate. The CFPB: "One point equals one percent of the loan amount." How much a point lowers the rate "depends on the specific lender, the kind of loan, and the overall mortgage market." Lender credits work in reverse: a higher rate in exchange for help with closing costs.
Used for
Trading cash today for a lower payment over the life of the loan.
What it means for you
Points pay off only if you keep the loan longer than the break-even time. Most people sell or refinance well before 30 years, so be honest about your horizon.

At the defaults — one point ($3,000) to cut a 7.00% rate to 6.75% on $300,000 — the payment falls by about $50 a month. The simple break-even is 60 months; counting the faster-falling balance as well, about 4 years. Kept 7 years, the point is worth about $2,300; sold or refinanced after 3 years, it loses about $740.

Private mortgage insurance (PMI)
What it is
Insurance that protects the lender, not you, usually required on conventional loans with less than 20% down. It is paid monthly, upfront, or both.
Used for
Letting lenders make loans with smaller down payments.
What it means for you
The Homeowners Protection Act of 1998 gives you the right to remove it on most conventional loans. Loans insured by the Federal Housing Administration (FHA) follow different rules.
Homeowners Protection Act ruleWhenConditions
Borrower requestThe date the balance is scheduled to reach 80% of the home's original value (or earlier, if you pay it down)Written request; good payment history; current on payments; no junior liens; may need proof the value has not declined
Automatic terminationThe date the balance is scheduled to reach 78% of original valueYou are current on payments
Final terminationThe month after the midpoint of the amortization schedule (15 years on a 30-year loan)You are current on payments, even if 78% has not been reached

Source: CFPB, "When can I remove private mortgage insurance (PMI) from my loan?" "Original value" generally means the lower of the purchase price and the appraised value at purchase.

Going furtherFHA mortgage insurance

FHA loans charge an upfront premium of 1.75% of the base loan and an annual premium. Under Mortgagee Letter 2023-05 from the Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD), effective for loans endorsed from March 20, 2023, on a term over 15 years and a base loan at or below the national conforming loan limit ($726,200 when the letter was issued), the annual premium is 0.50% if the loan-to-value is 95% or less (0.55% above 95%). It lasts 11 years if the original loan-to-value was 90% or less and for the whole mortgage term if it was above 90%. With the minimum down payment, the usual way to end FHA insurance is to refinance into a conventional loan once you have enough equity.

Practitioner depthPoints and taxes

IRS Publication 936: points paid on a loan to buy your main home can often be deducted in full in the year paid if a list of conditions is met; points paid to refinance generally must be deducted evenly over the life of the loan. A deduction helps only if you itemize (INV-063). Lender credits reduce your costs rather than creating a deduction.

Replacing the loan

When refinancing pays, and the trap inside it

Refinancing
What it is
Taking out a new mortgage to pay off the old one, usually for a lower rate, a different term, or to take cash out (a cash-out refinance).
Used for
Cutting interest costs when rates fall, dropping PMI or FHA insurance, or switching from an ARM to a fixed rate.
What it means for you
It costs money: closing costs similar to a purchase loan. It pays only if you keep the new loan past the break-even point.
Simple break-even (months) = closing costs ÷ monthly payment saving

The defaults describe a household that bought with a 7.75% loan and still owes $380,000 with 28 years left. Refinancing into a new 30-year loan at 6.75% with $6,000 of closing costs cuts the payment from $2,773 to $2,465 — $308 a month — and breaks even in about 20 months. Kept ten years, it is worth about $29,300. Notice the term reset: the new loan runs two years longer than the old one, so part of the lower payment comes from stretching the debt. Total interest still falls, from about $551,700 to $507,300, because the rate cut is large.

Going furtherAvoiding the term-reset trap

A household that refinances every few years into a fresh 30-year loan can pay a mortgage for 40 years. Three ways to avoid it: choose a new term that matches the years you had left (20 or 15 years); keep making your old, higher payment on the new loan; or compare total interest from today to payoff under each option, which the calculator shows.

Practitioner depthThe rate lock-in effect

When market rates rise far above existing loans' rates, owners stop selling, because moving means giving up a cheap loan. After 30-year rates averaged 2.96% in 2021 and 3.11% in 2020, then 6.81% in 2023 (Freddie Mac), many owners faced exactly this choice. The lock-in effect is rational up to a point: the Riveras' 3.1% loan costs $1,486 a month, against about $2,197 for a new 25-year loan of the same $310,000 at 7.03% — a saving of about $710 a month. But if a household needs a different home, the lower rate is worth less than the life it prevents.

The classic dilemma

Extra money: to the mortgage, or to investments?

Paying extra principal earns exactly the mortgage's interest rate, with no risk: every dollar prepaid stops that rate being charged on it. Investing earns an uncertain return that may be higher or lower. So the comparison is a guaranteed return at your mortgage rate against an expected return elsewhere, after taxes, with the difference in risk and liquidity on top.

With the defaults — the Riveras' $310,000 at 3.1%, $500 extra a month, and a 4% after-tax return on the alternative — prepaying ends the loan in 16 years and 9 months, and investing the old payment afterward leaves them with about $234,400 by year 25. Investing the $500 from the start leaves about $254,400, roughly $20,000 more. At a 2% return, prepaying would win by about $21,700; at 7%, investing would win by about $125,700, with much more risk. The break-even is close to the mortgage rate itself.

💡

The order of operations matters more than the rate comparison. Before any extra mortgage payment: an emergency fund (INV-019), the full employer 401(k) match (INV-031), and higher-rate debt paid off (INV-020). Money prepaid into a house cannot be withdrawn in an emergency without selling or borrowing against it.

Going furtherTaxes change the effective rate

If you itemize, the mortgage interest deduction lowers the effective cost of your mortgage; if you take the standard deduction, it does not. The Riveras' first-year interest is about $9,500, far below the 2026 standard deduction of $32,200 for married couples filing jointly, so their effective mortgage rate is the full 3.1%. On the other side, investment returns in a taxable account are taxed; returns inside a 401(k) or IRA are sheltered (Stage 5). Compare after-tax to after-tax.

Practitioner depthPaying off before retirement

Near retirement the question shifts. A paid-off house lowers the income you must withdraw each year, which can lower taxes and the risk of selling investments in a bad market (INV-077). Against that, pulling a large sum from a pre-tax 401(k) to pay off a mortgage is itself taxable income. Many planners suggest paying it off from taxable savings or cash flow before retirement, when the rate is not far below safe yields, rather than with a large retirement-account withdrawal.

Real history and real law

Three mortgage stories

Case study 11981: the 16.64% year
What happened
With inflation high and the Federal Reserve raising short-term rates sharply, Freddie Mac's 30-year fixed rate averaged 16.64% for 1981, its highest annual average since the survey began in 1971. By 1993 the average was 7.31%.
What it teaches
A fixed-rate loan with no prepayment penalty is a one-way bet in the borrower's favor: borrowers who took 1981 loans could refinance as rates fell, while lenders holding 1970s loans at 8% to 9% could not raise them. That option has value, and borrowers pay for it in the rate.
Source
Freddie Mac, Primary Mortgage Market Survey, annual averages via FRED (MORTGAGE30US).
Case study 22020–2026: the refinance wave and the lock-in
What happened
Average 30-year rates fell to 3.11% in 2020 and 2.96% in 2021, and millions of owners refinanced; the Riveras' 3.1% loan is typical of that period. Rates then rose to 6.81% in 2023 and stood at 7.03% on September 24, 2026.
What it teaches
A low fixed rate is an asset that cannot be moved to a new house. The Riveras would pay about $710 a month more to borrow the same $310,000 over the same 25 years today. It is a good reason to keep the loan and to invest spare cash rather than prepay it; it is not a reason to stay in a home that no longer works.
Source
Freddie Mac PMMS (release of September 24, 2026; annual averages via FRED).
Case study 3The Homeowners Protection Act of 1998
What happened
Before the act, removing private mortgage insurance depended on the lender's willingness. The law created a borrower's right to request cancellation at a scheduled 80% of original value, required automatic termination at 78%, and set a final termination date at the loan's midpoint.
What it teaches
Knowing a rule is worth money. A borrower who asks at 80% rather than waiting for 78% saves more than a year of premiums in Figure 64.4; one who prepays to reach 80% sooner saves more.
Source
CFPB, "When can I remove private mortgage insurance (PMI) from my loan?"

Decisions

Three mortgage decisions

Scenario 1 · The Riveras

The Riveras have $41,000 sitting in checking on top of a $9,000 emergency fund. Marcus wants to put it all against the 3.1% mortgage "to be debt-free sooner." What would you advise?

Prepay it allIt feels good but earns 3.1% while safe alternatives pay more, and the money is locked in the house. With two children and one emergency fund, liquidity matters.
Put it to work, keep the loanThe strongest choice. Treasury bills alone beat the mortgage rate (Figure 64.7), and 529 plans and 401(k) contributions add tax benefits (INV-034, INV-031). The low-rate mortgage is the cheapest money they will ever borrow.
Leave it in checkingSafe but costly: most checking accounts pay little, so $41,000 loses ground to inflation every year (INV-008).
The principle: prepay a mortgage only when its rate beats what safe money can earn, and only after the higher priorities are funded.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers

The Harpers owe $90,000 on their Raleigh home and plan to retire at 62–63. Rachel wants the mortgage gone before then. Tom suggests withdrawing $90,000 from his pre-tax 401(k) now to pay it off. What should they consider?

401(k) withdrawal nowA $90,000 withdrawal is taxed as ordinary income on top of $260,000 of wages, likely in the 24% federal bracket or higher (the 24% bracket for 2026 starts above $211,400 of taxable income for joint filers), plus North Carolina tax. They would need to withdraw well over $90,000 to net $90,000. And Tom is 57 and still working: a 401(k) generally cannot pay out elective deferrals before age 59½ while he is employed unless he has a hardship, and a distribution before 59½ usually also owes the 10% additional tax on early distributions.
Pay down from cash flowSensible. With $260,000 of income they can direct extra cash to the loan, and their taxable account could cover part of it (INV-035). Entering retirement without a payment lowers the withdrawals they need each year.
The principle: the source of the payoff money matters as much as the payoff itself.
Scenario 3 · Jordan

If Jordan did buy the $325,000 home from INV-063, the lender offers 7.0% with no points or 6.75% for one point. Jordan may move in about four years, after Ava's college years begin. Points or no points?

Pay the pointOn a $292,500 loan the point costs $2,925 and saves about $49 a month. The simple break-even is about 60 months; even counting the faster-falling balance, the point only just breaks even at four years, so any earlier move loses money.
No pointsRight for a short or uncertain stay. If anything, a lender credit (a slightly higher rate in exchange for lower closing costs) could fit Jordan better.
The principle: pay points only for a loan you expect to keep past the break-even.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

What is the monthly principal and interest on a $250,000, 30-year fixed loan at 6%?

r = 0.06 ÷ 12 = 0.005; n = 360. (1.005)−360 = 0.16604. M = 250,000 × 0.005 ÷ (1 − 0.16604) = 1,250 ÷ 0.83396 = $1,498.88.
Exercise 2

You pay 2 points on a $400,000 loan and your payment falls by $100 a month. What is the simple break-even, in months?

2 points = 2% × $400,000 = $8,000. $8,000 ÷ $100 = 80 months, about 6.7 years.
Exercise 3

You bought a $400,000 home (its appraised value too). Under the Homeowners Protection Act, at what scheduled balance must PMI end automatically, in dollars?

Automatic termination at 78% of original value: 0.78 × $400,000 = $312,000. You may request cancellation earlier, at $320,000 (80%).
Exercise 4

The Riveras can earn 4.08% on Treasury bills or save 3.1% by prepaying. On $41,000, how many more dollars a year (before tax) do the bills earn?

$41,000 × 4.08% = $1,672.80; $41,000 × 3.1% = $1,271.00. Difference ≈ $402 a year, before federal tax on the bill interest, and the cash stays available.
