FIRE Variants

"Financial independence, retire early" is not one plan but a family of them: live lean, live large, work part-time, or save hard early and then let compounding finish the job. Each variant trades a different mix of savings, spending, work and risk. The longer the retirement, the more one risk — the order of returns — dominates.

The question this module answersWhich version of early financial independence fits my life, and what withdrawal rate can a 50-year retirement really support?

Start here

One formula, several ways to live with it

If you worked through INV-072, you already have the core idea: the FI number is annual spending divided by a withdrawal rate, and the savings rate sets how fast you get there. The FIRE movement (financial independence, retire early) grew around that formula, and its members soon found that people wanted different things from it.

Some want out as soon as possible and are happy to live frugally. Some want financial independence without giving up an expensive life. Some want to leave full-time work but keep a part-time job they enjoy. Some just want to stop worrying about retirement saving at 40 and let the money grow. These are the four named variants, and they sit on a spectrum shown in Figure 73.1. None is an official category; the names are community conventions, and the dollar lines between them are informal.

Spending is the variable

Lean FIRE and Fat FIRE

Lean and Fat FIRE use the same formula as INV-072; they differ only in the spending you plug in. That single input changes the target by millions of dollars and the timeline by decades.

Lean FIREfrugal financial independence
What it is
Reaching financial independence on a deliberately modest budget, often well below the typical household's, so the FI number is small and reachable quickly.
Used for
Leaving full-time work as early as possible, often in one's thirties or forties.
What it means for you
A small budget leaves little room to cut in a bad market, and costs that are hard to control (health care, housing, family needs) take a large share of it.
Fat FIREcomfortable financial independence
What it is
Financial independence at a generous spending level, with discretionary spending well above what is strictly needed.
Used for
High earners who want independence without lowering their standard of living.
What it means for you
The target is large and takes longer to reach, but a large share of spending is optional, which is a powerful buffer: you can cut in a crash without hardship.
Lean FIREFat FIRE
Main advantageSmall target; can be reached fast on a modest incomeLarge optional spending cushion
Main riskNo room to cut; one large cost (health, housing, a family need) breaks the budgetTakes longer; the temptation to keep working "one more year"
Flexibility in a crashLow: most spending is essentialHigh: much spending is discretionary
Health insuranceOften depends on marketplace premium credits (INV-074)Often paid at full price
Going furtherThe share of spending you could cut matters as much as the total

Two retirees each spend $60,000. One has $50,000 of essential costs and $10,000 of travel; the other has $35,000 of essentials and $25,000 of travel. In a bad market the second can cut 40% of spending without hardship, the first only 17%. Withdrawal strategies that cut spending after poor returns, such as guardrails (INV-076), support higher starting rates only if the cuts are actually possible. A Lean FIRE budget has little to cut, so it should be paired with a lower withdrawal rate or a source of part-time income.

Practitioner depthGeography as a spending lever

Some FI planners move to lower-cost areas, a strategy the community calls geographic arbitrage. It changes housing, taxes and often health-insurance prices at once. State income tax treatment of retirement income varies widely (INV-041), and marketplace premiums vary by rating area (INV-074), so a move should be modeled on after-tax, after-premium spending, not rent alone.

Let time finish the job

Coast FIRE: saving hard early, then stopping

Coast FIRE starts from a simple observation about compounding (INV-002): money saved in your twenties has decades to grow. If you save enough early, you can stop contributing for retirement altogether and let growth carry the balance to your FI number by a traditional retirement age. You still work to pay today's bills; you just no longer need to save for later.

Coast FIa milestone, not a retirement
What it is
Having invested enough that, with no further contributions, expected growth alone should reach your FI number by a chosen age.
Used for
Deciding when it is safe to take a lower-paying job, work fewer hours, or redirect savings to other goals.
What it means for you
Coasting relies on a return assumption over 20 to 40 years. It is a reasonable planning milestone, not a guarantee; keep checking it as markets move.
Coast number today = FI number ÷ (1 + r)years until FI age

Worked example. Maya is 58 and plans to work to about 67. Suppose she will spend in retirement about what her essentials cost today, $5,200 a month or $62,400 a year, plus about $3,500 a year of federal income tax on her withdrawals, and that Social Security pays her estimated $2,300 a month, or $27,600 a year (all three are assumptions). Her portfolio must then supply $62,400 + $3,500 − $27,600 = $38,300 a year from 67. At 4%, the FI number is $38,300 ÷ 0.04 = $957,500. With a 5% real return and 9 years to go, the coast number today is $957,500 ÷ 1.059 = $617,213. Maya has $370,000 invested (her other $30,000 is cash, kept as her emergency reserve), so she is $247,213 short: growth alone would take $370,000 to about $573,991 by 67, and would not reach $957,500 until about age 77.5. To reach the target by 67, she needs to keep saving about $34,780 a year (the gap of $383,509 divided by the 9-year future-value factor of 11.03). Her 12% contribution plus the 4% match is $14,720 a year, enough for about $736,302 at 67, 77% of the target. Adding the full 2026 catch-up schedule for savers 50 and older, $8,000 a year but $11,250 at ages 60 to 63 (2028 to 2031), brings her to about $840,731, 88%. Working longer closes the rest: with the catch-ups she passes the target at 69, with about $973,482; on her current saving alone, at 71, with about $958,425, before counting the larger Social Security benefit that waiting would bring. And her plan must also leave money for Eli, which this FI number does not include.

Going furtherCoasting is very sensitive to the return

Because the coast number divides by a large power, a small change in the return can have a big effect. For Maya, with 9 years to go, a 4% real return instead of 5% raises the coast number from $617,213 to $672,727, a 9% increase. For a 24-year-old with 41 years to go, the same one-point change raises it by about 48%. A short horizon makes the coast number less sensitive to returns, but it leaves little time to make up a shortfall. Figure 72.4 in INV-072 showed that 17-year real returns have ranged from below zero to nearly 13%. A prudent coaster uses a conservative return and rechecks every few years.

Practitioner depthCoast FI and Social Security together

The spending the portfolio must cover should be net of expected Social Security, which is why Maya's example uses $38,300 rather than total spending. But coasting changes the Social Security estimate itself: benefits are based on up to 35 years of indexed earnings, so years of lower pay while coasting reduce the benefit somewhat. Download your record at my Social Security and rerun both numbers together; INV-078 explains the benefit formula.

Part-time work, part-time portfolio

Barista FIRE: let a smaller job pay part of the bill

The name comes from the idea of leaving a career for a part-time job, such as working in a coffee shop, that pays some bills and, ideally, offers health insurance. The job need not be a coffee shop: consulting, seasonal work or a small business all fit. What matters is that the portfolio only has to cover the difference.

Barista FIpartial financial independence
What it is
A portfolio large enough to cover spending minus the income from part-time work.
Used for
Leaving full-time work years before full FI, while keeping some earned income and possibly employer benefits.
What it means for you
Every $1,000 of reliable part-time income reduces the FI number by $25,000 at 4%. Part-time income also lets you withdraw less after a crash, which directly reduces sequence risk.
Barista FI number = (annual spending − part-time income) ÷ withdrawal rate

Health insurance is often the deciding factor. Before Medicare begins at 65, an early retiree must buy coverage. A part-time job with benefits, a spouse's plan, COBRA continuation (under the Consolidated Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act) of a former employer's plan (usually up to 18 months after leaving a job, at up to 102% of the plan's full cost), or a marketplace plan are the options. For 2026, marketplace premium credits end entirely above 400% of the federal poverty line, which for a household of two is $84,600 of income. INV-074 covers the rules and has a calculator.

Going furtherPart-time income as sequence-risk insurance

The most dangerous years for any retiree are the first few, because a market fall combined with withdrawals can shrink the portfolio beyond recovery. Earning even $15,000 a year during those years means selling less at depressed prices. Many FI planners therefore treat the first five to ten years of early retirement as a "flexible" phase in which some work income is expected, then rely more fully on the portfolio later, when the remaining horizon is shorter.

Practitioner depthEarnings, taxes and credits interact

Part-time earnings raise your modified adjusted gross income, which can reduce marketplace premium credits dollar for dollar in some ranges and eliminate them above 400% of the poverty line. They also restart payroll taxes and can add years to your Social Security record. Before age 62 there is no Social Security earnings test to worry about; if you claim benefits before full retirement age while still working, the earnings test applies (INV-078).

The early retiree's biggest risk

Why 50-year retirements need lower withdrawal rates

The 4% rule was built for a 30-year retirement. An early retiree at 40 or 45 may need the money for 50 years or more, and a longer horizon changes the answer in two ways. There are more years in which a bad stretch can occur, and there is less chance of simply running out of years before running out of money.

Sequence-of-returns riskthe order matters
What it is
The danger that poor returns early in retirement, while withdrawals are being taken, permanently reduce how long a portfolio lasts, even if average returns over the whole period are normal.
Used for
Explaining why two retirees with the same average return can have completely different outcomes.
What it means for you
You cannot choose your sequence, so you plan for a bad one: a lower withdrawal rate, spending you can cut, some work income, or safe assets for the first years.

What the data say. Stretching the horizon from 30 to 60 years lowered the historical worst-case rate by about half a percentage point, from roughly 4% to roughly 3.5%, and withdrawing at the start of each year (money in hand before the year begins) cost about another third of a point. This agrees with Bengen's 1994 finding that rates up to about 3.5% lasted at least 50 years in every case he tested. A popular blog series by Early Retirement Now, a secondary source, reaches similar conclusions for 50- and 60-year horizons.

Going furtherThe US record may be flattering

The United States had one of the best-performing stock markets of the 20th century. A 2025 study in the Journal of Pension Economics and Finance by Aizhan Anarkulova, Scott Cederburg, Michael O'Doherty and Richard Sias used asset returns from 38 developed countries and concluded that a 65-year-old couple willing to accept a 5% chance of financial ruin could withdraw only about 2.31% a year, "materially lower than conventional advice." Their method differs from Bengen's and they model lifetimes rather than fixed horizons, but the direction is clear: outside the US record, safe rates have often been lower, and early retirees face longer horizons than a 65-year-old couple.

Practitioner depthReading historical tests correctly

Overlapping windows are not independent: 98 years of data contain fewer than two independent 50-year periods, so "80% of start years succeeded" is not an 80% probability. Results also depend on the bond series (Bengen used intermediate-term Treasuries; this tool uses 10-year Treasuries), withdrawal timing, rebalancing and fees. Fees matter a great deal: in the same data, deducting a 1% annual fee lowers the 50-year worst-case rate for a 75/25 mix with year-end withdrawals from 3.71% to 3.14%. Treat historical tests as stress tests. Monte Carlo methods (INV-077) and flexible spending rules (INV-076) complement them. Babak's 4% Rule vs BBD Analyzer compares the classic rule with a buy-borrow-die (BBD), borrowing-based strategy under Monte Carlo, including the margin-call risk that strategy adds.

What the spreadsheet leaves out

Criticisms and lifestyle realities

FIRE's critics are not all wrong, and its practitioners are the first to admit the plans have soft spots. The main criticisms fall into two groups: financial assumptions that may not hold, and human realities that no formula captures.

  1. "The math assumes the future looks like the past." Fair. The response is to use a lower withdrawal rate than 30-year studies suggest, to keep some spending flexible, and to recheck the plan as markets move.
  2. "Health care will wreck the budget." Before 65, premiums can run to many thousands of dollars a year per person. In 2026 the enhanced marketplace credits of 2021–2025 have expired, restoring a cliff at 400% of the poverty line. This is the most important cost to price before leaving work.
  3. "Most of your money is locked up until 59½." Partly true, and solvable with planning: the Rule of 55, 72(t) payments, Roth contributions and conversion ladders, and taxable accounts (INV-074).
  4. "Life changes." Children arrive, parents need care, marriages change. A plan built on one budget can break when the household changes. Build margin, and revisit the plan after every major life event (INV-058).
  5. "People need work." The adjustment is not only financial: work supplies structure, status and friendships, and leaving it means replacing them deliberately. A clear answer to "retire to what?" matters.
  6. "It is only for high earners." A high savings rate is easier on a high income, but the math is income-neutral (INV-072). Lean and Barista paths exist precisely for moderate incomes.
Going further"One more year" and the fear of stopping

The opposite failure is common too: reaching the number and continuing to work out of anxiety, each year promising "one more." A written plan with a defined target, a withdrawal rate chosen with a margin, and a list of what would make you cut spending is the practical antidote. INV-028 shows how to write an investment policy statement that covers exactly this.

Practitioner depthSurvivorship in the stories you read

Public FIRE stories come disproportionately from people for whom it worked, often during one of the strongest bull markets on record (2009 onward). Those who returned to work after a bad sequence or a health shock rarely write about it. Treat anecdotes as illustrations, not evidence; the evidence is in the historical and international studies in the previous tab.

Evidence on long horizons

Three pieces of evidence

Case study 1Bengen's 50-year charts, 1994
What happened
In the paper that launched the 4% rule, Bengen charted how long portfolios lasted for every start year, capping the charts at 50 years. He wrote that a 3% first-year withdrawal was "absolutely safe" (to the extent history is a guide), lasting at least 50 years in every case, and that the same was true for rates up to about 3.5%. At 4%, the shortest-lived portfolio lasted about 33 years.
What it teaches
The original research already contained the early retiree's answer: over 50 years, about 3.5%, not 4%.
Source
Bengen, W. P. (1994, October). Determining withdrawal rates using historical data. Journal of Financial Planning (reprinted March 2004).
Case study 2The 1966 early retiree, computed from 98 years of data
What happened
Across every start year from 1928, the worst time to begin a long retirement was 1966, just before a decade of high inflation and poor real returns. For a 75/25 portfolio with year-end withdrawals, the highest rate that lasted 60 years from 1966 was 3.63%; with withdrawals at the start of each year, 3.32%.
What it teaches
The worst case in the US record was an inflation shock, not a crash. Early retirees should think about inflation protection (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities, TIPS, INV-010) as well as about stock-market drops.
Source
Computed from Damodaran, A. (2026), Historical returns on stocks, bonds and bills: 1928–2025, NYU Stern.
Case study 338 countries, 2025
What happened
Anarkulova, Cederburg, O'Doherty and Sias assembled asset-class returns for 38 developed countries and tested retirement spending rules against them. For a 65-year-old couple accepting a 5% chance of ruin, the sustainable rate was 2.31% a year.
What it teaches
A plan built only on US history rests on the experience of one unusually successful market. Diversifying internationally (INV-054) and keeping spending flexible are the practical responses.
Source
Anarkulova, A., Cederburg, S., O'Doherty, M. S., & Sias, R. (2025). The safe withdrawal rate: Evidence from a broad sample of developed markets. Journal of Pension Economics and Finance, 24(3), 464–500.

Decisions

Pick a path

Scenario 1 · Maya

Maya (58) has read about Coast FIRE and wonders whether she has saved enough to stop retirement saving and put the $14,720 a year toward Eli's future instead. What should she do?

Stop savingMaya's coast number is about $617,200; she has $370,000 invested. Growth alone would reach only about $574,000 by 67, and $957,500 not until about 77. A parent who runs short in her seventies becomes one more risk to the son she wants to protect.
Keep saving; plan for Eli with structureRight. Her own retirement is Eli's first safety net, and the 4% match is free money that should never be skipped. What Eli needs most from her now costs far less than $14,720 a year: a will naming a guardian, a third-party special needs trust named as beneficiary instead of Eli, and a 20-year term life policy payable to the trust (INV-084, INV-087, INV-102).
Later for EliRight about the saving, risky about the timing. Until the documents exist, money left to Eli directly could cost him SSI and Medicaid, and no guardian is named. The paperwork costs little and cannot wait for retirement.
The principle: coasting is a number you reach, not a feeling you have. Check it with the calculator.
Scenario 2 · Denise

Denise (24) is excited about Lean FIRE: she wants to live on $30,000 a year and retire at 40. What is the most important thing she is likely missing?

The simple target$750,000 is 25 times spending, which is a 30-year rule. A retirement from 40 could last 50 years or more.
A marginRight. At 3.5% the same budget needs about $857,000; at 3.25%, about $923,000. A Coast FI milestone by her mid-thirties would keep her options open without betting everything on one budget at 24.
The principle: the leaner the budget and the longer the horizon, the lower the withdrawal rate should be.
Scenario 3 · The Harpers

Tom (57) is offered consulting work at about $40,000 a year if he leaves his job now instead of at 62. Rachel (55) would keep working for now. Is this a reasonable Barista-style move?

Yes, with the homeworkRight. With Rachel's job (and perhaps her employer's health plan) plus $40,000 of consulting, their portfolio may not need to supply anything for several years, which sharply reduces sequence risk. If Tom leaves his employer in or after the year he turns 55, the Rule of 55 lets him draw from that employer's 401(k) without the 10% extra tax (INV-074).
Always too costlyNot necessarily. The cost depends on health coverage, on whether they would draw on the portfolio at all, and on their Social Security claiming plan.
The principle: part-time income in the first years of retirement is the cheapest protection against a bad sequence.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

Your FI number is $900,000 and your FI age is 25 years away. At a 5% real return, what is your coast number today, in dollars?

1.0525 = 3.3864, so $900,000 ÷ 3.3864 = $265,772.
Exercise 2

You spend $55,000 a year and expect $22,000 a year from part-time work. What is your Barista FI number at a 4% withdrawal rate?

($55,000 − $22,000) ÷ 0.04 = $33,000 × 25 = $825,000, versus $1,375,000 without the job.
Exercise 3

A Lean FIRE budget is $36,000 a year and the horizon is 50 years, so you plan with 3.5%. What is the FI number?

$36,000 ÷ 0.035 = $1,028,571, about $128,571 more than 25 × spending.
Exercise 4

You have $200,000 and an FI number of $1,000,000. With no more saving and a 5% real return, about how many years until you get there?

Solve 200,000 × 1.05n = 1,000,000: n = ln(5) ÷ ln(1.05) = 1.6094 ÷ 0.04879 = 33.0 years.
