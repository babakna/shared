The Cost of Investing

Returns are uncertain; costs are not. Every fee comes out of your balance every year, whether markets rise or fall, and the money it takes can never compound again. This module names every kind of cost, measures what each does over decades, and shows how to audit your own.

The question this module answersWhat am I actually paying to invest, and is it worth it?

Start here

The bill you never see

When you buy groceries, you see the price and pay it. Investment costs almost never work that way. Most are taken quietly out of the fund or the account, so the only sign of them is a balance that grows a little more slowly than it otherwise would.

"A little" is the trap. A fee of 1% a year sounds tiny next to a return of 7%. But it is charged on your whole balance, every year, for decades. The U.S. Department of Labor uses this example: a $25,000 balance, 35 years to retirement, no further contributions, 7% average returns. With 0.5% of fees, it grows to about $227,000. With 1.5% of fees, about $163,000 — 28% less, from a one-point difference.

The most common cost

What funds charge today

Expense ratio
What it is
The percentage of a fund's assets deducted each year to pay for management, administration and distribution. A 0.40% expense ratio costs $40 a year per $10,000 invested.
Used for
Comparing the ongoing cost of funds; it is already reflected in the fund's reported returns.
What it means for you
You never write a check for it, so it is easy to ignore. It is also the cost you have the most control over: the same kind of index fund can cost several times as much at one provider as at another.

The Investment Company Institute also reports that the asset-weighted average expense ratio of equity mutual funds fell 62% from 1996 to 2025, from 1.04% to 0.40%. It attributes the long decline mainly to a shift toward no-load funds: in 2025, 92% of gross sales of long-term mutual funds went to no-load funds without 12b-1 fees, compared with 46% in 2000. Lower average costs are one of the quiet successes of the past three decades for ordinary investors.

Going furtherReading a prospectus fee table

Every mutual fund and ETF prospectus opens with a standardized fee table: "Shareholder fees" (paid directly: loads, redemption fees) and "Annual fund operating expenses" (management fee, 12b-1 fee, other expenses, total). Below it is an example showing the cost of $10,000 over 1, 3, 5 and 10 years at an assumed 5% return. That example lets you compare funds in dollars rather than percentages.

Measure it

See your fees compound against you

Set Fee A to what a broad index fund might cost and Fee B to what a higher-cost option charges — an actively managed fund, or index funds plus an advisory fee. The gap between the lines is money that is never yours to spend.

With the default inputs — $100,000 now, $6,000 a year, 7% before fees, 30 years — a 0.05% fee ends at about $1.35 million and a 1.25% fee at about $994,000. The difference, about $356,000, is more than twice the $169,000 of fees actually deducted, because each dollar taken also lost its future growth. The 1.25% fee consumed about 27% of what the portfolio would have been worth with no fees at all.

Practitioner depthHow the calculator charges fees

Each year: add the contribution, apply the return, then deduct the fee as a percentage of the year-end balance: Bt = (Bt−1 + C)(1 + r)(1 − f). Real funds deduct fees daily, which gives almost the same result. A handy approximation: over n years, a fee f multiplies ending wealth by roughly (1 − f)n (exactly, for a single lump sum) — a reduction of about 26% for 1% over 30 years.

The evidence

Low cost is one of the few reliable predictors

Past performance is a poor guide to which funds will do well next; winners rarely stay winners. Cost is different. Morningstar's researchers sorted funds within each category by expense ratio and followed them. In US stock funds over 2010–2015, 62% of the cheapest fifth of funds survived and beat their category average, against 20% of the most expensive fifth.

The arithmetic of active managementWilliam Sharpe, 1991
What it is
Before costs, the average actively managed dollar must earn the market return, because together all investors are the market. After costs, the average active dollar must therefore trail the average passive (index) dollar by the difference in costs.
Used for
Explaining why low-cost index funds beat most active funds over long periods, without assuming anyone is foolish.
What it means for you
Paying more does not buy a higher expected return on average; it buys a chance of beating the market and a larger chance of trailing it. INV-043 and INV-045 examine the evidence in depth.

Advisors

What advice costs, and when it is worth it

Financial advice can be valuable: a good advisor can help with taxes, insurance, estate planning, retirement income and, above all, behavior in a crisis. It can also be expensive, and the fee structure changes both the cost and the advisor's incentives.

Read the fine print

Loads, 12b-1 fees, cash drag and tax drag

Sales load
What it is
A sales charge on some mutual funds, taken when you buy (front-end, "Class A" shares) or when you sell within a set period (back-end or deferred). Loads usually decline at larger purchase amounts, called breakpoints.
Used for
Compensating the broker who sold the fund.
What it means for you
A 5.75% front-end load on $10,000 means $575 never gets invested. After 20 years at 7%, that is about $36,500 instead of $38,700. Many equivalent no-load funds exist.
12b-1 fee
What it is
An annual marketing and distribution fee included in some funds' expense ratios. FINRA rules cap the asset-based sales charge at 0.75% a year and the separate service fee at 0.25%.
What it means for you
You pay it every year you hold the fund. Many low-cost funds charge none.

Tax drag deserves a special mention. In a taxable account, a fund that trades a lot distributes capital gains each year, and you owe tax on them even if you reinvest. Index funds, and ETFs in particular, tend to distribute fewer gains. Holding tax-inefficient investments inside tax-advantaged accounts is the subject of INV-036 on asset location.

Decisions

Run a fee audit

Scenario 1 · The Harpers' advisor

The Harpers pay an advisor 1.25% a year on $1.1 million — $13,750 this year — for investment management. They meet once a year, and the portfolio is mostly index funds. What should they consider?

Accept itAt 1.25% on a growing balance, the fee will likely exceed $20,000 a year within a decade. Over 20 years the gap versus a 0.30% all-in cost is roughly $580,000 in this illustration (Figure 7.7).
Evaluate what the fee buysRight. Five to ten years from retirement, the Harpers face exactly the decisions where good advice can pay for itself: Roth conversions, withdrawal order, Social Security timing. The question is whether they are getting that, and at what price.
Go fully do-it-yourselfPossible, and many people do it well. But self-management only saves money if they actually do the tax and retirement-income planning themselves, and hold steady in a crash.
The principle: pay for advice you use, at a price you understand.
Scenario 2 · Ruth's annuity pitch

A salesperson suggests Ruth move $300,000 of her individual retirement account (IRA) into a variable annuity with total annual charges of about 2.5%, offering "market upside with protection." What questions should she ask?

Protection at any priceAt 5% before fees for 20 years, $300,000 becomes about $480,000 at 2.5% of annual charges, versus about $765,000 at 0.2%. The guarantee has to be worth that difference.
Ask the questionsRight. Some annuities, especially simple income annuities, can be very useful in retirement (INV-082). But complex variable annuities often combine high ongoing fees, surrender charges lasting years, and generous sales commissions. An IRA is already tax-deferred, so the annuity's tax deferral adds nothing there.
The principle: the more complex the product, the more important it is to know every fee and who is paid.

Exercise 1

A $400,000 portfolio is in funds averaging a 0.75% expense ratio. What does that cost per year, in dollars?

$400,000 × 0.0075 = $3,000 a year. In 0.05% index funds, it would be $200.
Exercise 2

You invest $10,000 in a fund with a 5.75% front-end load. How many dollars go to the sales charge?

$10,000 × 0.0575 = $575; only $9,425 is invested.
