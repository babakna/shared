Caring for Aging Parents

Nearly one in four American adults provides ongoing care to someone. For most, it starts with a parent: a fall, a diagnosis, a missed bill. This module covers the conversation to have early, the legal documents that let you help, what care really costs and who pays for it, the 2026 tax rules for supporting a parent, and how to protect a parent from the fraud that targets older adults.

The question this module answersHow do I help my parents with money and care without putting my own finances, or theirs, at risk?

Start here

When the roles begin to reverse

Most parents manage perfectly well into their eighties, and most adult children never need to take over anything. That is the ordinary case. But almost every family eventually meets a moment when a parent needs more help than before, and families who have talked and prepared handle it far better than those who scramble.

You would not be alone. A 2025 national survey by AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving found that 63 million Americans — nearly 1 in 4 adults — provided ongoing care to an adult or to a child with a complex medical condition or disability in the past year, nearly 20 million more than in 2015. Six in ten caregivers also held a job, caregivers spent roughly $7,200 a year of their own money on caregiving, and nearly half had experienced at least one major financial impact, such as taking on debt or stopping saving.

Before a crisis

Starting the conversation

The hardest part is usually the first sentence. Parents may fear losing independence; children may fear seeming greedy. Framing it around your parent's wishes, not their money, helps: "If something happened, I'd want to do what you'd want. Can you tell me what that is, and where I'd find things?"

Capacity
What it is
The legal ability to understand a decision and its consequences well enough to make it, such as signing a power of attorney or a will. The exact test is set by state law.
Used for
Deciding whether a person can still sign legal documents. If a parent loses capacity without having signed them, the family usually has to ask a court to appoint a guardian or conservator.
What it means for you
The window to prepare can close suddenly, for example after a stroke. Guardianship is public, slower and more expensive than a power of attorney signed years earlier.

Siblings and fairness

Care rarely falls evenly. Often one child lives closest and does the most. Families who agree early who does what — one handles bills, one medical appointments, others contribute money or respite visits — avoid resentment. If a child is paid for caregiving, a written caregiver agreement at a fair hourly rate keeps it transparent for siblings and can matter later if Medicaid reviews past transfers (Tab 5).

Going furtherHelping without taking over

Many first steps preserve a parent's control: setting up automatic bill payment, adding "view-only" access to accounts, arranging for duplicate statements to come to you, or simplifying a dozen accounts into a few. Each is reversible, and each makes unusual activity easier to spot. Adding a child as a joint owner of a bank account is different: it gives the child legal ownership, can expose the account to the child's creditors, and can override the parent's will for that account. Ask a lawyer before doing it.

The right to help

Powers of attorney and the other ways to act for a parent

Banks, doctors and government agencies generally cannot deal with you on your parent's behalf just because you are family. You need legal authority, and each kind covers different ground.

Durable financial power of attorney
What it is
A document in which your parent (the principal) names you as agent to handle money and property. "Durable" means it keeps working if the parent becomes incapacitated, which is the point. See power of attorney.
Used for
Paying bills, managing accounts, handling taxes and dealing with insurers when the parent cannot or prefers not to.
What it means for you
As agent you become a fiduciary. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) guide for agents lists four basic duties: act only in the person's best interest; manage their money and property carefully; keep their money and property separate from yours; and keep good records.
Health care directiveadvance directive, living will, health care proxy
What it is
Documents that record a parent's wishes about medical treatment and name someone to make health decisions if they cannot. See health care directive.
Used for
Guiding doctors and family in serious illness, and avoiding disputes among siblings.
What it means for you
Keep copies with the parent's doctors and hospital records, not only in a drawer.
Practitioner depthWhat an agent may and may not do

The CFPB agent guide warns agents to avoid conflicts of interest, to be very careful with gifts to themselves or others, and not to pay themselves for their time unless the document or state law allows it and the fee is reasonable and documented. Many banks and brokerages have their own power-of-attorney forms or review procedures; presenting the document before it is urgently needed avoids delays. Because state laws differ, a document signed in one state may need review if a parent moves.

Budgeting for care

What care costs, and what Medicare will not pay

Long-term care means help with everyday activities — bathing, dressing, eating, moving around — over months or years, rather than medical treatment. It is expensive, and many families assume Medicare covers it. It does not.

⚠️

Medicare does not pay for long-term custodial care. In Medicare.gov's words, Medicare and most health insurance, including Medigap, "don't pay for long-term care services, including care in a nursing home or in the community." Medicare does cover short stays for skilled nursing or rehabilitation after a qualifying hospital stay, with a 2026 coinsurance of $217 a day for days 21 through 100 (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, CMS).

With the default inputs — 30 hours a week of paid help at home, $250,000 of savings and $24,000 a year of income available for care, prices rising 3% a year — care costs $54,600 in the first year and savings last about 7.0 years. In a private nursing home room, the same savings last about 2.3 years.

Going furtherPaying for care: the usual sources

Families usually combine several sources: the parent's income and savings; the value of the home, through a sale, a reverse mortgage or renting it out; a long-term care insurance policy if one exists (INV-083); veterans' benefits for eligible veterans and surviving spouses; family contributions; and, once savings are spent down, Medicaid. For 2026 the tax code lets people who itemize count long-term care insurance premiums as medical expenses up to an age-based limit, from $500 a year at 40 or younger to $6,200 above age 70 (Rev. Proc. 2025-32).

The payer of last resort

Medicaid and long-term care

Medicaid
What it is
A joint federal-state program that pays for health care for people with limited income and assets. See Medicaid. Unlike Medicare, it covers long-term nursing home care and, in many states, some care at home, for people who qualify.
Used for
Paying for long-term care once a person's own resources are largely used up. Each state sets its income and asset limits within federal rules.
What it means for you
Medicaid planning is state-specific and technical. Its rules decide whether a parent's home can be kept, how much a healthy spouse can keep, and whether past gifts delay coverage. See an elder law attorney in your parent's state before moving any assets.

The five-year look-back

To stop people from giving everything away to qualify, federal law requires states to review transfers made for less than fair market value in the 60 months before a person applies for Medicaid long-term care. If assets were given away in that window, the person is ineligible for a penalty period (42 U.S.C. 1396p(c)).

Protections for a spouse who stays at home

When one spouse needs nursing home care, federal "spousal impoverishment" rules let the other spouse keep a share of the couple's assets and income. For 2026, the community spouse resource allowance is at least $32,532 and at most $162,660, depending on the state; the minimum monthly maintenance needs allowance is $2,705 from July 1, 2026 in every state except Alaska and Hawaii, which are higher, and the maximum $4,066.50. A home generally does not count against eligibility if the equity is below the state's limit, which must be between $752,000 and $1,130,000 for 2026 (CMS, 2026 SSI and Spousal Impoverishment Standards).

Practitioner depthEstate recovery and the caregiver child

States must seek repayment from the estates of people who received Medicaid long-term care services at age 55 or older (42 U.S.C. 1396p(b)). That often means the home, after the death of a surviving spouse. Federal law limits liens in some cases: a state cannot place a lien on the home while a son or daughter lives there who resided in the home for at least two years before the parent's admission and provided care that let the parent stay at home rather than in an institution. The look-back rule dates from the Deficit Reduction Act of 2005, which extended the window from 36 to 60 months for transfers made on or after February 8, 2006.

Tax year 2026

The tax rules for supporting a parent

If you help pay for a parent's living costs or care, several tax rules may apply. The most important is whether your parent counts as your dependent — your qualifying relative.

Qualifying relativethe test that decides whether a parent is your dependent
What it is
A person who is related to you (a parent qualifies even without living with you), whose gross income for 2026 is under $5,300, and for whom you provide more than half of total support (26 U.S.C. 152(d); Rev. Proc. 2025-32).
Used for
Claiming the $500 credit for other dependents, head-of-household status for single filers, and deducting medical costs you pay for the parent.
What it means for you
Tax-exempt income, such as Social Security benefits that are not taxable, does not count toward the $5,300 (IRS Publication 501), so many parents living mainly on Social Security pass the income test. The support test is the harder one: add everything spent on the parent from every source, including the parent's own money.
  • Head of household. An unmarried adult child can file as head of household if the parent is their dependent and they pay more than half the cost of keeping up the parent's main home for the year — the parent does not have to live with them, and paying more than half the cost of a care facility counts (IRS Publication 501). For 2026 that raises the standard deduction from $16,100 to $24,150.
  • Medical expenses. You can deduct medical costs you pay for a parent you support if you itemize and your total medical costs exceed 7.5% of your adjusted gross income. For this purpose the parent counts as your dependent even if their gross income is above $5,300 (26 U.S.C. 213(a)).
  • Multiple support agreement. If siblings together provide more than half of a parent's support but none provides more than half alone, one who contributed more than 10% can claim the parent if the others who contributed over 10% sign a declaration on Form 2120 (26 U.S.C. 152(d)(3)).
  • Care credit and FSA. Paying for adult day care or a home aide so that you can work can qualify for the child and dependent care credit or a dependent care flexible spending account (FSA) — but only if the parent is physically or mentally unable to care for themselves and lives with you for more than half the year (26 U.S.C. 21(b)). The calculator in INV-096 compares the two.
Going furtherThe parent's own tax return

A parent 65 or older adds $2,050 to a single filer's standard deduction for 2026 ($1,650 per spouse if married), and from 2025 through 2028 can also claim the new senior deduction of up to $6,000 per person, reduced by 6% of modified adjusted gross income above $75,000 ($150,000 joint) (Rev. Proc. 2025-32; P.L. 119-21). A single parent over 65 with only $20,000 of Social Security and a little interest typically owes no federal income tax at all.

Protecting what they built

Elder financial exploitation: the risk and the defenses

Older adults are targeted because they often have savings, home equity and good credit, and because cognitive decline can make scams harder to spot. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) received 201,266 complaints from people aged 60 and over in 2025, reporting $7.748 billion in losses — 59% more than in 2024 — with an average loss of $38,500, and 12,444 complainants who lost more than $100,000.

Trusted contact person
What it is
Someone age 18 or older whom a brokerage firm may contact about your parent's account. Under Rule 4512 of the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA), firms must make reasonable efforts to ask for one when opening or updating an account.
Used for
Letting the firm reach a family member to address possible financial exploitation, to confirm the customer's contact information or health status, or to identify a guardian, executor, trustee or power-of-attorney holder.
What it means for you
It gives no authority to trade or withdraw. Under FINRA Rule 2165, a firm that reasonably believes exploitation is happening may place a temporary hold on a disbursement for customers 65 and older and must notify the trusted contact; the initial hold lasts up to 15 business days and can be extended in limited cases.

Warning signs

The CFPB's guide for agents lists common signs: money or property that seems to be missing; sudden changes in spending or savings, such as large unexplained withdrawals, attempts to wire large sums, or unpaid bills that were always paid; unfamiliar names added to accounts; new or unusual gifts to a "new best friend"; changed beneficiaries; a caregiver or relative who suddenly begins handling money; and a parent who seems afraid of someone or is kept from visitors and calls.

Five defenses

  1. Name a trusted contact on every brokerage and retirement account.
  2. Simplify and watch. Fewer accounts, automatic bill payment and duplicate statements make oddities visible.
  3. Slow down money that leaves. Ask banks about transfer limits and alerts; any request for gift cards, wires or cryptocurrency from a caller is a red flag.
  4. Freeze credit at the three credit bureaus if the parent is not planning to borrow.
  5. Know where to report. Adult protective services and local police; the FBI's IC3 for online fraud; the Eldercare Locator (1-800-677-1116), run by the Administration for Community Living, can find local help.

Real data, real law

Three stories behind the rules

Case study 1The rising cost of elder fraud, 2025
What happened
People 60 and older filed 201,266 complaints with the FBI's IC3 in 2025, 37% more than in 2024, reporting $7.748 billion in losses. Investment schemes caused the largest losses, $3.52 billion; 42,271 older complainants reported schemes involving cryptocurrency, with $4.35 billion lost.
What it teaches
The biggest losses come from scams that look like investing — a friendly contact, a trading app, early "profits" — not from obvious theft. Families should talk openly about any unsolicited investment opportunity, especially one involving cryptocurrency.
Source
Federal Bureau of Investigation. (2026). 2025 IC3 annual report, pp. 44–46.
Case study 2From three years to five: the 2006 look-back change
What happened
Medicaid's look-back for gifts used to be 36 months. Federal law now sets 60 months for any disposal of assets made on or after February 8, 2006, and makes the penalty start when the applicant is otherwise eligible, rather than when the gift was made.
What it teaches
Last-minute gifting to qualify for Medicaid usually backfires. Planning, if any, has to start more than five years ahead, and must be done with an elder law attorney.
Source
42 U.S.C. 1396p(c)(1)(B) and (D).
Case study 3Twenty million more caregivers in ten years
What happened
AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving estimated 63 million family caregivers in 2025, a 45% increase, or nearly 20 million more, over the decade. Caregivers averaged 51 years old, three in five were women, 29% cared for both children and adults, and more than 40% provided high-intensity care.
What it teaches
Caregiving often lands in peak earning and saving years, alongside children. Planning for it belongs in the same conversation as retirement saving and college.
Source
AARP and National Alliance for Caregiving. (2025). Caregiving in the U.S. 2025; AARP press release, July 24, 2025.

Decisions

Three families, three caregiving decisions

Scenario 1 · The Harpers

Suppose Rachel's widowed mother, 84, lives alone near Raleigh on $20,400 a year of Social Security and has had two falls. The Harpers are considering paying for 20 hours a week of help at home. What should they do first?

Help now, paperwork laterSafety first is understandable, but falls are exactly the kind of event after which capacity can change. The documents take days; a guardianship takes months.
Documents, then careRight. With care added, the Harpers would provide about 72% of her support. If her taxable income stays under $5,300, she becomes their dependent: a $500 credit, and they can count the medical costs they pay if they itemize.
Nursing homeAt a 2025 median of $129,575 a year for a private room, it would exhaust most families' resources quickly, and most people prefer to stay home. It is right only when the care needed exceeds what home care can safely provide.
The principle: authority first, then care; keep records of who pays for what.
Scenario 2 · Jordan

Suppose Jordan's father in Columbus needs daily help, and Jordan is thinking of cutting back from full-time work to care for him, while also raising Ava. What should Jordan weigh?

Cut backDeeply caring, and sometimes the right answer. But reducing an $88,000 salary by half costs about $44,000 a year before tax, plus 401(k) contributions and match, plus Social Security credits, and Jordan is the household's only earner.
Price the optionsRight. Adult day health care had a 2025 median of $24,700 a year, far less than lost pay. If the father moves in with Jordan and cannot care for himself, adult day care may also qualify for the dependent care credit or FSA.
The principle: a caregiver's own financial security is part of the care plan.
Scenario 3 · Ruth

Ruth, 68, is healthy but wants to spare Anna the scramble she went through when Walter died. What should she set up?

Joint ownershipIt gives Anna legal ownership now, can expose the money to Anna's creditors, and can override Ruth's estate plan. A power of attorney achieves the same practical access without those risks.
Documents and a trusted contactRight. Cheap, reversible and effective. If a scammer targets Ruth, her brokerage can call Anna and, if needed, place a temporary hold.
WaitThe documents require capacity to sign, and the risk of waiting is exactly the crisis she wants to avoid for Anna.
The principle: the best time to arrange help is when you do not yet need it.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A parent needs 25 hours a week of paid help at home at the 2025 median of $35 an hour. What does a year cost, in dollars?

25 hours × $35 × 52 weeks = $45,500 a year, or about $3,792 a month.
Exercise 2

A parent gave $96,000 to children three years before applying for Medicaid nursing home care. The state's average monthly private-pay cost is $12,000. How many months of penalty apply?

The gift is inside the 60-month look-back. $96,000 ÷ $12,000 = 8 months without Medicaid payment for nursing home care, starting when the parent would otherwise qualify.
Exercise 3

A parent's total support for 2026 is $30,000. To pass the support test on your own, you must provide more than half. Rounded to the next whole dollar, what is the least you must provide, in dollars?

Half of $30,000 is $15,000, and the test requires more than half, so $15,001. Exactly $15,000 fails. Count the parent's own spending on themselves as part of total support.
Exercise 4

Your 76-year-old parent pays $7,800 a year for a qualified long-term care insurance policy and itemizes. For 2026, how much of the premium can count as a medical expense, in dollars?

Rev. Proc. 2025-32 sets the 2026 limit at $6,200 for people over 70. The remaining $1,600 does not count. Total medical costs still have to exceed 7.5% of adjusted gross income to produce a deduction.
