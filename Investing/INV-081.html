RMDs and Retirement Taxes

Every dollar in a traditional individual retirement account (IRA) or 401(k) carries a tax bill that has only been postponed. Required minimum distributions (RMDs) decide when the government starts collecting. This module explains the rules as they stand in 2026, shows how RMDs interact with Social Security and the tax brackets, and how to manage them years in advance.

The question this module answersHow much must I take out of my retirement accounts, when, and how do I keep the tax on it as low as possible?

Start here

The deal you made when you took the deduction

When you put money into a traditional 401(k) or IRA, you usually skipped income tax on it. The government agreed to wait, not to forget. The rules for required minimum distributions are the terms of that bargain: from a set age, you must take out at least a set share of the account every year and pay income tax on it.

For many retirees RMDs are no burden at all, because they already withdraw more than the minimum to live on. For others — people with large pre-tax balances, other income, or no need for the money — RMDs push income into higher brackets, make more of their Social Security taxable, and can raise Medicare premiums. The difference between those two groups is mostly planning done in the ten years before RMDs start.

What this module covers

  1. When RMDs start: age 73 or 75 under the SECURE 2.0 Act (Setting Every Community Up for Retirement Enhancement), and the first-year deadline.
  2. How to calculate them with the Uniform Lifetime Table, and which accounts can be combined.
  3. What happens if you miss one: the 25% excise tax and the 10% correction rate.
  4. How retirement income is taxed in 2026: brackets, deductions and Social Security.
  5. Managing brackets before and after RMDs start: Roth conversions and qualified charitable distributions (QCDs).
  6. An RMD projector, set up for Ruth Kowalski.

SECURE 2.0

Your RMD age depends on the year you were born

Required minimum distributionRMD
What it is
The smallest amount you must withdraw each year from a traditional IRA, SEP IRA, SIMPLE IRA or workplace retirement plan once you reach the "applicable age" set by law.
Used for
Making sure tax-deferred savings are eventually taxed. You may always withdraw more; you may not withdraw less without penalty.
What it means for you
Your first RMD year is fixed by your birth year. Know it now, because the years just before it are the best time to reshape your tax picture.

The SECURE 2.0 Act of 2022 raised the starting age in two steps. The statute, 26 U.S.C. §401(a)(9)(C)(v), says the applicable age is 73 for anyone who reaches 72 after December 31, 2022 and 73 before January 1, 2033, and 75 for anyone who reaches 74 after December 31, 2032.

The first-year deadline, and the double year

For IRAs, the first RMD is due by April 1 of the year after you reach your applicable age; every later RMD is due by December 31. Waiting until April 1 is allowed but means two taxable RMDs in the same calendar year. The IRS example: John reached 73 on August 20, 2024; his 2024 RMD was due by April 1, 2025, and his 2025 RMD by December 31, 2025.

Going furtherThe still-working exception

If you are still employed at 73 and not a 5% owner of the business, your current employer's plan may let you delay its RMDs until April 1 after the year you retire. The exception does not apply to IRAs, or to plans of former employers. The IRS example: Jodie retires on their 73rd birthday, December 31, 2024; the plan RMD for 2024 is due April 1, 2025, and Jodie's IRA RMD is due on its own schedule. Rolling an old 401(k) into a current employer's plan before 73 can bring that money under the exception, if the plan accepts roll-ins.

The formula

Balance divided by a number from a table

RMD for this year = account balance on December 31 of last year ÷ divisor for your age this year
Uniform Lifetime TableTable III in IRS Publication 590-B
What it is
An IRS table of "applicable denominators" by age, based on the joint life expectancy of the owner and a hypothetical beneficiary ten years younger. It is used by unmarried owners, by married owners whose spouse is not more than 10 years younger, and by owners whose spouse is not the sole beneficiary.
Used for
Finding the divisor for your age as of your birthday this year. A married owner whose sole beneficiary is a spouse more than 10 years younger uses Table II instead, which gives a larger divisor and smaller RMD.
What it means for you
You never need to guess. Look up your age, divide, and you have the minimum. Your IRA custodian usually calculates it for you, but you are responsible if it is wrong.

The IRS's own worked example in Publication 590-B: an owner turns 75 in 2026 with a balance of $100,000 at the end of 2025. The divisor is 24.6, so the 2026 RMD is $100,000 ÷ 24.6 = $4,065. Taking more than the minimum one year does not reduce the next year's RMD; Publication 590-B's example of Justin shows an extra $2,150 withdrawn in 2025 cannot be credited against 2026.

Which accounts can be combined: aggregation

  • IRAs: calculate each IRA's RMD separately, then take the total from any one or more of your IRAs.
  • 403(b)s: the same, among your 403(b) contracts.
  • 401(k)s and 457(b)s: each plan's RMD must come out of that plan.
  • Never across types: an extra IRA withdrawal does not satisfy a 401(k) RMD, and inherited IRAs are calculated and taken separately from your own.

Source: IRS, Retirement plan and IRA required minimum distributions FAQs, Q5.

Practitioner depthAnnuities inside the IRA, and QLACs

If part of an IRA buys an annuity already paying income, the rules let the RMD for the rest of the account take the annuity payments into account (Publication 590-B describes the calculation). A qualifying longevity annuity contract (QLAC), bought within the 2026 premium limit of $210,000 (IRS Notice 2025-67), is excluded from the balance used for RMDs until its income begins, no later than age 85. INV-082 explains QLACs in detail.

Penalties and fixes

What happens if you take too little

If you take less than the required amount by the deadline, the shortfall is subject to an excise tax. SECURE 2.0 cut the rate from 50% to 25% of the shortfall (26 U.S.C. §4974(a)), and to 10% if you correct the mistake within the "correction window" (§4974(e)). The correction window ends at the earliest of: the date the IRS mails a notice of deficiency for the tax, the date the tax is assessed, or the last day of the second taxable year after the year the tax is imposed.

StepWhat to do (IRS Publication 590-B)
1. Take the missed amountWithdraw the shortfall as soon as you notice it.
2. File Form 5329Report the excise tax on "excess accumulations" with your return for the year of the shortfall.
3. Ask for a waiver if the error was reasonableIf the shortfall was due to reasonable error and you are fixing it, you can attach an explanation and request that the tax be waived, following the Form 5329 instructions.
💡

The easy protection. Most IRA custodians will calculate the RMD and send it automatically each year, with tax withheld if you choose. Setting that up, even for part of the year, removes the most common way people miss an RMD: forgetting.

Tax year 2026

How retirement income is taxed

Traditional IRA and 401(k) withdrawals, pensions and interest are ordinary income, taxed through the tax brackets. Qualified Roth withdrawals are tax-free. Social Security is partly taxable depending on your other income. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21) raised the standard deduction and added a temporary deduction for people 65 and older.

Deductions at 65 and over, for 2026
What it is
The standard deduction is $16,100 for a single filer and $32,200 for a married couple filing jointly. Each person 65 or older adds $1,650, or $2,050 if unmarried (Rev. Proc. 2025-32). On top of that, for 2025 through 2028, each person 65 or older may deduct an extra $6,000, reduced by 6% of modified adjusted gross income above $75,000 ($150,000 joint) (IRC §151(d)(5)(C)).
Used for
Lowering taxable income whether or not you itemize. For an unmarried 65-year-old with income under $75,000, the three pieces total $24,150 in 2026.
What it means for you
The $6,000 senior deduction ends after 2028 under current law. For people over 65 now, 2026–2028 are unusually cheap years to take extra taxable income, such as Roth conversions.

How Social Security is taxed: provisional income

Provisional income = other AGI + tax-exempt interest + ½ × benefits

Here "other AGI" means adjusted gross income (AGI) not counting Social Security.

For a single filer, if provisional income is $25,000 or less, no benefits are taxable. Between $25,000 and $34,000, up to 50% of benefits can be taxable; above $34,000, up to 85% ($32,000 and $44,000 for a joint return) (IRS Publication 915). These dollar thresholds are fixed in the statute (26 U.S.C. §86) with no inflation adjustment, so more retirees cross them each year.

The result is a hidden rate. In the range where each extra dollar of IRA income also makes 85 cents of Social Security taxable, a person in the 12% bracket pays 12% × 1.85 = 22.2% on the next dollar, and a person in the 22% bracket pays 22% × 1.85 = 40.7%. Planners call this the tax torpedo.

Going furtherMedicare premiums and state taxes

RMDs also count toward the income that sets Medicare Part B and Part D premiums. For 2026, the standard Part B premium is $202.90 a month; single filers with modified adjusted gross income above $109,000 ($218,000 joint) pay an income-related monthly adjustment amount (IRMAA), starting at a total of $284.10 (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, November 2025). IRMAA is based on the tax return from two years earlier (INV-079). State taxes vary widely: Pennsylvania, for example, does not tax Social Security and generally excludes retirement income taken after retirement age, so Ruth's RMDs are mainly a federal tax question (INV-041).

Plan ahead

Fill low brackets early, give from the IRA, and project

The years between retiring and your first RMD are often the lowest-income years of your adult life. Two tools make the most of them: the Roth conversion and, from 70½, the qualified charitable distribution.

Bracket filling with Roth conversions
What it is
Moving part of a traditional IRA into a Roth IRA each year and paying tax on it now, up to the top of a chosen bracket (INV-038).
Used for
Shrinking future RMDs and paying tax at today's lower rate instead of tomorrow's higher one. Roth IRAs have no RMDs for the owner.
What it means for you
Conversions make sense when today's effective marginal rate is below the rate you expect on RMDs later, including the torpedo and IRMAA effects. Pay the conversion tax from outside the IRA if you can.
Qualified charitable distributionQCD
What it is
A distribution from an IRA paid directly by the custodian to a qualifying charity, made on or after age 70½. It is excluded from income, up to $111,000 per person for 2026, and can include a one-time distribution of up to $55,000 to a split-interest entity such as a charitable gift annuity (IRS Notice 2025-67; 26 U.S.C. §408(d)(8)).
Used for
Giving to charity with pre-tax money. A QCD counts toward your RMD but never enters your adjusted gross income, so it also avoids the torpedo and IRMAA effects that a deduction cannot.
What it means for you
If you give to charity and take the standard deduction, a QCD is usually the cheapest way to give once you are 70½. The money must go straight from the IRA to the charity.

The projector below is set up for Ruth: a $780,000 IRA at the end of 2025, born 1958 (RMDs from 73, in 2031), $34,800 a year of Social Security and $2,400 of certificate of deposit (CD) interest, with a 3% return after inflation, all in today's dollars. With no conversions, her first RMD is about $34,122 in 2031 and her effective marginal rate that year is 22.2%, although her bracket is 12%. By 80 the RMD is about $40,671 and the next dollar is taxed at about 40.7%. Try a $30,000 conversion each year from 2026 to 2030: her projected federal tax from 68 to 95 falls by about $33,920, and by 95 she holds about $323,771 in a Roth IRA.

Practitioner depthWhat the projector assumes

Each year, the RMD is the prior December 31 balance divided by the Uniform Lifetime Table divisor; conversions happen only before the RMD age; QCDs start at 71 (the first full year after 70½ for most people) and count toward the RMD; withdrawals come out at the start of the year and the rest grows at the chosen real return. Federal tax uses 2026 single-filer brackets and deductions for every year, which approximates inflation indexing; the senior deduction applies only through 2028 and only from age 65. It ignores state tax, IRMAA, the taxes heirs would pay on what is left, and any law changes. It is a planning sketch, not a tax return.

How the law changed

Three rule changes that affect every retiree

Case study 1The missed-RMD penalty falls from 50% to 25%, or 10% (2022)
What happened
For decades, a missed RMD cost 50% of the shortfall. Section 302 of the SECURE 2.0 Act (Public Law 117-328) substituted "25 percent" for "50 percent" in §4974(a) and added a 10% rate for shortfalls corrected within a correction window.
What it teaches
The penalty is still large, but a mistake discovered and fixed promptly is now much cheaper: on a $10,000 shortfall, $1,000 instead of the old $5,000.
Source
26 U.S.C. §4974(a) and (e), with amendment notes; IRS Publication 590-B (2025).
Case study 2The 1959 overlap
What happened
As written, §401(a)(9)(C)(v) gives age 73 to people who reach 73 before 2033 and age 75 to people who reach 74 after 2032. Someone born in 1959 reaches 73 in 2032 and 74 in 2033, so both clauses describe them. In July 2024 the Treasury and IRS proposed regulations under which the applicable age for people born in 1959 would be 73.
What it teaches
Even a statute can contain a drafting problem. If you were born in 1959, plan on 73 and confirm when the regulations are final.
Source
26 U.S.C. §401(a)(9)(C)(v); Treasury and IRS, Required minimum distributions, proposed rule, Federal Register, July 19, 2024.
Case study 3Roth 401(k) accounts stop requiring RMDs (2024)
What happened
Before 2024, designated Roth accounts in 401(k) and 403(b) plans required RMDs even though withdrawals were tax-free, so many people rolled them to Roth IRAs to avoid that. SECURE 2.0 §325 added §402A(d)(5), removing pre-death RMDs from designated Roth accounts for taxable years beginning after December 31, 2023.
What it teaches
Roth money in a workplace plan and in an IRA now follow the same no-RMD rule for the owner. The choice of where to keep it can rest on fees, investment options and creditor protection instead.
Source
26 U.S.C. §402A(d)(5) and effective-date note; IRS, Retirement topics — Required minimum distributions.

Decisions

Timing the tax bill

Scenario 1 · Ruth, 68

Ruth has five years before her first RMD. In 2026 the first $15,750 of IRA income is tax-free for her and the next dollars cost 18.5% to 22.2%. After 73, most of her RMD dollars will cost 22.2%, rising to about 40.7% by her 80s. What should she do now?

WaitLeaves her lowest-rate years unused. The projector shows larger RMDs later, taxed at 22.2% and then around 40.7%, and about $33,920 more federal tax to 95 than steady $30,000 conversions.
Steady conversionsA strong plan. A $30,000 conversion in 2026 costs about $2,894 in federal tax, an average of 9.6%, and her modified adjusted gross income stays near $50,000, well under the $75,000 where the senior deduction starts to shrink. The Roth also passes to Anna free of income tax (INV-091).
All at onceConverting hundreds of thousands in one year would push most of it into the 22%, 24% and 32% brackets, erase the senior deduction and trigger IRMAA two years later. Spreading conversions is almost always cheaper.
The principle: pay tax in the years your marginal rate is lowest, not the years the law forces you to.
Scenario 2 · Ruth's first RMD, 2031

Ruth turns 73 in 2031. She can take her first RMD by December 31, 2031, or wait until April 1, 2032. Which is better?

WaitWaiting puts about $34,000 for 2031 and about $35,000 for 2032 in the same tax year. On top of Social Security, about $69,000 of IRA income pushes her through the 40.7% torpedo zone and into the 22% bracket. The delay saves nothing and costs a lot.
Take it in 2031Right for Ruth. Delaying the first RMD helps mainly someone whose income in the first year is unusually high and will fall the next year, such as a person who worked through part of the year.
The principle: the April 1 option moves income; it does not remove it. Use it only if next year's rate will be lower.
Scenario 3 · The Harpers' giving

In their 70s, Tom and Rachel Harper expect to give about $10,000 a year to their church and alma mater. They will take the standard deduction. How should they give?

ChecksWith the standard deduction, a cash gift saves them no tax at all. Their RMDs remain fully taxable.
QCDsRight. Each $10,000 of QCDs counts toward their RMDs and never enters adjusted gross income. At a 22% or 24% bracket, that is $2,200 to $2,400 a year of federal tax saved, plus any IRMAA benefit. Their 401(k)s would first need to be rolled into IRAs, since QCDs come from IRAs.
The principle: after 70½, give pre-tax dollars directly from the IRA.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

In the projector, Ruth's IRA is worth $904,234 on December 31, 2030. She turns 73 in 2031. What is her 2031 RMD, in dollars?

The divisor at 73 is 26.5. $904,234 ÷ 26.5 = $34,122.
Exercise 2

A 75-year-old has three IRAs worth $200,000, $150,000 and $50,000 at the end of last year. What is the total RMD this year, in dollars (it may be taken from any of the three)?

Each IRA's RMD is its balance ÷ 24.6: $8,130 + $6,098 + $2,033. The total, $400,000 ÷ 24.6 = $16,260, can come from one or more of the IRAs.
Exercise 3

An owner misses $8,000 of an RMD, notices within the year, takes it and reports it on Form 5329 within the correction window. What is the excise tax, in dollars?

Corrected within the window, the rate is 10%: $8,000 × 0.10 = $800 (versus $2,000 at 25%). A waiver for reasonable error can reduce it to zero.
Exercise 4

At 75 Ruth's RMD is $36,053. She sends $5,000 directly from her IRA to her church as a QCD and takes the rest. How much IRA income is taxable, in dollars?

The QCD counts toward the RMD but is excluded from income: $36,053 − $5,000 = $31,053 taxable.
