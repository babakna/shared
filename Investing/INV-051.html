Lump Sum vs Dollar-Cost Averaging

An inheritance, a bonus, the sale of a house, or simply too much sitting in checking: sooner or later most people hold a pile of cash they intend to invest. Put it all in today, or feed it in over months? The math has a clear answer most of the time. Your nerves may have a different one, and this module takes both seriously.

The question this module answersI have a lump of cash to invest. Should I invest it all now or spread it out, and how do I live with the choice if the market falls?

Start here

The question only comes up when you have a pile of cash

If you contribute to a 401(k) from every paycheck, you are already investing in stages, but not by choice: you are investing each dollar as soon as you earn it. That is the right thing to do and nothing in this module argues against it. The real dilemma arrives when a large sum is available all at once.

Suppose the Riveras finally decide to invest the $41,000 that has been sitting idle in checking. They could buy their chosen funds tomorrow. Or they could invest about $3,400 a month for a year, so that if the market drops next month, most of their money is still safe in cash. Both choices are reasonable, and the second one feels safer. The question is what the feeling costs. Figure 51.1 shows the idea behind the answer.

Definitions first

Lump sum, cost averaging, and the price of waiting

Lump-sum investing
What it is
Investing the entire amount at once, as soon as it is available, into the portfolio you intend to hold.
Used for
Maximizing time in the market.
What it means for you
Usually the higher-return choice, and the one with the most short-term regret if the market falls right after.
Dollar-cost averaging (cost averaging)
What it is
Investing a lump sum in equal pieces at regular intervals — for example a third each month for three months — while the rest waits in cash.
Used for
Reducing the chance of investing everything just before a fall, and the regret that would follow.
What it means for you
It lowers risk only temporarily, and it costs return in most historical periods. Its real value is behavioral: helping you invest at all.
Opportunity cost
What it is
What you give up by choosing one option over the next best one. For money held in cash, it is the extra return the invested portfolio would probably have earned.
Used for
Measuring the price of waiting, of holding too much cash, or of any choice with a cheaper-looking alternative.
What it means for you
Cash that feels "safe" is not free. Its cost is invisible because it is return you never see.

A rough expected cost of spreading out a lump sum follows from how much money waits, how long, and how much more the portfolio is expected to earn than cash:

Expected cost ≈ amount × (average share waiting in cash during the year) × (expected portfolio return − cash return)

Spread $41,000 over 12 equal monthly installments, starting today, and on average about 46% of the money sits in cash during the year (the uninvested share falls from 11/12 to 0; its average is 66 ÷ 144 = 45.8%). If you assumed stocks earn 7% and cash 4% — illustrative numbers, not a forecast — the expected cost would be about 41,000 × 0.458 × 0.03 ≈ $560. Small, but it is the average; in a strong year it is much more, and in a falling year it is a saving.

Going further"But I buy more shares when prices are low"

True. Investing a fixed $300 at share prices of $10, $5 and $10 buys 30, 60 and 30 shares: 120 shares for $900, an average cost of $7.50 while the average price was $8.33. Fixed-dollar purchases always produce an average cost at or below the average price. But the lump-sum investor who bought 90 shares at $10 on day one is compared against a different benchmark: owning the market the whole time. The favorable average-cost result does not tell you whether you would have been better off investing everything at the start — which, when prices trend upward, you usually would.

Practitioner depthWhy theory has long been skeptical of cost averaging

George Constantinides (1979) published a formal argument, in the Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis, that dollar-cost averaging is a suboptimal investment policy. Vanguard's 2023 paper describes the literature since then as "generally in favor of lump-sum investing." The case for cost averaging rests instead on behavior; Meir Statman (1995) offered a behavioral framework to explain its lasting popularity. Both views appear in the next tabs.

Test it on 98 years

Lump sum versus spreading it over several years

This course's dataset is annual, so the tester spreads a windfall over two to five years, investing an equal share at the start of each year while the rest earns the Treasury bill rate. Each bar is one starting year. Above zero, investing all at once ended ahead.

With the default settings (three yearly installments into 100% stocks, cash earning T-bill rates), the lump sum ended ahead in 72 of the 96 starting years from 1928 to 2023: 75% of the time, with a median advantage of 7.6% of ending wealth. But the losses from lump-sum investing in bad starting years were larger than the median gain. Starting at the beginning of 1930, the lump sum ended 40% behind spreading the money over 1930–1932 (put the other way, spreading ended with 67% more); starting in 2008, the lump sum ended 22% behind. The best year for the lump sum was 1954, when it ended 38% ahead.

⚠️

What yearly data cannot show. Most people who spread out a windfall do it over 3 to 12 months, not years. With one return per year, this tester cannot model monthly installments, so treat its multi-year results as an exaggerated version of the same trade-off: the longer the phase-in, the larger both the typical cost and the occasional rescue. For monthly evidence, see Vanguard's research in the next tab.

Going furtherWhy the gap is fixed once you are fully invested

After the last installment, both portfolios own exactly the same thing, so their returns from then on are identical in percentage terms. Whatever lead one gained during the phase-in is locked in forever as a ratio. That is why the tester measures the result at the end of the phase-in: waiting ten more years changes the dollar gap but not the percentage gap.

Monthly evidence

What Vanguard found, and how to read it

In February 2023, Vanguard researchers Megan Finlay and Josef Zorn published Cost averaging: Invest now or temporarily hold your cash? They compared investing a lump sum immediately with a common cost-averaging plan: splitting it into three equal parts invested one month apart, with performance measured after one year.

  1. Lump sum won about two-thirds of the time. Using MSCI World Index returns for 1976–2022, a 100% equity lump sum beat three-month cost averaging 68% of the time, with no interest on the waiting cash.
  2. Interest on the cash narrows the gap only slightly. Paying the waiting cash the 3-month Treasury bill rate, lump sum still won 65% of the time.
  3. Cost averaging still beat staying in cash. It outperformed holding cash for the whole year 69% of the time; lump sum beat cash 70% of the time.
  4. Longer phase-ins cost more. For the US (Russell 3000, 1979–2022), lump sum's hit rate rose from 66.4% against a three-month split to 73.7% against a six-month split.
  5. The typical gap is modest. At the median, a 60/40 portfolio ended the year at $109,360 with a lump sum versus $107,453 with three-month cost averaging, about 1.8% more; for 100% equity the median gap was 2.2%.

The same paper shows the other side. In the worst historical outcomes the ranking reverses: at the 5th percentile, a $100,000 all-equity lump sum was worth $82,947 after a year versus $85,906 with cost averaging. Cost averaging's appeal lives in that tail. The authors' recommendation rests on the far more common outcomes between the 25th and 75th percentiles, where the lump sum came out ahead.

Practitioner depthReading the fine print

The headline 68% uses a global index in US dollars with zero interest on cash; results for individual markets range from 61.6% (emerging markets) to 68.1% (UK) for the three-month split. The paper also ran 10,000 simulated return paths as a robustness check and found, for a 60/40 portfolio, that three-month cost averaging ended about $504 behind lump sum on $100,000 after one year, and six-month cost averaging about $1,491 behind. These are medians of models, not guarantees.

The human side

Minimizing regret without giving up too much

If the math favors the lump sum two times in three, why does cost averaging remain popular? Because the two kinds of mistake do not feel the same. Investing everything the week before a 20% drop feels like a personal failure. Missing a 20% gain because you phased in slowly feels like bad luck. Psychologists call the stronger pain of losses loss aversion, and the tendency to choose whatever minimizes future self-blame regret aversion.

Certainty equivalent
What it is
The guaranteed return a person would accept instead of an uncertain one. Someone who fears losses accepts a lower certain return to avoid the risky one.
Used for
Comparing strategies by how an investor actually values them, not just by average outcome.
What it means for you
If a strategy's worst case would make you abandon your plan, its average return overstates what it is worth to you.

Vanguard's paper tested exactly this. Using a model with both risk aversion and loss aversion (a loss-aversion factor of 2.5), it found that an "adventurous" investor preferred the lump sum either way, but a "moderately conservative" investor preferred the lump sum without loss aversion and cost averaging once loss aversion was added. The authors' conclusion: cost averaging "might be considered for investors with very high aversion to both risk and losses who might be tempted to hold a lump sum entirely in cash," and even then the phase-in should be short, such as three months.

One practical point makes the dilemma smaller than it looks: the lump sum does not have to go into 100% stocks. If a windfall will be part of a 60/40 portfolio, investing it all at once into 60/40 carries far less short-term risk than an all-stock lump sum, and Vanguard's data show the lump-sum advantage is smaller for lower-equity mixes (1.2% at the median for 40/60 versus 2.2% for 100% equity). Choosing the right mix first often dissolves the urge to wait.

Before you invest a windfall

Questions that come before "now or later"

Retirement contributions. Vanguard's paper suggests a version of lump-sum thinking for regular savers: making a year's retirement-account contribution as early in the year as you can, rather than at the end, adds months of market exposure every year. In a 401(k), ask your plan how the employer match is calculated before front-loading; if the match is computed each pay period, maxing out early could leave match money on the table.

Selling one investment to buy another. If you are moving from a concentrated stock or an expensive fund into a diversified portfolio, the "lump sum" question is really about the tax cost of selling (INV-035) and the risk of staying concentrated, not about timing the purchase.

Money that is already invested. Some people spread out a rebalancing move or a switch between funds out of the same anxiety. If both the old and new holdings are invested, there is no cash drag, so the reasoning here does not apply the same way.

History's extreme cases

Three windfalls, three outcomes

These are illustrations computed from the dataset: $100,000 received at the start of a year, invested in the S&P 500 either at once or in three equal yearly installments, with waiting cash earning T-bill rates.

Case study 1A windfall at the start of 2008
What happened
Stocks returned −36.6% in 2008, +25.9% in 2009 and +14.8% in 2010. Invested at once, $100,000 was worth about $91,800 at the end of 2010; spread over the three years, about $118,000.
What it teaches
When a crash comes early, spreading out wins by a wide margin: the lump sum ended about 22% behind, and the spread-out investor had about 29% more. These are the cases people remember and the reason cost averaging feels wise.
Source
Computed from Damodaran, NYU Stern, 2008–2010 returns.
Case study 2A windfall at the start of 1995
What happened
Stocks rose in each of 1995, 1996 and 1997. Invested at once, $100,000 became about $224,000 by the end of 1997; spread over three years, about $179,000.
What it teaches
The more common case is the quiet one: the waiting cash simply misses gains. Nobody tells stories about the $45,000 they did not make.
Source
Computed from Damodaran, NYU Stern, 1995–1997 returns.
Case study 31930: the worst start for a lump sum
What happened
Stocks fell in 1930, 1931 and 1932. A lump sum invested in January 1930 was worth about $38,400 at the end of 1932; three yearly installments, about $64,300. The lump sum ended about 40% behind; spreading out left 67% more.
What it teaches
The tail really is fat. But even this investor had a better answer than cost averaging: a balanced mix. At 60/40 the lump-sum shortfall in the same start year was about 24%, not 40%.

Decisions

Windfalls in the five households

Scenario 1 · The Riveras

After topping up their emergency fund, the Riveras have $32,000 of the $41,000 left to invest for the long run, in a mostly-stock portfolio. Elena worries about "buying at the top." What should they do?

All nowThe higher expected return, and the historical winner about two times in three. Right if they can watch a 20% drop next month without selling.
Half now, rest over three monthsA sound compromise. The expected cost is small because the phase-in is short, and the regret protection is real. The key is automating it.
Wait for a correctionThis is market timing (INV-052). Corrections do come, but not on schedule, and the market may rise well past today's level first. It is the choice most likely to leave money in cash for years.
The principle: any plan that gets the money invested on a fixed schedule beats waiting for the right moment.
Scenario 2 · Ruth

A $40,000 CD in Ruth's savings matures. She does not need the money for spending; her Social Security covers her bills. She is nervous about markets but knows cash has lagged inflation for long periods. What fits her best?

All stocks at onceHer whole-portfolio mix matters more than this $40,000. Adding it all to stocks at 68 may push her beyond what she is comfortable holding.
Balanced fundRight. Deciding the mix first makes the timing question smaller; a balanced fund's lump-sum risk is far lower than an all-stock one. Either timing is reasonable.
Another CDFine for money she may need soon. For money meant to last 20-plus years, holding it permanently in CDs is the cash drag Vanguard warns about.
The principle: choose the right mix first; the timing question shrinks once you do.
Scenario 3 · Denise

Denise receives a $4,000 tax refund. Her 401(k) is invested from each paycheck. She has $28,000 of student loans at 6.8% and $3,000 of savings. Should she cost-average the refund into an IRA?

Twelve months into a Roth IRANot harmful, but the timing question is the least important one here. Her emergency fund is thin, and a 6.8% guaranteed return on loan payoff is attractive.
Emergency fund, then debt or RothRight. The checklist in Figure 51.6 comes first. If she chooses the Roth IRA, investing the amount at once is simplest.
The principle: for small sums, the order of priorities matters far more than the timing of the purchase.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

You invest $300 a month for three months at share prices of $10, $5 and $10. What is your average cost per share, in dollars?

Shares: 300/10 + 300/5 + 300/10 = 30 + 60 + 30 = 120. Cost $900. Average cost = 900 ÷ 120 = $7.50, below the average price of $8.33.
Exercise 2

Using Figure 51.11, what is the average share of the money waiting in cash over the four months, in percent?

(75% + 50% + 25% + 0%) ÷ 4 = 37.5% of the money, on average, sits in cash during the four months.
Exercise 3

Using the rough formula, what is the expected cost of spreading $41,000 over 12 months if on average 45.8% waits in cash and you assume the portfolio earns 3 points more than cash? Answer in dollars.

41,000 × 0.458 × 0.03 = $563 (about $560). A rough expectation under an assumption, not a forecast.
Exercise 4

In Vanguard's data, the median 60/40 lump sum ended at $109,360 and three-month cost averaging at $107,453. By what percentage was the lump sum ahead (one decimal)?

109,360 ÷ 107,453 = 1.0177, so about 1.8% ahead, matching the paper.
