Choosing a Financial Advisor

"Financial advisor" is a job title, not a legal category. Behind it can stand a salesperson paid by commission, a fiduciary paid only by you, or one person who is both depending on the account. This module shows how to tell them apart, what each way of paying costs over decades, and how to check anyone before you hand over a dollar.

The question this module answersIf I want help, how do I find someone who is qualified, honest and worth what they charge?

Start here

Same title, different jobs

You already know how to hire help you cannot fully judge: check the license, get the quote in writing, and notice who profits from the recommendation. A plumber who sells you a new water heater earns more than one who fixes the old one.

Financial help is the same, with one complication: the person across the table may owe you different legal duties depending on how they are registered, and sometimes on which account you are discussing. Most people called financial advisors work in one of two regulated businesses. An investment adviser is paid to give advice and owes you a fiduciary duty for the whole relationship. A broker-dealer is paid to buy and sell securities for you, usually through commissions, and must act in your best interest when it recommends something. Many large firms, and many individuals, are both: dual registrants. Figure 103.1 lays out the three possibilities.

Commission-based help is not automatically bad, nor fee-only help automatically good. The incentives and duties differ, and you should know which you are buying. It matters: on a portfolio the size of the Harpers', two reasonable-sounding ways to pay differ by about half a million dollars over 20 years.

What this module covers

  1. The two legal standards: an adviser's fiduciary duty and a broker's Regulation Best Interest.
  2. Who is who: advisers, brokers, dual registrants, and who regulates each.

  4. Fee models, with a calculator comparing a percentage fee, a flat fee and hourly advice over 20 years.
  5. Credentials and background checks: the Certified Financial Planner (CFP) mark, BrokerCheck, the Investment Adviser Public Disclosure (IAPD) site and the CFP Board lookup.
  6. The questions to ask in an interview, and the red flags that should end one.

The legal duty

Fiduciary duty versus Regulation Best Interest

A fiduciary is trusted to act for another person and legally required to put that person first. A salesperson must sell you something suitable, but works for the seller. US law puts investment advisers in the first group. Broker-dealers were held to a "suitability" standard; since June 30, 2020, Regulation Best Interest has moved them a long way toward the first group, but not all the way.

An investment adviser's fiduciary dutyInvestment Advisers Act of 1940; SEC interpretation, 2019
What it is
In the words of the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), a duty of care plus a duty of loyalty. Care: advice in your best interest based on your objectives, best execution of trades, and monitoring over the relationship. Loyalty: never putting the adviser's interests ahead of yours, and eliminating each conflict or disclosing it fully enough for your informed consent. It applies "at all times" and cannot be waived.
Used for
Every relationship with a registered investment adviser.
What it means for you
The duty follows the relationship, not the trade: if you agree to ongoing management, the adviser must keep watching. And a conflict must be explained well enough that you understand what you are consenting to.
Regulation Best InterestSEC rule adopted June 5, 2019; compliance from June 30, 2020
What it is
The standard for broker-dealers when they recommend a securities transaction, a strategy, or an account type (including rolling a 401(k) into an IRA) to a retail customer: act in your best interest at the time of the recommendation, without putting their interests ahead of yours, through four obligations: disclosure, care, conflict of interest and compliance.
Used for
Brokerage accounts, commission-based purchases, and rollover recommendations.
What it means for you
The SEC's guide says the rule "does not impose a duty to monitor" your account unless the broker agrees to. Between recommendations, no one may be watching.

The rule also polices the word itself: the SEC presumes that a broker, or broker representative, not also registered as an adviser violates the disclosure obligation by using "adviser" or "advisor" in a name or title. So someone whose card says "advisor" is usually registered as an adviser somewhere, but may still be acting as a broker for the account in front of you.

Going furtherWhat about advice on retirement plan rollovers?

Workplace plans also fall under the Employee Retirement Income Security Act (ERISA), enforced by the Department of Labor. Its 2024 "Retirement Security Rule," which would have made more rollover recommendations fiduciary advice, was stayed by two federal courts in July 2024 and later vacated. On March 18, 2026 the department removed it, restored ERISA's "five-part test" for who is an investment advice fiduciary, and said it had no current plans for new rulemaking. So a rollover recommendation is covered by Regulation Best Interest (broker) or the Advisers Act (adviser); whether ERISA also applies depends on the facts. Ask, in writing, which standard applies.

Practitioner depthThe exact words firms must print

Form CRS instructions prescribe the duty sentence. Advisers write: "When we act as your investment adviser, we have to act in your best interest and not put our interest ahead of yours." Brokers write: "When we provide you with a recommendation, we have to act in your best interest..." Both must add that "the way we make money creates some conflicts with your interests." "When we act as your adviser" versus "when we provide a recommendation" is the difference between a relationship duty and a transaction duty.

The care obligation also requires a reasonable basis to believe a series of recommended trades is not excessive for your profile: the modern ban on churning, trading mainly to generate commissions.

Registration

Advisers, brokers and dual registrants

Anyone paid for investment advice, or selling securities to the public, must register somewhere, and the registration is public.

Registered investment adviser (RIA)the firm; its people are investment adviser representatives
What it is
A firm paid to advise on securities. With more than $110 million of regulatory assets under management it generally must register with the SEC; under $100 million it generally may not, and registers with the states instead. Its advice-givers are investment adviser representatives.
Used for
Portfolio management, planning, and advice-only work.
What it means for you
Small firms are usually state-registered, large ones SEC-registered. Either way the firm files Form ADV, free to read on the SEC's Investment Adviser Public Disclosure (IAPD) site.
Broker-dealerthe firm; its people are registered representatives
What it is
A firm registered with the SEC and a member of FINRA, the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority, a private not-for-profit that supervises member firms under federal law and SEC oversight. Its people are registered representatives, often called stockbrokers.
Used for
Holding accounts, trading, and selling products, usually for commissions.
What it means for you
Every broker and representative appears on FINRA BrokerCheck, with job history and any disputes or discipline.
Dual registrant
What it is
A firm or professional registered as both adviser and broker. Which standard applies "will depend on the capacity" in which they act for that recommendation, says the SEC.
Used for
Offering advisory and brokerage accounts under one roof.
What it means for you
Two accounts with the same person can carry different duties. Ask of each: "Here, are you my investment adviser or my broker?"
Going further"Fee-only," "fee-based" and "commission" are not the same

Fee-only means paid only by clients. "Fee-based" usually means fees plus possible commissions, typically from a dual registrant. "Commission-based" means the product provider pays. These are industry labels, so ask for every way the person and firm are paid, in dollars, and compare it with the firm's Form CRS.

Read before you sign

Two documents that answer most of your questions

You do not have to interrogate anyone to learn how they are paid: regulators make firms write it down.

Form CRSCustomer or Client Relationship Summary, required since June 30, 2020
What it is
A plain-English summary every SEC-registered adviser and broker serving retail investors must file, post online and give you: at most two pages (four for a dual registrant covering both services), with standard headings in a set order.
Used for
Comparing firms on services, fees, conflicts, duty and disciplinary history before you open, end or switch a relationship.
What it means for you
Ask for it at the first meeting. Firms must deliver it within 30 days of a request and update it within 30 days of it becoming materially inaccurate.

For an investment adviser, the second document is Form ADV. Part 1 is for regulators; Part 2A, the "brochure," is for clients; Part 2B, the "brochure supplement," covers your individual adviser: education, experience, discipline, other business and extra compensation. Form CRS is Part 3. Of the brochure's 19 items, seven do most of the work.

Form ADV Part 2A itemWhat to look for
Item 5 Fees and CompensationThe fee schedule, whether it is negotiable, whether fees are deducted from your account or billed, and whether the firm or its people also accept pay for selling investment products
Item 9 Disciplinary InformationLegal or disciplinary events that would matter to your evaluation
Item 10 Other Financial Industry Activities and AffiliationsAffiliated broker-dealers, insurance agencies or fund companies, and the conflicts they create
Item 11 Code of Ethics, Personal TradingWhether the adviser trades in the same securities it recommends to you
Item 12 Brokerage PracticesHow the adviser chooses the brokers that trade for you, and any research or other "soft dollar" benefits it receives in return for client trades
Item 14 Client Referrals and Other CompensationWho pays the adviser besides you, and who the adviser pays for referrals
Item 15 CustodyConfirmation that you will receive statements from a qualified custodian, with a reminder to compare them with any reports from the adviser
Going furtherWhy custody is a separate item

Under the SEC's custody rule, an adviser with custody of client assets generally must keep them at a qualified custodian, such as a bank or broker-dealer, and have a reasonable basis to believe the custodian sends you statements at least quarterly. Someone other than your adviser holds the money and tells you directly what is there. Comparing the custodian's statement with your adviser's report is one of the simplest fraud checks there is (INV-062).

What advice costs

Percentage, flat, hourly or commission

INV-007 introduced the ways of paying for advice; here we measure them over 20 years. The key difference: a percentage fee grows with your balance whether or not the work does; a flat or hourly fee grows only when the adviser raises the price.

Assets under management (AUM) fee
What it is
A yearly percentage of the assets managed, usually deducted from your account. Tiered schedules charge lower rates on each additional slice.
Used for
Ongoing portfolio management, often bundled with planning.
What it means for you
Easy to ignore, because it never arrives as a bill. It also rewards keeping money under management: the adviser earns less if you use the dollars to pay off a mortgage. Form ADV Item 5 must say whether the rate is negotiable.
Flat fee, retainer, hourly and project feesoften called "advice-only"
What it is
A fixed price for a defined scope of work, an hourly rate, or a one-time price for a written plan.
Used for
Planning without asset management, and second opinions.
What it means for you
The price is visible and does not rise with markets, but you must place the trades yourself.

The defaults describe the Harpers: $1.1 million, 6% before costs, 0.05% index funds, no additions.

The percentage fee costs about $777,000 of ending wealth for $463,000 of fees paid, because each fee dollar would have kept compounding. And the fee itself grows from about $14,600 in year 1 to $34,400 in year 20 (Figure 103.5), though advising the Harpers at 77 is not two and a half times the work of advising them at 57.

Going furtherWhat can advice be worth?

Vanguard, which itself sells advice, estimates that advisers following its best practices "can potentially add up to, or even exceed, 3% in net returns," with behavioral coaching the largest piece at "up to 200 or more" basis points. Its notes add that the value "may vary significantly" and accrues "over an unspecified period of time," not every year. The value of advice arrives in lumps: the year an adviser stops you selling in a crash or fixes a tax mistake. If you would not have made those mistakes, you are buying insurance you do not need.

Practitioner depthConverting any fee into a percentage and back
Effective rate = annual fee in dollars ÷ portfolio value     Break-even value added ≈ fee rate A − fee rate B

$6,000 on $1.1 million is 0.55% a year; on $3 million, 0.2%. Against a 0.55% flat fee, a 1.25% adviser must add about 0.7 points a year of extra value just to break even. The calculator charges the percentage on the year-end balance, so year 1 is 1.25% of $1,165,450 = $14,568, not 1.25% of $1,100,000 = $13,750.

Check before you trust

What credentials mean, and a ten-minute background check

A registration is a license to operate; a credential is a claim about training. Check both. The best-known planning credential is the CFP mark.

CFP® certificationawarded by CFP Board
What it is
A planning credential with four requirements. Education: a bachelor's degree plus coursework through a CFP Board Registered Program. Exam: 170 multiple-choice questions in two 3-hour sessions. Experience: 6,000 hours, or 4,000 in a qualifying apprenticeship. Ethics: a declaration and a background check. Then 30 hours of continuing education each reporting period, 2 of them on ethics.
Used for
Signaling broad training across taxes, retirement, insurance, estate and investments.
What it means for you
CFP Board's Code of Ethics says that "at all times when providing Financial Advice to a Client, a CFP® professional must act as a fiduciary." That is a professional standard enforced by CFP Board, separate from the securities laws, and it applies to a CFP professional whatever their registration.

Other designations vary enormously in rigor. FINRA's free professional-designations database shows each one's training, continuing education, and whether the issuer takes complaints or lets you confirm who holds it; FINRA "does not approve or endorse any professional credential." If you cannot confirm a credential, give it no weight.

BrokerCheck draws on the industry's registration database and the SEC's adviser database. It shows registrations, ten years of employment, qualifications, and "customer disputes, disciplinary events and certain criminal and financial matters." Records generally stay up for ten years after someone leaves the industry, longer for final regulatory actions. On IAPD, a firm search shows its full Form ADV.

💡

Read the disclosures, not just the count. One denied complaint from 15 years ago differs from three settled complaints about one product in two years. Research (Case studies tab) found that past misconduct strongly predicts future misconduct.

The interview

Ten questions to ask, and the answers that should end the meeting

A good adviser expects to be interviewed. Bring these in writing and ask for the key answers by email.

  1. "Are you a fiduciary at all times, for every account you would handle for me? Will you put that in writing?" If the answer is "when I act as your adviser," ask which accounts that excludes.
  2. "How are you and your firm paid, from every source, for what you would do for me?" Include commissions, 12b-1 fees, revenue sharing and referral fees.
  3. "If I give you $10,000, how much goes to fees and costs in a year, in dollars?" The Form CRS question; include fund expenses.
  4. "What exactly do I get for the fee?" Investments only, or taxes, retirement income, insurance and estate? How often do we meet?
  5. "Who holds my money, and who sends my statements?" The answer should name an independent custodian.
  6. "What is your investment approach, and how will you choose investments for me?" Look for low costs and broad diversification (INV-044), not a promise to beat the market.
  7. "Do you or your firm have any disciplinary history? For what type of conduct?" Then check it yourself.
  8. "What are your credentials, and how can I verify them?"
  9. "Who are your typical clients?" A retiree specialist may be a poor fit for a 24-year-old.
  10. "How do I leave, and what would it cost?" Look for surrender charges, closing fees and products that cannot be transferred (INV-104).

Real enforcement and research

What regulators and researchers have found

Case study 1The SEC's share-class initiative, 2019
What happened
On March 11, 2019 the SEC announced settled charges against 79 investment advisers that returned more than $125 million to clients, mostly retail investors. They had put clients in mutual fund share classes carrying 12b-1 fees when cheaper classes of the same fund were available, without adequate disclosure; the 12b-1 fees flowed to the advisers acting as brokers, to affiliated broker-dealers, or to their staff. The firms self-reported under a 2018 program that waived penalties for those who repaid clients.
What it teaches
Fiduciaries still have conflicts, and dual registration is where many hide. Ask whether you hold the lowest-cost share class available to you, and check the prospectus.
Source
U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (2019, March 11). SEC share class initiative returning more than $125 million to investors (Press Release 2019-28).
Case study 2An early Regulation Best Interest case: L Bonds sold to retirees
What happened
In June 2022 the SEC sued broker-dealer Western International Securities and five brokers, alleging that from July 2020 to April 2021 they sold about $13.3 million of L Bonds (unrated, illiquid, high-risk bonds from GWG Holdings) to retail customers, many retired, on fixed incomes and with moderate risk tolerances, although the issuer said the bonds suited only customers with substantial resources. The defendants were ordered to pay, and paid, $327,280.52 in total; in March 2026 a court set up a Fair Fund to return it to investors.
What it teaches
"Best interest" is judged against your profile, not just the product. The amount recovered was a small fraction of the amount sold.
Source
SEC (2022, June 16). SEC charges firm and five brokers with violations of Reg BI (Press Release 2022-110); SEC, SEC v. Western International Securities, Inc., et al., harmed-investor notice (updated March 16, 2026).
Case study 3Research: misconduct is concentrated and it repeats
What happened
Economists Mark Egan, Gregor Matvos and Amit Seru studied the records of US financial advisers registered with FINRA. 7% had misconduct records, more than 15% at some of the largest firms; about a third of those were repeat offenders; and prior offenders were five times as likely as the typical adviser to offend again. About half lost their jobs, but many were rehired elsewhere.
What it teaches
Most advisers have clean records, so a blemish is informative. A BrokerCheck search is the cheapest due diligence there is.
Source
Egan, M., Matvos, G., & Seru, A. (2019). The market for financial adviser misconduct. Journal of Political Economy, 127(1), 233–295.

Decisions

Three households, three kinds of help

Scenario 1 · The Harpers

Tom and Rachel pay 1.25% a year and meet their adviser once a year. Big decisions are coming: Social Security timing, Roth conversions of their $820,000 of pre-tax savings, and unwinding 25 technology stocks without a large tax bill. What should they do?

Stay as they areInertia has a price: about $498,000 over 20 years against a $6,000 flat fee in the illustration. The fee may be defensible if the adviser does the tax and income planning; they should know.
Get the scope in writing and compareRight. This is when good planning pays. Even if their adviser does all of it, a lower rate is worth asking for; Form ADV Item 5 says whether fees are negotiable.
Go fully do-it-yourselfSaves the most, if they actually do the planning and hold steady in a crash. A middle path: a one-time retirement plan, then self-management (INV-104).
The principle: pay for the work you need, priced in dollars, and review the price whenever the work changes.
Scenario 2 · Ruth

A friendly representative at Ruth's bank branch, whose card says "Financial Advisor," suggests moving $300,000 of her IRA into a product "with protection" and says there is "no fee to you." What should Ruth do?

Trust the bankStocks, bonds and mutual funds are not FDIC-insured, even when bought at a bank branch. "No fee to you" often means a commission paid by the product, with the cost inside it.
Ask, verify, and bring AnnaRight. The representative owes her a Form CRS and a recommendation that meets the care obligation for her profile. A trusted second person in the room is sensible protection.
Avoid all adviceUnderstandable, but Ruth has decisions ahead, including required minimum distributions from 2031. An hourly fee-only planner can review the offer for a known price.
The principle: "no fee to you" is a question, not an answer: find out who pays whom.
Scenario 3 · Jordan

Jordan has $3,000 in savings, $28,000 of student loans and a new 401(k). A friend's adviser offers to manage her money for 1% a year with a $100,000 minimum she does not meet, or "a starter plan" for commissions on the funds she buys. What is her best option?

Commission starter planOn $3,000 a 5% sales load is $150 before anything is invested, and her decisions are simple enough that ongoing paid management adds little.
One hourly session, then simple fundsRight. Her real questions (the full match, the 6.8% loans, the emergency fund; INV-018 to INV-022) fit one focused session.
The principle: match the kind of help to the size and complexity of the problem.

What kind of help fits each household

A session, not a subscription. Jordan's questions are clear and one-off. An hourly planner or free employer resources fit; a 1% relationship does not.
A project plan. Consolidating six overlapping funds, deploying $41,000 of idle cash and starting 529s for Sofia and Leo is a well-defined flat-fee project.
A specialist for Eli, a project planner for herself. A third-party special needs trust, Eli's SSI application at 18 and a guardianship-or-alternatives decision call for an attorney experienced in special-needs planning (INV-102). Her own questions, such as catch-up saving and how much term life insurance should fund the trust, suit a flat-fee or hourly planner: a 1% relationship would cost about $4,000 a year on her $400,000.
The most to gain, and the most to lose. Their fee is about $14,000 a year and rising. They need the most planning of any household; they should confirm they get it, and compare prices.
A fiduciary, and a second pair of eyes. Ruth is the most likely to be sold complex products. A fee-only planner at a known price, a trusted contact on her accounts, and Anna at key meetings protect her best (INV-098).

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

The Harpers pay 1.25% a year on $1,100,000. What is this year's fee, in dollars, on today's balance?

$1,100,000 × 0.0125 = $13,750. In 0.05% index funds alone, fund costs would be $550.
Exercise 2

A flat-fee planner quotes the Harpers $6,000 a year. What is that as a percentage of $1,100,000? (Answer in percent, to two decimals.)

6,000 ÷ 1,100,000 = 0.00545 = 0.55%. The AUM adviser costs 0.70 percentage points more this year, and the gap widens as the portfolio grows.
Exercise 3

A tiered schedule charges 1.00% on the first $1,000,000 and 0.75% on assets above that. What is the annual fee on $1,200,000, in dollars?

$1,000,000 × 0.01 = $10,000; $200,000 × 0.0075 = $1,500; total $11,500, an effective rate of 0.96%. Tiers apply to each slice, not the whole balance.
Exercise 4

Using the Form CRS question: an adviser charges 1% of assets, and the funds it uses average 0.10%. If you give it $10,000, how many dollars a year go to the adviser's fee alone?

$10,000 × 0.01 = $100 to the adviser, plus $10 of fund expenses: $110 in total, 1.10% a year.
