Long-Term Care

Long-term care is the largest uninsured risk most retirees face: many will need little or none, and a minority will need years of help that can cost more than a house. This module measures the risk with federal data, prices it with a current cost survey, explains who pays what, and compares insurance with paying your own way.

The question this module answersHow likely am I to need long-term care, what would it cost, and how should I plan to pay for it?

Start here

A risk shaped like a lottery ticket in reverse

You already insure your house against fire and your car against a crash, even though you probably will never file a big claim. Long-term care is a similar kind of risk — unlikely to be catastrophic for any one person, ruinous when it is — except that most people have no insurance for it, and the health insurance and Medicare they do have do not cover it.

Long-term care means help with the basic tasks of daily life, such as bathing, dressing, eating, using the toilet and moving around, or supervision because of severe memory loss. It is mostly not medical care. It is provided at home, in adult day programs, in assisted living, and in nursing homes, most often by unpaid family members.

The risk, measured

How likely, and for how long

Activities of daily livingADLs
What it is
Basic self-care tasks: bathing, dressing, toileting, eating, continence and transferring (getting in and out of a bed or chair).
Used for
Measuring need. Tax-qualified long-term care insurance generally pays when you need help with at least two ADLs expected to last at least 90 days, or need substantial supervision because of severe cognitive impairment; federal research uses the same threshold.
What it means for you
A long-term care (LTC) insurance policy's benefits start when a professional certifies you meet this test, not when you decide you would like help.

In planning terms these tasks are called activities of daily living. Two federal sources give the size of the risk. They measure slightly different things, which is why their headline numbers differ.

Going furtherWhy women face a larger risk

ASPE projects that 64% of women turning 65 will develop a significant disability and 26% will need at least five years of care, and that women's expected LTSS costs average $154,300 against $85,400 for men. Women live longer, are more likely to be widowed at older ages, and so are less likely to have a spouse at home to provide unpaid care. That is exactly Ruth's situation.

Practitioner depthThe value of unpaid care

ASPE estimates that, valued at the wage of a paid caregiver, unpaid family care for older adults with significant disabilities who receive care is worth $204,000 on average, more than the expected cost of all paid LTSS. Plans that assume a spouse or child will provide care are, in effect, relying on a very large unpriced asset. A plan should say who that caregiver is and what happens if they cannot do it.

Who pays

Medicare pays for recovery; Medicaid pays for the long haul

The most common planning mistake is assuming Medicare will pay for long-term care. Medicare.gov is blunt: "Medicare doesn't pay for long-term care," meaning custodial help with daily living. What it does pay for is short-term skilled care after a hospital stay.

Medicare skilled nursing facility coverage2026
What it is
Part A covers care in a Medicare-certified skilled nursing facility after a qualifying inpatient hospital stay of at least three days (time "under observation" does not count), when you need daily skilled nursing or therapy.
Used for
Recovery. For 2026 you pay $0 for days 1–20 (after the Part A deductible of $1,736, if not already paid), $217 a day for days 21–100, and all costs after day 100 in a benefit period.
What it means for you
Even the covered days end when you stop needing skilled care. Help with bathing and dressing alone is custodial care, which Medicare does not pay for.

For long stays the payer of last resort is Medicaid.

Medicaid long-term care
What it is
The joint federal-state health program for people with low income and few assets. ASPE projects it will be the largest payer of LTSS for people turning 65 now, covering 43% of their paid LTSS costs overall and 52% of costs in institutions.
Used for
Paying for nursing-home care, and in many states home and community-based services, once you meet strict income and asset tests. States set the rules within federal limits, and asset limits are strict; for reference, the Supplemental Security Income (SSI) resource standard in the same Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) chart is $2,000 for an individual in 2026. Check your own state's Medicaid limits.
What it means for you
Many people who start paying privately "spend down" to Medicaid eligibility. That is a legitimate safety net, but it can mean nursing-home care only, limited choice of facility, and a claim on your estate afterward.

The 60-month look-back

You cannot simply give your savings away the month before applying. Under Social Security Act §1917(c), when someone applies for Medicaid long-term care, the state looks back at asset transfers for less than fair value. The Deficit Reduction Act of 2005 set the look-back at 60 months (five years) for transfers made on or after its enactment; it had been 36 months. Transfers in that window trigger a period of ineligibility equal to the uncompensated value transferred divided by the average monthly private cost of nursing-facility care in the state (or community, at the state's option).

Practitioner depthEstate recovery

Section 1917(b) requires states to seek recovery from the estate of a person who was 55 or older when they received Medicaid nursing-facility services, home and community-based services, and related hospital and prescription drug services (and allows states to recover for other services). Recovery may be made only after the death of a surviving spouse, and only when there is no surviving child under 21 or blind or disabled child (§1917(b)(2)), among other protections. For a widow like Ruth whose main asset is a house, that means Medicaid-paid care can become a claim on the house her daughter might otherwise inherit. Planning with an elder-law attorney, well before the look-back window, is the lawful way to address it.

Transfer the risk

Long-term care insurance and hybrid policies

Long-term care insurance pays for care when you meet the benefit trigger. ASPE reports that less than 8% of Americans have bought coverage, partly because premiums have been high and rising and insurers have left the market.

Traditional long-term care insurance
What it is
A policy you pay premiums on every year, which pays a daily or monthly benefit for care for a set benefit period, after a waiting period called the elimination period.
Used for
Covering the costly tail: the 20% or so of people who need years of care. The main design choices are the daily benefit, the benefit period, the elimination period and inflation protection.
What it means for you
Premiums on traditional policies are generally not guaranteed, and ASPE notes that premiums in this market have been high and rising. Ask exactly how and when the premium can change, budget for increases, and know your options (such as reducing benefits) if they come.
Hybrid (linked-benefit) policy
What it is
A life insurance policy or annuity with a long-term care benefit attached. If you need care, it pays; if you never do, the policy pays a death benefit or keeps its value.
Used for
Avoiding the "use it or lose it" feel of traditional coverage. Premiums are often paid in a lump sum or over a few years; ask whether they are guaranteed. Federal law allows a tax-free exchange of a life insurance or annuity contract for a qualified long-term care contract (26 U.S.C. §1035).
What it means for you
You pay for flexibility: per dollar of care benefit, hybrids usually cost more than traditional coverage. Compare the care benefit, the death benefit and what you give up in liquidity.

Deductible medical expenses help only if you itemize and exceed the medical-expense floor, so for many retirees the deduction is small. Two other safeguards matter more. First, if your insurer fails, the state guaranty association continues coverage up to a limit: in Pennsylvania, $300,000 of long-term care benefits (tab 7). Second, buy only a policy you can keep paying for: a policy that lapses after 15 years of premiums, just before you need it, is the worst outcome of all.

Pay your own way

Setting aside money for care

For households with substantial savings, self-funding is often the default plan, and for some it is the right one. The question is not whether you could pay for an average need, but whether your plan survives a long one. The calculator prices a care episode in future dollars and tells you how much to earmark today.

At the defaults — home care at the 2025 national median, starting in 12 years, lasting 3 years, costs rising 3% a year — the episode costs about $358,156, and setting that aside today at a 4% return takes about $210,872. Add a policy paying $200 a day (rising 3% a year) after a 90-day elimination period for up to 3 years, and the policy pays about $300,735, leaving $57,422 out of pocket, about $34,654 in today's money. The question then becomes whether the premiums over those 12 years cost more than the $176,218 difference in what you would need to set aside.

Going furtherHome equity as the reserve

For many retirees, a paid-off home is the long-term care fund. If care moves you out of the house permanently, selling it can pay for years of assisted living or nursing care. Ruth's $380,000 house would cover about three years in a private nursing-home room at the 2025 national median. Naming the house as the reserve, and telling family, keeps everyone's expectations aligned.

Real events and research

Three lessons from real long-term care history

Case study 1Penn Treaty's liquidation (Pennsylvania, 2017)
What happened
On March 1, 2017, the Commonwealth Court of Pennsylvania approved the liquidation of Penn Treaty Network America Insurance Company and its subsidiary American Network Insurance Company. The companies covered about 76,000 policyholders nationwide, more than 98% of them with long-term care insurance. State guaranty associations took over the policies, usually in the state where each policyholder lived at the time; in Pennsylvania, the guaranty association's limit for long-term care benefits is $300,000.
What it teaches
A long-term care policy is a promise that may have to be kept 30 years after purchase. Guaranty associations kept paying claims and guaranteed-renewable policies could not be canceled, but benefits above the state limit were not protected. Check the insurer's strength when you buy, and know your state's limit.
Source
Pennsylvania Insurance Department (2017), consumer brochure Your long-term care insurance company is closing (in liquidation); Washington State Office of the Insurance Commissioner, Penn Treaty liquidation.
Case study 2The look-back grows from 36 to 60 months (Deficit Reduction Act of 2005)
What happened
Section 1917(c)(1)(B)(i) of the Social Security Act sets the look-back date at 36 months before application, "or in the case of any other disposal of assets made on or after the date of enactment of the Deficit Reduction Act of 2005, 60 months." Transfers inside the window create a penalty period equal to the value given away divided by the average monthly private cost of nursing-facility care.
What it teaches
Medicaid planning is long-range planning. Gifts to family, such as giving a house to a child, must be made more than five years before a Medicaid application to avoid a penalty, and they have their own tax and control consequences (INV-092, INV-090).
Source
Social Security Act §1917(c), as published by the Social Security Administration.
Case study 3What federal researchers project (ASPE, 2022)
What happened
Using a microsimulation model, HHS's Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation projected that 56% of Americans turning 65 in 2021–2025 will develop a disability serious enough to require LTSS, that 22% will have one for more than five years, and that 14% will spend at least $100,000 out of pocket on future LTSS, while 55% will use no paid care at all.
What it teaches
The average hides the real problem. Planning for the average ($120,900 of lifetime costs, of which families pay 37%) protects no one; planning should target the long, expensive tail.
Source
ASPE (2022, revised August), Long-term services and supports for older Americans: Risks and financing, research brief.

Decisions

Planning for care

Scenario 1 · Ruth, 68

Ruth lives alone in Pittsburgh; Anna lives in another state. She has a $780,000 IRA, $60,000 in cash and a $380,000 paid-off house. She is healthy. What should her long-term care plan be?

MedicareMedicare pays only for short-term skilled care after a hospital stay, at $217 a day from day 21 to day 100 in 2026. It does not pay for custodial care, which is most of what long stays involve.
Self-fund, and compare insuranceA sound plan. Her house and IRA together could fund even a long stay, and naming them as the reserve protects the rest of her plan. At 68, traditional coverage is expensive and may face premium increases, so a price check is worthwhile but not obligatory. A power of attorney and a health care directive (INV-084) matter as much as the money.
Give away the houseAny gift within 60 months of a Medicaid application triggers a penalty period, and Anna would take over Ruth's low tax basis (26 U.S.C. §1015) instead of the step-up in basis she would get by inheriting (INV-090). Ruth would also lose control of her home.
The principle: decide in advance which assets will pay for care, and make sure the people who will manage it know the plan.
Scenario 2 · Tom and Rachel Harper, 57 and 55

The Harpers have $1.1 million saved and plan to retire at 62–63. Should they buy long-term care coverage now, and if so, what kind?

Self-fund everythingPossible for one long stay, but two long stays, one for each spouse, could consume most of their savings and leave the survivor short. In the calculator, three years of private nursing-home care for Rachel starting at 75 costs about $734,121 in future dollars.
Price coverage nowRight. Coverage requires health underwriting, so pricing it while both are healthy keeps the option open; waiting risks both higher prices and being declined. In the calculator, a $200-a-day policy with 3% inflation protection would pay about $380,962 of that $734,121 stay. Rachel's longer life expectancy makes her the more likely claimant.
The principle: insure the risk that would change the survivor's life, while you can still qualify.

Apply it

What long-term care means for each household

Protect the paycheck first. At 24, Jordan's version of this risk is disability insurance through work (INV-023), which replaces income if she cannot work. Long-term care insurance is decades away.
Think about parents. The Riveras' likelier encounter with long-term care is as caregivers for their own parents (INV-097). Knowing their parents' plans, and documents, now avoids a crisis later.
The caregiver's own care. Monica is Eli's caregiver and has no spouse to care for her. Because insurers decide on health when you apply, pricing a policy at 58, while she is healthy, is worth doing (her 2026 limit on deductible premiums is $1,860), weighed against a savings target she has not yet reached (INV-075). Eli's own lifelong support is a separate plan, built on Medicaid and his trust (INV-084).
Buy while healthy. In their mid-50s and in good health, the Harpers are well placed to price traditional or hybrid coverage, especially for Rachel, who is younger and likely to live longer.
Name the reserve. Ruth's house and part of her IRA can be her care fund. Getting powers of attorney and a health care directive in place, and telling Anna the plan, is the most urgent step.

Exercise 1

At the 2025 national median of $129,575 a year for a private nursing-home room, what would three years cost in today's dollars?

Exercise 2

In 2026, how much coinsurance does a Medicare patient without supplemental coverage pay for days 21 through 100 of a skilled nursing stay, in dollars?

Days 21–100 are 80 days at $217 a day: 80 × $217 = $17,360.
Exercise 3

Illustration: a person gives away $60,000 within the look-back period. If the state's average monthly private nursing-home cost were $10,798 (the 2025 national monthly median for a private room), how many months of Medicaid ineligibility would result?

Penalty months = value transferred ÷ average monthly private cost: $60,000 ÷ $10,798 = 5.56 months. Each state publishes its own divisor.
Exercise 4

Ruth is 68 at the end of 2026. What is the most of a tax-qualified LTC insurance premium she could count as a medical expense for 2026, in dollars?

Rev. Proc. 2025-32 sets $4,960 for attained ages more than 60 but not more than 70: $4,960. It helps only if she itemizes and her medical expenses exceed the floor.
