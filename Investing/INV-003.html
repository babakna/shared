Risk and Return

Every investment trades some certainty for some expected reward. This module defines risk precisely, shows how much of it disappears — and how much does not — as the holding period lengthens, and lets you replay the worst markets of the last century with your own money in them.

The question this module answersWhat does "risky" actually mean, and how much of it can I live with?

Start here

You already manage risk every day

You check the weather before a long drive, you buy insurance on a car, you do not keep all your cash in your wallet. Each of those is a small decision about uncertainty: what could go wrong, how likely it is, and how bad it would be.

Investment risk is the same idea applied to money. When people say stocks are "risky," they mean something specific: the result over any particular year is very uncertain, and it includes a real chance of losing a large amount. When they say Treasury bills (T-bills) are "safe," they mean the dollar result is almost certain — not that it is a good result.

What this module covers

  1. A working definition of risk, and the two numbers used to measure it: volatility and drawdown.
  2. How the holding period changes the odds — with every rolling window since 1928.
  3. A crash replay: put your own amount into 1929, 1973, 2000, 2008 or 2022.
  4. Why a loss needs a bigger gain to recover, and why that makes averages misleading.
  5. The many kinds of risk beyond price swings, including inflation and running out of money.

Two numbers

Volatility and drawdown

Risk (in investing)
What it is
The chance that an investment's actual result differs from what you expected — and especially that it falls short when you need the money.
Used for
Choosing how much of your money to put in each type of investment, and knowing what to expect in bad years.
What it means for you
The risk that matters most is personal: being forced to sell at a low point, or reaching your goal date without enough. Price swings are only dangerous if they coincide with a need.
Volatility (standard deviation)
What it is
How widely returns spread around their average, measured as the standard deviation. For a roughly bell-shaped spread, about two years in three fall within one standard deviation of the average, and about 19 in 20 within two.
Used for
Comparing how bumpy different investments are, and building portfolios (INV-004).
What it means for you
US stocks' annual standard deviation was about 19% around an average of about 12%. So a year anywhere from roughly −7.5% to +31.3% (one standard deviation either side of the average) was "ordinary" — 65 of 98 years were in that range.
Drawdown
What it is
The fall from a previous peak to a later low, in percent. The maximum drawdown is the worst such fall over a period; the recovery time is how long it took to regain the old peak.
Used for
Understanding the worst experience you could have had, which is often what determines whether investors stay the course.
What it means for you
Measured at year-ends, US stocks' deepest fall was about 65% (end of 1928 to end of 1932). Inside those years, the decline was deeper still.
Going furtherWhy volatility is an incomplete measure

Standard deviation treats a surprise gain exactly like a surprise loss, and it assumes returns roughly follow a bell curve. Real markets have "fat tails": extreme years such as 1931 (−44%) happen more often than a bell curve predicts. That is why this course pairs volatility with drawdown, holding-period results and actual crash histories.

Practitioner depthComputing it

Sample standard deviation s = √[Σ(ri − r̄)² ÷ (n − 1)]. Annual volatility from monthly data is usually scaled by √12, which assumes months are independent — an approximation. Downside measures such as semideviation and the Sortino ratio, and tail measures such as value at risk and conditional value at risk, address the asymmetry; INV-057 covers risk-adjusted performance.

Time changes the odds

How often did investors lose money?

A single year of stock returns is close to a coin flip with a thumb on the scale. Over longer periods, good and bad years start to offset. The explorer below computes every rolling window since 1928. Try 1 year, then 5, 10, 15 and 20.

⚠️

Longer is safer, but not safe. The windows overlap, so 79 twenty-year windows contain far fewer than 79 independent observations. After inflation, the worst ten-year stretch (1999–2008) still lost 3.8% a year, and the worst fifteen-year stretch lost money too. Other countries' markets have had much longer droughts. Long holding periods shrink the chance of loss; they do not remove it.

Going furtherThe spread of ending wealth widens with time

The range of annual average returns narrows as the period lengthens, which is what the table shows. But the range of ending dollar amounts widens: over 30 years, the difference between a 6% and a 10% average is the difference between 5.7 and 17.4 times your money. Time reduces the chance of losing money, while increasing how different the good and bad outcomes are in dollars. Both statements are true, and planners argue about which matters more.

Live through it

Replay a market crash

Numbers like "−37%" are easy to read and hard to feel. Pick an episode, enter an amount that means something to you, and choose a mix of stocks and bonds. Then watch what happened, year by year, until it recovered.

1929

$100,000 in stocks at the start of 1929 was worth about $35,000 four years later. It regained $100,000 at the start of 1937 — then fell 35% again that year.

2000

Stocks lost money in 2000, 2001 and 2002. Just as they recovered, 2008 cut them by 37%. After inflation, money invested at the start of 2000 was still below its starting value at the end of 2012.

2008

In 2008 stocks fell 36.6% while 10-year Treasuries gained 20.1%. A 60/40 mix fell 13.9%. That is diversification doing its job (INV-004).

All figures are from calendar-year data (Damodaran, NYU Stern). Peak-to-trough declines within each year were deeper than year-end figures show.

The asymmetry of losses

A 50% loss needs a 100% gain

Lose 50% and $100 becomes $50. To get back to $100, the $50 must double — a 100% gain. Losses and gains are not symmetrical, and the gap grows with the size of the loss.

Average return versus compound return
What it is
The arithmetic average adds the yearly returns and divides by the number of years. The compound (geometric) return is the steady yearly rate that would produce the same ending wealth.
Used for
The compound return describes what an investor actually experienced; the arithmetic average is used to estimate a single future year.
What it means for you
+50% then −50% averages 0%, yet leaves you with 75% of your money. When someone quotes an "average return," ask which kind. For US stocks, 1928–2025: arithmetic 11.9%, compound 10.0%.
compound return ≈ arithmetic average
   − ½ × (standard deviation)²
11.9% − ½(19.4%)² ≈ 10.0%

This gap is sometimes called volatility drag. It is one reason a steadier portfolio can end up closer to a volatile one than their average returns suggest: a 60/40 mix of stocks and 10-year Treasuries had an annual volatility of 12.1% and a compound return of 8.3% over 1928–2025, against stocks' 19.4% and 10.0%.

Quick exercise

A portfolio falls 20%. What percentage gain does it need to get back to where it started?

After a 20% loss, $100 is $80. It needs to grow by $20, which is 20 ÷ 80 = 25%.

The trade-off

More risk has usually meant more return — but not always

Plot each asset's volatility against its compound return and a rough pattern appears: the bumpier assets have mostly paid more. That is the risk premium from INV-001 showing up in the data.

💡

Compensated and uncompensated risk. The market as a whole pays a premium for bearing risk that cannot be diversified away. Risk that can be diversified away — betting on one company, one sector or one collectible — has no reason to be rewarded, because other investors will not pay you for a risk they can avoid for free. INV-004 shows how.

Practitioner depthThe Sharpe ratio, briefly

The Sharpe ratio divides an investment's return above the risk-free rate by its volatility: (R − Rf) ÷ σ. Using the arithmetic means and standard deviations over 1928–2025, stocks score about (11.9 − 3.4) ÷ 19.4 ≈ 0.43, and gold about (7.4 − 3.4) ÷ 21.5 ≈ 0.18. It is a useful comparison of reward per unit of volatility, with all of volatility's limitations; INV-057 covers it and its alternatives.

Real history

Three stress tests

Case study 11929–1937: the Depression and the false recovery
What happened
Calendar-year stock returns: 1929 −8.3%, 1930 −25.1%, 1931 −43.8%, 1932 −8.6%. A dollar at the start of 1929 was worth 35 cents at the start of 1933. Strong years in 1933–1936 brought it back above $1 by the start of 1937 — and 1937 then fell 35.3%.
What it teaches
Recovery can be interrupted. Investors who needed to sell in 1932, or who gave up after 1937, locked in the loss. Meanwhile 10-year Treasuries rose in every one of 1929–1933 except 1931 — the diversification that saved balanced investors.
Source
Damodaran, NYU Stern, annual returns.
Case study 22008: when bonds did their job
What happened
The global financial crisis: stocks −36.6%, home prices −12.0%, Baa corporate bonds −3.4% — and 10-year Treasuries +20.1% as investors fled to safety and interest rates fell.
What it teaches
A 60/40 portfolio fell 13.9%, well under half of stocks' loss. Investors who rebalanced — selling some of the bonds that had risen to buy stocks that had fallen — bought at unusually low prices. Stocks returned 25.9% in 2009 and 14.8% in 2010.
Source
Damodaran, NYU Stern.
Case study 32022: when bonds did not
What happened
With inflation at 6.5% and interest rates rising fast, stocks fell 18.0% and 10-year Treasuries fell 17.8% — their worst year in the dataset. A 60/40 mix fell about 18%, nearly as much as stocks alone.
What it teaches
Bonds protect best against recessions and deflationary scares, when rates fall. They protect poorly against an inflation shock, when rates rise. Treasuries fell alongside stocks in only five calendar years since 1928 (1931, 1941, 1969, 2018 and 2022) — rare, but real.
Source
Damodaran, NYU Stern.

Decisions under stress

Your portfolio just fell. Now what?

Scenario 1 · The Harpers, late 2008 (a rewind)

Imagine the Harpers were 57 and 55 at the end of 2008, their portfolio of 80% stocks and 20% Treasuries down about 25% for the year. Tom wants to "get out until things settle down." What should they do?

Sell everythingThis locks in the loss and requires getting two decisions right — when to leave and when to return. Historically, some of the best days and years have come soon after the worst, and most sellers return late or never.
Hold and rebalanceThe disciplined response: selling some bonds to buy stocks keeps the risk level where they chose it. The honest follow-up question is whether 80% stocks was right with retirement under a decade away — which is a question to settle calmly, not in the middle of a crash (INV-025).
All in on stocksIt might have worked in hindsight, but it is a bet sized by emotion. It would leave them with nothing to spend from if the next few years were bad, which is exactly the risk that matters close to retirement.
The principle: decide your risk level before the crash, and let the crash test your plan rather than rewrite it.
Scenario 2 · Denise

A market drop of 25% happens two years into Denise's career. Her 401(k) balance falls from $12,000 to $9,000. How should she feel about it?

TerribleUnderstandable, but the loss is $3,000 on paper, and her future contributions over 40 years will be many times her current balance.
Indifferent or gladFor a young net buyer of stocks, lower prices mean each month's contribution buys more shares. What matters to her is the price she pays over decades, not this year's balance.
The principle: whether a fall hurts depends on whether you are mostly buying or mostly selling in the years that follow.

Exercise 1

An investment falls 50%. What gain, in percent, is needed to recover?

$100 becomes $50; to reach $100 it must double: 100%.
Exercise 2

A fund gains 10% one year and loses 10% the next. What is the total change over the two years, in percent?

1.10 × 0.90 = 0.99, a total change of −1%. The arithmetic average of +10% and −10% is 0%, but you lost money.
Exercise 3

An asset averages 12% a year with a standard deviation of 19%. Roughly what return marks the top of the "one standard deviation" range, in percent?

12% + 19% = 31%; the bottom is 12% − 19% = −7%. About two years in three fall in that range for a bell-shaped spread.
