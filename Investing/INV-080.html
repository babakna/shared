Pensions: Lump Sum vs Annuity

A pension check for life and a single large payment can be worth exactly the same on paper and very different in your life. This module shows how to put a price on a pension, how plans compute the lump sum they offer, what the federal insurer guarantees, and how to choose.

The question this module answersIf my plan offers me a lump sum instead of a monthly pension, how do I know which is worth more to me?

Start here

A paycheck you cannot outlive, or a pile you control

You already understand the two choices, because you make a smaller version of them every time you compare a monthly payment with a price paid up front. A pension that pays $2,000 a month for life and a single payment of $285,000 are two ways of receiving the same promise. The question is which one is worth more, and to whom.

The lifetime pension protects you against longevity risk, the chance of outliving your money. For most people the ordinary answer is simple: take whichever the plan's rules make more valuable, and if the numbers are close, take the one that fits your life. A person in poor health with no spouse often does better with the lump sum. A healthy person, especially a woman or anyone with a younger spouse, often does better with the lifetime income. What makes the decision hard is that "more valuable" depends on three things you have to estimate: how long you will live, what the lump sum could safely earn, and how the plan converted one into the other.

The first of those is the one people get most wrong. Nobody knows their own lifespan, and the average hides a wide spread. Figure 80.1 shows it using the Social Security Administration's (SSA) latest period life table.

💡

The core idea. A lump sum shifts two risks onto you: that your investments do badly, and that you live a long time. A lifetime pension keeps both risks with the plan. The price of that insurance is hidden inside the conversion rate the plan uses, and this module shows you how to find it.

What this module covers

  1. How to value a pension: present value, weighted by the chance you are alive.
  2. Life expectancy, survivor options and the spousal-consent rule.
  3. How plans compute the lump sum under Internal Revenue Code section 417(e), and why it moves with interest rates.
  4. What the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation (PBGC) guarantees in 2026.
  5. Taxes on each choice, and a step-by-step decision framework.

Put a price on it

A pension is a stream of payments with a question mark on each one

In INV-002 you learned that money received in the future is worth less than money today, because today's money could be earning interest in the meantime. That idea, present value, is the tool for comparing a pension with a lump sum. A pension adds one twist: every payment is conditional. You receive the check for month 300 only if you are alive in month 300.

Formally, what you are weighing is a pension, one of the oldest forms of lifetime income.

Defined benefit pensiona promise of income, not an account
What it is
An employer plan that promises a monthly benefit, usually set by a formula such as 1.5% of final average pay for each year worked. The employer invests the plan's money and bears the investment risk. You have no account balance, only a promised benefit.
Used for
Providing retirement income that lasts for life, often with a survivor benefit for a spouse. Many plans also let you swap the promise for a single payment: the lump-sum option.
What it means for you
The monthly amount on your benefit statement is only half of the picture. To compare it with a lump sum, you need its present value, which depends on your age, your health and interest rates.
Discount ratethe rate that turns future money into today's money
What it is
The yearly return you use to shrink future payments into their value today. A higher rate makes future payments worth less.
Used for
Comparing a stream of payments with a lump sum. The fair rate to use is what the lump sum could earn with similar safety: high-quality bonds, not the stock market.
What it means for you
If you value a guaranteed pension using a stock-market return, the lump sum will almost always look better. That is comparing a guarantee with a gamble.
Value of pension = Σ (monthly benefit) × (chance you are alive to receive it) ÷ (1 + r)t

Start with the calculator you already know. The present value tool below, in its "Lump sum vs payments" mode, treats the payments as certain for a fixed number of years. That is a useful first check, and it shows how strongly the discount rate matters. Its limitation is that a pension does not last a fixed number of years; it lasts a lifetime of unknown length.

The pension calculator below fixes that. It weights every monthly check by the probability that you (and, for a survivor option, your spouse) are alive to receive it. You can value with the Internal Revenue Service's (IRS) 2026 mortality table for pension-plan participants, the table pension actuaries use, or with the SSA table for the general population; plan participants live longer on average. At its default settings, a 65-year-old man with a $2,000-a-month single-life pension is offered $285,000, close to what the IRS rules produced with May 2026 rates (tab 4). Discounted at 5% with the pension-participant table, the pension is worth about $292,600. The rate that makes the two equal is about 5.3%: if he can earn more than 5.3% a year with the safety of the pension, the lump sum wins; if not, the pension does. Switch to the general-population table and the break-even rate falls to about 4.3%, because an average man is expected to collect for fewer years.

Going furtherWhy "break-even age" is the wrong question

A common shortcut divides the lump sum by the yearly pension: $285,000 ÷ $24,000 = 11.9 years, so "the pension wins if you live past about 77." That ignores interest. Money taken as a lump sum keeps earning while the pension checks trickle in, so the true break-even is later. It also treats your lifespan as a single guess. The expected-value method in the calculator uses the whole distribution of lifespans in Figure 80.1, which is what an insurer does.

Practitioner depthThe actuarial formula

For monthly payments B starting after a deferral of d years: PV = 12B · Σm>12d (m/12px) vm/12 / 12, where tpx is the probability a person aged x survives t years and v = 1/(1 + r). The calculator builds tpx from one-year death probabilities qx (IRS Notice 2025-40 tables or the SSA table), assuming a constant force of mortality within each year, pays at the end of each month, and applies any cost-of-living raise once a year. For a joint-and-survivor annuity with survivor fraction s: PV = Bj[ax + s(ay − axy)], where axy uses the probability that both are alive, assuming independent lifetimes.

Married retirees

Single life, or joint and survivor?

Social Security already pays a survivor benefit to a widowed spouse, but a private pension does so only if you choose it. A pension that stops when you die can leave a surviving spouse with a sudden drop in income. Federal law therefore makes the survivor option the default for married participants in most private pension plans.

Qualified joint and survivor annuityQJSA, the default for married participants
What it is
A pension paid for your life, then to your surviving spouse for their life at a set fraction of your benefit. The law requires the survivor fraction to be at least 50% and no more than 100% (26 U.S.C. §417(b)).
Used for
Protecting a spouse's income. In exchange, your own monthly check is usually reduced, because the plan expects to pay for longer.
What it means for you
You cannot quietly waive it. Your spouse must consent in writing, the consent must acknowledge its effect, and it must be witnessed by a plan representative or a notary public (§417(a)(2)). Plans must also offer a qualified optional survivor annuity at a second level: 75% if the default is below 75%, or 50% if the default is 75% or more (§417(g)).

The survivor option is priced by the same arithmetic as everything else in this module: the plan cuts your check by enough to pay for the expected extra years of payments to your spouse. How big a cut is fair depends mostly on how long your spouse is likely to outlive you. Figure 80.2 compares two couples.

For the first couple, the chance that the wife outlives the husband is about 63%, and there is about a 57% chance that at least one of them is still alive when he would be 90. That is why the survivor option is so often worth its price for a husband with a younger wife. Some plans subsidize the survivor option by cutting the check by less than the fair amount; if yours does, the survivor option is a bargain you should rarely give up.

⚠️

"Pension maximization" pitches. A salesperson may suggest taking the higher single-life pension and buying life insurance to protect your spouse instead. That can work, but only if the policy is priced well, stays in force for as long as needed, and pays enough to replace the lost income. Compare the insurance premium with the cost of the survivor cut, using the calculator, before agreeing.

Going furtherOther forms plans may offer

Besides single life and joint and survivor, plans often offer a period certain option (for example, life with 10 years guaranteed, so a beneficiary receives the rest of the 10 years if you die early) and sometimes a "pop-up" joint annuity, where your check rises back to the single-life amount if your spouse dies first. Each option is priced so it is roughly equivalent in value under the plan's assumptions. The one that is worth most to you depends on your own health and your spouse's.

Section 417(e)

The formula behind the offer

A plan cannot pick any lump-sum number it likes. Internal Revenue Code section 417(e)(3) sets a floor: the lump sum must be at least the present value of your benefit calculated with an IRS-specified mortality table and an IRS-specified set of interest rates. Plans may pay more than that minimum, but not less.

Segment ratesthe interest rates in 417(e)
What it is
Three interest rates the IRS publishes each month, based on yields of high-quality corporate bonds. The first segment rate reflects bonds maturing within 5 years, the second bonds maturing in years 5 to 20, and the third bonds maturing after 20 years (26 U.S.C. §430(h)(2)). For 417(e), they are monthly rates, not the 24-month averages used for plan funding (§417(e)(3)(D)).
Used for
Discounting each future pension payment: payments due in the first five years use the first segment rate, and so on. The IRS posts the rates on its "Minimum present value segment rates" page.
What it means for you
When corporate bond yields rise, your lump sum falls, sometimes by a quarter or more, even though your pension did not change. Your plan document says which month's rates it uses (the lookback month) and for how long they apply (the stability period).

The mortality part of the formula uses a table the IRS publishes each year. For distributions with annuity starting dates in stability periods beginning in 2026, it is the "Unisex" table in IRS Notice 2025-40, derived from the tables for pension-plan participants under section 430(h)(3). It is a single table for men and women combined, and that detail matters: because women live longer on average, a unisex table undervalues a woman's pension and overvalues a man's. Other things equal, the lump sum is a better deal for a man and a worse deal for a woman.

Switch the pension calculator (tab 2) to "How the plan sets the lump sum" to run this for your own benefit. If you are younger than the plan's normal retirement age, set "Years until payments start": the lump sum then values a deferred pension, and a larger share of the payments falls into the higher-rate second and third segments, which makes the lump sum even more sensitive to rates.

Practitioner depthLookback months, stability periods and timing

Section 417(e)(3)(C) sets the rate as the segment rates "for the month before the date of the distribution or such other time as the Secretary may by regulations prescribe." That is why plan documents fix a lookback month and a stability period, and why IRS Notice 2025-40 describes its 2026 table as applying to annuity starting dates "during stability periods beginning in 2026." If one month's rates apply for a whole plan year, the lump sum is predictable within that year, and a rise in rates during the year shows you in advance that next year's lump sum will be smaller. Before you choose, your plan must give you a written explanation of the terms and conditions of the survivor annuity forms (§417(a)(3)); ask for the value of each form compared with the single-life annuity.

If the plan fails

What the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation guarantees

PBGCPension Benefit Guaranty Corporation
What it is
A federal corporation created by the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974 (ERISA) to insure private-sector defined benefit pensions. PBGC says it protects the retirement security of about 30 million workers.
Used for
Paying pensions, up to limits set by federal law, when a plan ends without enough money. It does not cover government pensions, and multiemployer (union) plans have a separate, lower guarantee.
What it means for you
Most pensions are well below the limit. If yours is large, or you retire early, check the table: the part above the guarantee could be lost if the plan fails.

PBGC's own guidance explains how to read the table: use the table for the year the plan terminates (or the year the employer entered bankruptcy, if the plan ends during a bankruptcy), and generally the age at which you first receive benefits. Lower amounts apply at younger ages because younger retirees are expected to receive more monthly checks, and lower amounts apply to survivor forms. PBGC also notes that most benefits in PBGC-trusteed plans are below the maximum.

💡

Where plan risk enters the decision. A lump sum removes you from the plan entirely, so plan failure no longer matters to you. For a benefit well under the PBGC limit from a financially healthy plan sponsor, that is a small advantage. For an early retiree with a large benefit from a weak sponsor, it can be decisive. Your plan's annual funding notice shows how well funded it is.

Going furtherWhen your pension is moved to an insurance company

Some employers transfer pension obligations to an insurer by buying a group annuity, a pension risk transfer. Your checks then come from the insurer. Protection behind an insurer comes from state life and health insurance guaranty associations, with limits set by each state for the present value of annuity benefits; in Pennsylvania, for example, individual annuity benefits are protected up to $250,000 in present value. INV-082 explains insurer strength and guaranty limits.

Keep more of it

How each choice is taxed, and a framework for deciding

Pension checks are taxed as ordinary income in the year you receive them (minus any after-tax contributions you made). A lump sum is also ordinary income — all of it, in one year — unless you roll it into an individual retirement account (IRA) or another employer plan, where it keeps growing tax-deferred until you withdraw it.

If you…What happens (IRS Topic 413)
Take the monthly pensionEach check is taxable income when paid. Nothing to roll over.
Direct rollover of the lump sum to an IRAThe plan sends the money straight to the IRA. No tax now and no mandatory withholding.
Take the check yourself, then roll overThe plan must withhold 20% for federal tax. To roll over the full amount within 60 days you must replace the withheld 20% from other money, or that part becomes taxable.
Keep the cashThe whole taxable amount is income this year, often pushing much of it into high brackets. Before 59½, a 10% additional tax may also apply unless an exception fits, such as leaving the employer in or after the year you turn 55 (Internal Revenue Code §72(t)(2)(A)(v)).

A rolled-over lump sum becomes part of your IRA for required minimum distributions at 73 or 75 (INV-081). A pension already in payment counts as its own distribution and needs no separate calculation.

Two further points belong in every decision. First, most private pensions have no cost-of-living adjustment. At 3% inflation, a fixed $2,000 check buys about what $1,100 does today after 20 years (2,000 ÷ 1.0320 = $1,107). Second, you can often combine the choices: take the pension if it is the better deal and invest other savings for growth, or take the lump sum and use part of it to buy an income annuity (INV-082) if an insurer offers more income per dollar than your plan does.

Real rules and real rates

Three stories that shaped the lump-sum decision

Case study 1The IRS tries to stop retiree lump-sum windows, then reverses (2015–2019)
What happened
Some employers offered retirees already receiving pension checks a temporary window to swap their remaining payments for a lump sum. On July 9, 2015, in Notice 2015-49, the Treasury and IRS announced they intended to amend the required minimum distribution regulations so that plans generally could not replace an annuity already being paid with a lump sum. In Notice 2019-18 they announced they no longer intended to make that change, and Notice 2015-49 was superseded.
What it teaches
Lump-sum windows for retirees remain possible. If you receive such an offer, the analysis is the same as in this module, with one extra fact: you already know how the plan's monthly checks have fit your life. Offers are voluntary; declining keeps your pension exactly as it is.
Source
Internal Revenue Service, Notice 2015-49 (2015) and Notice 2019-18 (2019).
Case study 2Rising rates shrank lump sums by about a quarter (2021–2023)
What happened
As interest rates rose in 2022, the IRS 417(e) segment rates rose from 1.16%, 2.72% and 3.10% (December 2021) to 5.77%, 6.14% and 6.19% (October 2023). For a $2,000-a-month single-life pension at 65, our estimate of the minimum lump sum (holding the 2026 IRS table constant) fell from about $372,980 to about $272,042, a drop of 27%. The monthly pension itself did not change at all.
What it teaches
A lump sum is a bond-like asset priced at today's rates. When rates are high, the lump sum is smaller, but it can also be reinvested at those higher rates, so the trade-off is less dramatic than the headline number suggests. Compare the offer with what the same money would buy from an insurer at the same time, not with last year's offer.
Source
IRS, Minimum present value segment rates; IRS Notice 2025-40; lump sums computed in this module (illustrative).
Case study 3What the PBGC limit means for an early retiree (2026 table)
What happened
Federal law caps what PBGC can pay. For single-employer plans ending in 2026, the maximum straight-life guarantee is $7,789.77 a month at 65 but $3,505.40 at 55. A participant who retired at 55 with a $5,000 monthly pension from a plan that later failed could therefore see the guaranteed part fall to about $3,505 a month, depending on the special rules for plan termination and bankruptcy dates.
What it teaches
The guarantee protects the great majority of pensions in full, but its steep age scale means early retirees with large benefits carry real plan risk. For them, the health of the sponsor is part of the lump-sum decision.
Source
PBGC, Maximum monthly guarantee tables, 2026 table and FAQs.

Decisions

Rachel Harper's frozen pension

Scenario 1 · Rachel Harper, 55

Rachel left a hospital system years ago with a frozen pension of $1,450 a month payable at 65, with no cost-of-living raise. The plan now offers her a lump sum of about $109,200 (May 2026 rates). She and Tom have $1.1 million saved and plan to retire around 62–63. What should she do?

Cash for the mortgageThe whole $109,200 would be taxable income in one year, on top of a $260,000 household income, much of it in the 24% bracket or higher; and she is under 59½, having left that employer before 55, so a 10% additional tax would likely apply too. Paying off a mortgage that has only $90,000 left is not worth that tax bill.
Keep the pensionA strong choice. The unisex table undervalues a woman's pension, and her implied return from keeping it is about 6.1% a year, guaranteed, which safe bonds do not currently offer. It also provides a floor alongside Social Security. The main risks: no inflation protection, and plan failure, although $1,450 is far below the PBGC limit at 65.
Direct rollover and investReasonable if she wants control, flexibility or money for heirs, and it avoids tax now. But she must beat about 6.1% a year to come out ahead in expectation, which means taking stock-market risk to replace a guarantee. The Harpers already have $1.1 million exposed to markets.
The principle: when the lump sum is below the value of the pension at safe interest rates, you are being paid to keep the pension.
Scenario 2 · Rachel at 65, choosing a form

Ten years later Rachel starts the pension. Tom is 67. The plan offers single life at $1,450, or joint and 50% survivor at a 6% reduction. Tom has his own 401(k)s and Social Security. Which form?

Single lifeDefensible. Figure 80.2 shows the fair cut for a 50% survivor benefit for this couple is only about 5.6%, so a 6% cut is roughly fair, not a subsidy. If Tom's own income is ample, the extra $87 a month now may be more useful to them. Tom would need to sign a notarized consent.
Joint and 50%Also defensible. The cost is small because Tom is older, and it hedges the chance (roughly one in three) that he outlives her. It matters most if Tom's own income would be thin without hers.
The principle: price survivor protection like insurance, against how long the survivor is likely to need it and what else they will have.

What pensions mean for each household

Her "pension" is the match. Denise's employer offers a 401(k) match rather than a pension. If a future employer offers a pension, she should learn its vesting schedule; a lump sum of a small benefit after a job change should be rolled directly to an IRA, never cashed.
Survivor protection comes from elsewhere. Without pensions, the Riveras' survivor income rests on Social Security survivor benefits for Sofia, Leo and the surviving parent, plus life insurance (INV-023).
Build the floor herself. With no pension, Maya's only lifetime income will be Social Security: $2,300 a month at 67 or $2,852 at 70, and her record will also pay Eli $1,150 while she lives and $1,725 after. Waiting from 67 to 70 buys $552 a month of inflation-adjusted income for life for $82,800 of forgone checks, the cheapest pension-like income available to her (INV-078).
Keep Rachel's pension, probably. At May 2026 rates, the offered lump sum is below the pension's value to her at any safe rate under about 6.1%. At 65, the survivor choice costs little because Tom is older.
She is living the survivor option. When Walter died, Ruth kept the higher of their two Social Security benefits, $2,900 a month: a joint and survivor annuity in all but name. If she wants more guaranteed income, INV-082 shows how an income annuity compares.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

Ignoring mortality, what is the present value of $1,500 a month ($18,000 a year, paid at year-end) for 20 years at a 5% discount rate, in dollars?

1.0520 = 2.6533, so 1.05−20 = 0.3769. PV = 18,000 × (1 − 0.3769) ÷ 0.05 = 18,000 × 12.462 = $224,320.
Exercise 2

A plan offers $300,000 or $2,000 a month. Using the simple (no-interest) shortcut, after how many years of checks does the pension pay back the lump sum?

$2,000 × 12 = $24,000 a year; $300,000 ÷ $24,000 = 12.5 years. With interest, the true break-even is later, because the lump sum would keep earning in the meantime.
Exercise 3

A 65-year-old's single-employer plan fails in 2026. The plan promised a straight-life pension of $9,000 a month. Using the 2026 PBGC maximum at 65, how much of the monthly benefit is above the guarantee, in dollars?

The 2026 maximum straight-life guarantee at 65 is $7,789.77 a month. $9,000 − $7,789.77 = $1,210.23 a month is above the limit and could be lost.
Exercise 4

A plan pays a $250,000 lump sum directly to you instead of by direct rollover. How much must it withhold for federal tax, in dollars?

Mandatory withholding is 20%: $250,000 × 0.20 = $50,000. You receive $200,000; to roll over the full $250,000 within 60 days, you must add $50,000 from other money.
