Stocks

A share of stock is a small piece of ownership in a business. Over the 98 years of data used in this course, US stocks turned $1 of buying power into more than $600. This module explains what you actually own, how the stock market is divided by size, sector and country, and where that return came from — so you can judge what to expect next.

The question this module answersWhat am I buying when I buy stocks, and what drives the return I can expect?

Start here

Owning a slice, not renting a ticker

Most people meet stocks as prices on a screen that jump around every second. It is natural to think of them as numbers to guess. But behind every price is a company that sells things, pays workers, earns profits and, often, pays some of those profits to its owners. Owning a share makes you one of those owners.

Stockalso called a share or equity
What it is
A unit of ownership in a corporation. The Securities and Exchange Commission's (SEC's) investor site, Investor.gov, describes stocks as a security that "gives stockholders a share of ownership in a company." If a company has 1,000,000 shares and you own 100, you own 0.01% of it: of its profits, its assets and its votes.
Used for
Companies sell shares to raise money; investors buy them to share in the company's future profits, through dividends and a rising share price.
What it means for you
Your return depends on how the business does and on what other investors will pay for it. Owners are paid last, after lenders, which is why stocks are riskier than bonds and have historically earned more.

What you own

Common stock, preferred stock and your rights

Common stock
What it is
The ordinary form of ownership. The SEC's investor site: common stock "entitles owners to vote at shareholder meetings and receive dividends."
Used for
Almost all stock investing. When people say "stocks," and when index funds buy "the market," they mean common stock.
What it means for you
Unlimited upside, and last in line if the company fails. As a shareholder of a corporation you cannot lose more than you paid for your shares (unless you borrowed to buy them, INV-005).
Preferred stock
What it is
A hybrid between a bond and a stock. Per the SEC's investor site, preferred stockholders "usually don't have voting rights but they receive dividend payments before common stockholders do," and have priority over common stockholders in a liquidation.
Used for
Income investing. Many preferreds pay a fixed dividend, have no maturity date, and may be redeemed by the issuer.
What it means for you
You get bond-like income without a bond's legal claim to repayment. Preferreds tend to fall when interest rates rise, like long bonds, and when the issuer is in trouble, like stocks.

Corporate actions you will see

  • Dividends: cash paid per share, usually quarterly, at the board's discretion; they can be raised, cut or stopped.
  • Stock splits: more shares at a lower price, with no change in value. The SEC's example: 100 shares at $100 become 200 shares at $50 after a two-for-one split.
  • Share buybacks: the company buys back its own shares, so each remaining share owns a bigger slice (INV-006).
  • Voting: shareholders elect the board and vote on major changes, usually by proxy. Some companies have several share classes with different voting rights, which is why some tickers have an "A" and a "C" share.
Going furtherGrowth, value and income stocks

The SEC's investor site describes growth stocks as companies whose earnings grow faster than the market average and which "rarely pay dividends"; income stocks as those paying dividends consistently, such as established utilities; and value stocks as those with low price-to-earnings ratios, which may have "fallen out of favor with investors." The labels describe style, not quality; INV-046 examines the evidence on whether any style earns more.

Large, mid and small

Market capitalization and a concentrated market

Market capitalization is share price times shares outstanding: Lakeside Coffee's $48 × 50 million = $2.4 billion. Stocks are grouped by it into large-cap, mid-cap and small-cap, with "microcap" for the smallest. There is no official cutoff; each index provider sets and periodically moves its own bands, so "small" at one fund company may be "mid" at another.

Most broad indexes weight each company by its market value, so the biggest companies dominate. As of August 31, 2026, the S&P 500 had 503 constituents (some companies have two share classes) worth a combined $68.8 trillion, covering about 80% of the available US market value.

Concentration cuts both ways. An index fund automatically owns the winners that grew huge (Bessembinder's finding in INV-004 was that a few big winners account for all of the market's net wealth creation). But it also means a "diversified" index fund depends heavily on a handful of firms, most of them in one sector.

Going furtherSmall companies

Small-company stocks are more volatile, trade less, and are more often unprofitable. Whether they earn a reliable premium over large companies is debated; INV-048 on factor investing reviews the research. A total-market index fund holds small and mid caps in proportion to their size, so you own them without making a separate bet.

What the companies do

The 11 GICS sectors

GICSGlobal Industry Classification Standard
What it is
A classification developed in 1999 by S&P Dow Jones Indices and MSCI. It has four levels: 11 sectors, 25 industry groups, 74 industries and 163 sub-industries.
Used for
Sorting companies for indexes, sector funds and research, and for measuring how concentrated a portfolio is.
What it means for you
Check your portfolio's sector mix against the market's. Owning 25 stocks in one sector, as the Harpers do, is closer to one bet than to 25.

Sector boundaries are not permanent. Communication Services, for example, includes companies producing interactive games and platforms for content and information, which is why several large internet companies sit there rather than in Information Technology. When you compare funds, look at the sector breakdown on the fact sheet rather than the fund's name.

Going furtherSectors and the economy

Consumer Staples and Utilities sell things people buy in any economy, so their profits tend to be steadier; Consumer Discretionary, Industrials and Materials depend more on the business cycle. Sector funds let you tilt toward a sector, but they concentrate risk and have been a common way for investors to chase recent winners (INV-060 on behavioral biases).

Geography

US versus international stocks

A US investor who owns only US stocks is making a large, concentrated bet on one country, even if it is the largest. Most investors do it anyway — home bias — because familiar companies feel safer and because US stocks have been the long-run winner in the data most Americans see. INV-004 showed what a single-country bet cost Japanese investors after 1989.

International stocksdeveloped and emerging markets
What it is
Shares of companies based outside the US. Index providers sort countries into "developed" and "emerging" markets; the MSCI ACWI covered 23 developed and 24 emerging markets in August 2026.
Used for
Diversifying across economies, currencies and valuation levels.
What it means for you
You take on currency risk: when the dollar strengthens, foreign returns shrink in dollars, and vice versa. INV-054 covers how much international exposure is reasonable.
Going furtherThe case each way

Arguments commonly made for more US: many large US companies sell worldwide; US markets are deep and closely regulated; international funds often cost a little more. For more international: no country leads forever; valuations differ across markets (INV-006 showed how starting valuation matters); a global index is the neutral, market-weighted starting point. Holding the world roughly in proportion to its market value is the default that requires no forecast.

The building blocks

Dividends, earnings growth and valuation

A stock market's return over any period can be split into parts. Income arrives as dividends. The price rises if earnings grow — partly real growth, partly inflation — or if investors become willing to pay more for each dollar of earnings (valuation change).

Total return ≈ dividend yield + real earnings growth + inflation + change in valuation

Robert Shiller's data on the S&P Composite (the S&P 500 and its predecessors) let us measure each part from December 1928 to December 2025, 97 years:

Computed from Shiller, ie_data.xls (shillerdata.com, September 2026): prices, dividends, earnings and CPI for December of each year. Real price growth of 2.87% = real earnings growth and the rise in the trailing P/E, compounded; dividends contribute the rest of the real total return. Damodaran's series used elsewhere in this course, which uses year-end prices rather than monthly averages, gives 9.7% a year nominal for 1929–2025.

Now apply the same building blocks to today. In December 2025 the S&P 500's dividend yield was about 1.16%, far below its 97-year average of 3.62%, and in September 2026 the CAPE ratio was 40.6, higher than every year-end reading in the Shiller record except December 1999 (44.2). Companies also return cash through buybacks (INV-006), which do not show up in the dividend yield. But the arithmetic is unforgiving: if valuations merely stay where they are, the return is dividends plus earnings growth; if they fall toward historical levels, they subtract.

With the defaults — a 1.16% dividend yield, 2.3% real earnings growth, 2.5% inflation and an unchanged CAPE of 40.6 — the estimate is about 6.0% a year. If the CAPE drifted down to 25 over ten years, valuation would subtract about 4.7 points a year and the estimate would be about 1.1%. If it fell to the long-run median of 16.6 used in INV-006, the ten-year estimate would be about −3.0% a year. None of these is a forecast; they show how much of a decade's return depends on something no one can predict.

Practitioner depthWhy the pieces are approximate

The exact identity is (1 + price growth) = (1 + earnings growth) × (1 + P/E change), and total return adds reinvested dividends each period. Shiller's prices are monthly averages, so his total return differs slightly from year-end series. Reported earnings are noisy: in 2008–2009 write-offs collapsed them, which is why Shiller's CAPE uses a ten-year average of real earnings. Buybacks move cash from the dividend column into the earnings-per-share-growth column, so "dividend yield plus growth" should be read together, not separately.

When valuation took over

Three periods, one lesson

Case study 11966–1981: growing businesses, shrinking prices
What happened
From December 1965 to December 1981, S&P earnings per share grew 7.0% a year, the same as inflation. But the CAPE fell from 23.7 to 7.8, subtracting almost 7 points a year from prices. Real total return: −0.97% a year for 16 years (Damodaran's series gives the same figure for 1966–1981).
What it teaches
Businesses can keep pace with inflation while their shareholders lose ground, if investors start the period paying high prices and end it paying low ones.
Source
Computed from Shiller, ie_data.xls; Damodaran, NYU Stern (2026).
Case study 21982–1999: the great re-rating
What happened
From a CAPE of 7.8 at the end of 1981 to 44.2 at the end of 1999, rising valuations added about 10 points a year. Earnings grew 6.6% a year nominally. Real total return: 14.6% a year for 18 years (Damodaran, 1982–1999).
What it teaches
Much of the best stretch in US history came from valuations rising from very low to record levels. That boost cannot repeat from a high starting point; investors who expected those returns to continue were disappointed.
Source
Computed from Shiller, ie_data.xls; Damodaran, NYU Stern (2026).
Case study 32000–2009: the lost decade
What happened
Starting from the record CAPE of 44.2, US stocks lost 3.4% a year after inflation from 2000 through 2009 (Damodaran). Earnings grew only 0.6% a year from December 1999 to December 2009, and the CAPE fell to 20.3.
What it teaches
Starting valuation is one of the few measures that has told investors something about the following decade (INV-006 plots the relationship). It says nothing about next year. The sensible response is not to abandon stocks but to diversify across countries and assets and to plan for a range of returns.
Source
Computed from Shiller, ie_data.xls; Damodaran, NYU Stern (2026).

Decisions

Owning stocks sensibly

Scenario 1 · The Harpers

The Harpers' taxable account holds 25 technology stocks worth $220,000, much of it large gains. Five years from retirement, they ask whether they are diversified.

YesTwenty-five stocks in one sector behave much like one sector fund. Information technology was already 31% of world stocks; their account is 100%.
Diversify over timeRight. Selling in stages, starting with lots that have the smallest gains, and directing new money elsewhere reduces the bet without a single large tax bill. INV-035 (cost basis), INV-037 (tax-gain harvesting) and INV-039 (charitable strategies) supply the tools.
Sell all nowIt solves the risk but may push a large gain into one tax year. The goal is the same; the path can be cheaper.
The principle: count sectors and countries, not tickers.
Scenario 2 · Denise

Denise has $1,000 to invest outside her 401(k) and wants to buy shares of three companies she likes.

Three stocksKnowing a product is not knowing a stock's price relative to its future profits. Bessembinder found that most individual US stocks lagged Treasury bills over their lifetimes (INV-004).
Fund firstRight. With fractional shares (INV-005), $1,000 buys a slice of thousands of companies. Her bigger wins are still the 401(k) match and her 6.8% loans.
The principle: own the market first; make individual bets, if at all, with money you can afford to learn from.
Scenario 3 · Ruth

Ruth wants more income and is considering moving part of her individual retirement account (IRA) into high-dividend stocks and preferred stock.

Chase dividendsA $1 dividend lowers the share price by about $1; it is not free money. Dividends can be cut, and a high-dividend portfolio can end up concentrated in a few sectors. Preferreds, with fixed payments and no maturity, are sensitive to interest rates like long bonds.
Total returnRight. Inside an IRA, dividends and share sales are taxed the same way when withdrawn, so there is no tax reason to prefer dividends. INV-047 and INV-076 cover dividend investing and withdrawal strategies.
The principle: spend from total return; do not let the search for yield decide your allocation.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A company has 1,000,000 shares. You own 100. What percentage of the company do you own?

100 ÷ 1,000,000 = 0.0001 = 0.01%.
Exercise 2

Dividend yield 1.16%, real earnings growth 2.3%, inflation 2.5%, and valuation unchanged. Roughly what nominal annual return does the building-block formula give, in percent?

Price growth: 1.023 × 1.025 − 1 = 4.86%. Add the dividend yield: 4.86 + 1.16 = 6.0%.
Exercise 3

If the CAPE falls from 40.6 to 25 over 10 years, what is the valuation effect per year, in percent?

(25 ÷ 40.6)0.1 = 0.953, so about −4.7% a year, enough to wipe out most of the dividend and growth.
Exercise 4

Lakeside Coffee's shares cost $48 and it earns $2.40 per share. What is its price-to-earnings ratio?

48 ÷ 2.40 = 20. Its earnings yield is the inverse, 5.0%.
