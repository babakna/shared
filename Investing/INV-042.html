Tax-Aware Withdrawals

In retirement you choose, every year, which account pays the bills: taxable savings, tax-deferred individual retirement accounts (IRAs) and 401(k)s, or Roth accounts. The same spending can cost very different amounts of tax depending on that choice, and on how your Social Security gets pulled into taxable income along the way.

The question this module answersIn retirement, which account should I spend from first, and how much should I take from each?

Start here

Spending the same money, paying different tax

While you work, taxes follow your paycheck. In retirement you have more control: your taxable income is largely whatever you decide to withdraw from tax-deferred accounts, plus Social Security and investment income.

For retirees with most of their savings in one kind of account, there is little to choose; the order barely matters. It matters for people with meaningful balances in two or three account types, such as the Harpers, and for people whose Social Security sits right where extra income makes it taxable, such as Ruth. For them, the familiar rule of thumb — spend taxable savings first, then tax-deferred, then Roth — is a reasonable default but often not the best.

The choices

Conventional, bracket-based and proportional

Conventional withdrawal ordertaxable, then tax-deferred, then Roth
What it is
Spend the taxable account until it is gone, then tax-deferred accounts, and leave Roth money for last.
Used for
Letting tax-advantaged accounts grow as long as possible. It is the default advice of many financial institutions.
What it means for you
Taxable income is very low in the early years and much higher later, when required distributions and Social Security arrive together. Low brackets in the early years go unused.
Bracket-based orderfill a bracket from tax-deferred money each year
What it is
Each year, withdraw from tax-deferred accounts up to the top of a chosen low bracket (converting any amount you do not need to Roth), then meet the rest of your spending from taxable and Roth money.
Used for
Evening out taxable income over the whole retirement so that more of it is taxed at low rates.
What it means for you
You may pay more tax early and less later. It combines naturally with Roth conversions (INV-038).
Proportional order
What it is
Each year, each account pays its share of spending in proportion to its balance.
Used for
A simple rule that spreads taxable income evenly without yearly bracket calculations.
What it means for you
Usually better than the conventional order, usually not quite as good as a well-tuned bracket strategy; easy to automate.
Going furtherWhat the research found

The intuition comes from the after-tax view in INV-036: you effectively own (1 − t) of a tax-deferred account, and that share grows tax-free, so with one flat tax rate for life the order of withdrawals would not matter. With progressive brackets it does. Cook, Meyer and Reichenstein (2015, Financial Analysts Journal) studied retirees with at least two kinds of account and found that more tax-efficient strategies "can add more than three years to the portfolio's longevity relative to the strategy suggested by the conventional wisdom" of taxable first, tax-deferred second and tax-exempt last. The size of the gain depends on each household's balances and income.

Up to 85% of benefits

Provisional income and the two thresholds

Provisional incomesometimes called combined income
What it is
Your other income (everything in adjusted gross income (AGI) apart from Social Security, plus tax-exempt interest) plus half of your Social Security benefits. IRS Publication 915's worksheet compares it with two base amounts.
Used for
Deciding how much of your Social Security is taxable: none, up to 50%, or up to 85%.
What it means for you
Tax-exempt municipal bond interest counts. So does every IRA withdrawal, Roth conversion and capital gain.
Provisional incomeSingle, head of householdMarried filing jointlyTaxable share of benefits
Below the first baseup to $25,000up to $32,000None
Between the bases$25,000–$34,000$32,000–$44,000Up to 50%
Above the second baseover $34,000over $44,000Up to 85%

Sources: IRS Publication 915 (Worksheet 1); 26 U.S.C. §86. The dollar thresholds are written into the statute with no inflation adjustment; the 50% tier dates from 1983 (P.L. 98-21) and the 85% tier from 1993 (P.L. 103-66). Married people filing separately who lived together have a zero base.

Provisional income = other income + tax-exempt interest + ½ × benefits
Taxable benefits = lesser of { 85% × benefits ; A + 85% × (PI − second base) }

Here PI is provisional income and A is half of the amount by which PI exceeds the first base, capped at half the benefits and at $4,500 ($6,000 joint); below the second base, the taxable amount is simply A. Publication 915's Worksheet 1 walks through it line by line.

Ruth's 2026 numbers: $30,000 IRA withdrawal + $2,400 interest = $32,400 of other income; half her $34,800 benefit is $17,400; provisional income $49,800. Following Publication 915, $17,930 of her benefits is taxable — 51.5% of them. Her AGI is $50,330.

The tax torpedo

While benefits are being pulled into taxable income, each extra dollar of IRA income adds $1.50 or $1.85 of taxable income. Ruth is in the 12% bracket, but her next dollar of IRA income costs 22.2 cents. The map of her 2026 marginal federal rates, from the calculator:

Going furtherWhy the torpedo grows over time

Social Security benefits rise with inflation each year, and so do most retirees' other incomes, but the $25,000/$34,000 and $32,000/$44,000 thresholds do not. Every year more retirees cross them. The senior deduction of 2025–2028 lowers taxable income but, after 2028 under current law, disappears; Ruth's zero-rate band then shrinks from about $15,800 to $12,200 of IRA withdrawals.

When the law decides for you

Required minimum distributions shape the whole plan

From age 73 (or 75 for people born after 1959), the law requires a minimum yearly withdrawal from tax-deferred accounts: the prior year-end balance divided by a factor from the Uniform Lifetime Table in IRS Publication 590-B. The factor falls with age, so the required share rises from about 3.8% at 73 to 8.2% at 90.

Large RMDs arriving on top of Social Security are what push many retirees into higher brackets and IRMAA tiers in their late seventies and eighties. The two main tools for shrinking them are drawing from tax-deferred accounts earlier than required (the bracket-based order) and Roth conversions (INV-038). A third, for people who give to charity, is the qualified charitable distribution, which counts toward the RMD but is excluded from income (INV-039).

Going furtherThe surviving spouse

When one spouse dies, the survivor typically keeps the larger Social Security benefit and inherits the IRAs, so income falls only modestly — but the survivor soon files as a single taxpayer, with brackets roughly half as wide and a single IRMAA table. Ruth has lived this since Walter died in 2023. For married couples, drawing more from tax-deferred accounts while both are alive and filing jointly is often cheaper than leaving it for the survivor.

Two lessons. Paying more tax can leave more wealth. Ruth's bracket-based plan pays about $15,500 more federal tax over her life than the conventional order but leaves about $30,200 more, because it pays at 12% (really 22.2%) what her daughter would otherwise pay at 24%, and moves money into her Roth. No order wins everywhere. For the Harpers at these defaults, the bracket-based order leads at every age, even though proportional withdrawals pay less lifetime tax ($74,577 against $86,157). The lead is narrow and fragile: at a 2% real return proportional withdrawals end up ahead by 95, and if their son Ethan would pay 0% on inherited pre-tax money, the conventional and proportional orders leave more. The right answer depends on balances, spending, Social Security timing, heirs, and how long you live.

Beyond the order

Five more ways to lower retirement taxes

  1. 0% capital gains in low-income years. For 2026, long-term gains fit at 0% under $49,450 of taxable income (single) or $98,900 (joint); taxable-account withdrawals in early retirement can realize gains for free (INV-037). Remember the Social Security effect.
  2. The senior deduction, 2025–2028. $6,000 per person 65 or older, reduced by 6% of modified AGI above $75,000 ($150,000 joint). Keeping modified AGI under those levels keeps the full deduction; above them, each dollar costs an extra 6% of your bracket rate.
  3. Medicare IRMAA. Withdrawals two years earlier set this year's premiums; a 2026 single return with MAGI above $109,000 (joint $218,000) triggers the first surcharge tier (INV-038).
  4. Qualified charitable distributions. From 70½, up to $111,000 per person in 2026 can go from an IRA to charity, excluded from income and counted toward RMDs (INV-039).
  5. State tax. Pennsylvania does not tax IRA withdrawals after retirement age; North Carolina does. The state treatment can favor one account over another (INV-041).

Research and law

Three sources that shape withdrawal planning

Case study 1The conventional order, tested
What happened
Kirsten Cook, William Meyer and William Reichenstein examined retirees with at least two of taxable, tax-deferred and tax-exempt accounts. They showed that the conventional order is not optimal under progressive tax brackets, and that more tax-efficient strategies can add more than three years to a portfolio's longevity.
What it teaches
The value comes from using low brackets that the conventional order leaves empty. The size of the benefit varies with each household's balances and income.
Source
Cook, K. A., Meyer, W., & Reichenstein, W. (2015). Tax-efficient withdrawal strategies. Financial Analysts Journal, 71(2), 16–29.
Case study 2How Social Security became taxable
What happened
The Social Security Amendments of 1983 made up to half of benefits taxable above $25,000 (single) or $32,000 (joint) of provisional income. In 1993, Congress added a second tier: up to 85% taxable above $34,000 or $44,000. The dollar thresholds in section 86 have never been adjusted for inflation.
What it teaches
A tax once aimed at higher-income retirees now reaches middle-income ones like Ruth, and the torpedo zone falls squarely on typical IRA withdrawal amounts.
Source
26 U.S.C. §86, as added by Public Law 98-21 (1983) and amended by Public Law 103-66 (1993); IRS Publication 915.
Case study 3A temporary deduction with a phase-out
What happened
Public Law 119-21 added a $6,000 deduction for each taxpayer or spouse 65 or older for tax years beginning before 2029, reduced by 6% of modified AGI above $75,000 ($150,000 joint). It is available whether or not you itemize.
What it teaches
For 2025–2028, withdrawals that keep modified AGI below the phase-out preserve a valuable deduction; those in the phase-out range carry an extra 6% of the bracket rate. Plans that stretch beyond 2028 should not count on it.
Source
Public Law 119-21, section 70103; IRS, "Working Families Tax Cuts: Tax deductions for working Americans and seniors."

Scenario 1 · Ruth

Ruth needs about $29,200 a year from savings on top of Social Security. Her CDs hold $60,000. Should she spend the CDs first, as her bank suggests?

CDs firstShe would pay no federal tax for two years but waste two years of low brackets, and later RMDs would be larger. In the simulator this leaves about $30,000 less after tax at 95.
Fill the 12% bracket from the IRARight, for her numbers. It keeps her CDs as an emergency reserve, shrinks future RMDs, and moves money into a Roth at an effective 22.2% instead of leaving it to be taxed at Anna's rate. Pennsylvania does not tax the withdrawals.
The principle: use low brackets every year; a zero-tax year is not automatically a good year.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers' first retirement years

Retired at 62 and 60, before Social Security, the Harpers need $90,000 a year. Their taxable account holds $220,000 of technology stocks with a 55% basis; their 401(k)s hold $820,000. Which account should pay?

Taxable onlyVery low tax now, and diversifies the stocks, but leaves the 10% and 12% brackets empty and the 401(k)s growing toward large RMDs.
401(k)s onlyFills low brackets, but leaves concentrated stock unsold and gives up 0% gains room.
A blendRight. Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A retiree has $20,000 of IRA withdrawals and interest and $30,000 of Social Security. What is their provisional income, in dollars?

Other income plus half of benefits: $20,000 + $15,000 = $35,000.
Exercise 2

A single retiree has $20,000 of Social Security and $20,000 of other income. How much of the benefit is taxable, in dollars?

Provisional income = $20,000 + $10,000 = $30,000, which is $5,000 over the $25,000 base but under $34,000. Taxable = 50% × $5,000 = $2,500 (less than half the benefits, so no cap applies).
Exercise 3

An IRA owner turning 75 had $500,000 at the end of the prior year. What is the RMD, in dollars?

$500,000 ÷ 24.6 = $20,325.20.
Exercise 4

A retiree is in the 22% bracket, and each extra dollar of IRA income also makes $0.85 of Social Security taxable. What is the marginal rate on that dollar, in percent?

0.22 × 1.85 = 40.7% — the heart of the tax torpedo.
