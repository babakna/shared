Charitable Legacy

Leaving something to causes you care about can be as simple as one line in a will or one name on a beneficiary form. Which asset you use, and whether you give at death or through a trust that pays you first, can change how much reaches your family, how much reaches the charity, and how much reaches the IRS.

The question this module answersIf I want to leave money to charity, which assets and which structure do the most good for both the charity and my family?

Start here

The same gift, made smarter

Many people leave a set amount to their church, school or a favorite charity in their will. That works. But the will usually pays the gift from whatever is in the estate — cash, investments, the house — while the IRA passes by beneficiary form to the children.

Flip it, and the family often comes out ahead with the charity receiving exactly the same amount. A charity pays no income tax on money it receives from an IRA. Children do: every dollar they withdraw from an inherited traditional IRA is taxable income (INV-091). Stocks and a house, on the other hand, reach children with a stepped-up basis and little or no tax (INV-090). So the tax-efficient order is to give charity the retirement money and the family everything else.

What this module covers

  1. Bequests: the ways to leave money to charity at death, and the unlimited estate-tax deduction.
  2. Retirement accounts to charity, at death and through qualified charitable distributions while living.
  3. Charitable remainder trusts: income for you, the rest to charity, and no tax on selling appreciated assets inside.
  4. Charitable lead trusts: the reverse, for families facing estate tax.
  5. Donor-advised funds and private foundations as a family's giving legacy.

Giving at death

Four ways to leave money to charity

Charitable bequest
What it is
A gift to a charity that takes effect at death, made through a will, a revocable trust, a beneficiary designation or a transfer-on-death registration.
Used for
Supporting causes without giving up any money during life. The gift can be changed at any time until death.
What it means for you
It costs nothing today and keeps your options open if your own needs change. Bequests to qualified charities are deducted in full from a taxable estate for estate tax (IRC 2055), and Pennsylvania's inheritance tax exempts charitable organizations.

Fixed amount or percentage? A fixed dollar bequest can crowd out family if the estate shrinks, for example after years of long-term care. A percentage of the estate, or of one account, grows and shrinks with it. Many people leave a percentage of the residue, which is what remains after specific gifts, debts and expenses.

Going furtherIncome-tax deductions for gifts during life, 2026

Bequests produce an estate-tax deduction, not an income-tax deduction. Gifts during life can produce an income-tax deduction, and the rules changed for 2026. Itemizers may deduct cash gifts to public charities up to 60% of adjusted gross income and appreciated long-term property generally up to 30% (IRC 170(b)); beginning in 2026 only the amount above 0.5% of adjusted gross income is deductible. Non-itemizers may deduct up to $1,000 ($2,000 married filing jointly) of cash gifts to qualifying public charities, not supporting organizations or donor-advised funds (IRC 170(p); IRS Publication 505, Topic 506). Strategies for lifetime giving — bunching, gifts of appreciated shares, qualified charitable distributions — are the subject of INV-039.

The most tax-efficient gift

Leave the IRA to charity, the rest to the family

A traditional IRA is income in respect of a decedent: it never gets a step-up, and heirs pay income tax as they withdraw. A charity that inherits the same dollars pays no income tax. That asymmetry makes pre-tax retirement accounts the best assets to leave to charity, and the worst to leave to someone in a high bracket.

While living: qualified charitable distributions

Qualified charitable distribution (QCD)from age 70½
What it is
A transfer made directly from an IRA to a charity by an owner aged 70½ or older. It is excluded from income and counts toward required minimum distributions.
Used for
Giving from pre-tax money without the income ever appearing on the return, which also helps anyone who takes the standard deduction.
What it means for you
For 2026 the limit is $111,000 per person, and a one-time QCD of up to $55,000 may go to a charitable remainder trust or charitable gift annuity (IRS Notice 2025-67). Donor-advised funds and supporting organizations cannot receive QCDs, and most private foundations do not qualify (IRC 408(d)(8)(B)).
Practitioner depthGetting the IRA gift right

Name the charity as a percentage beneficiary of the IRA (for example "25% to [charity], 75% to Anna"), not in the will. The charity's share should be separated from individual beneficiaries' shares as the account is divided, so it does not affect the individuals' distribution rules (INV-091). An estate that names charity through a will but lets the IRA fall into the estate turns tax-free charitable money into a messier, slower process. A donor-advised fund can also be named as an IRA beneficiary; confirm with the sponsor.

Income now, charity later

Charitable remainder trusts

A charitable remainder trust (CRT) is for people who want to give an appreciated asset to charity eventually but still need the income from it. You transfer the asset to an irrevocable trust. The trust pays you (or you and a spouse) an amount each year for life or for up to 20 years. At the end, what remains goes to charity.

Charitable remainder trustIRC 664
What it is
An irrevocable trust paying at least one non-charitable beneficiary between 5% and 50% a year, for life or a term of up to 20 years, with the remainder to charity. The remainder must be worth at least 10% of the starting value. A CRAT (annuity trust) pays a fixed dollar amount; a CRUT (unitrust) pays a fixed percentage of the trust's value, recalculated every year.
Used for
Turning a concentrated, low-basis holding into a diversified income stream without paying capital gains tax at the sale, while committing the remainder to charity.
What it means for you
You get a partial income-tax deduction for the present value of the charity's remainder, and income for years. Your heirs get nothing from the trust assets, and you cannot take it back.

Why the sale inside is tax-free. The trust takes your carryover basis, but as a tax-exempt trust it can sell the stock and reinvest the whole value without paying tax at that moment. The gain is not forgiven: payments to you carry out the trust's income in a fixed order — ordinary income first, then capital gains, then other income, then return of principal (IRS, Charitable remainder trusts). You pay tax as you receive payments, spread over the years.

With the Harpers' $220,000 of tech stocks as the example, a 20-year unitrust paying 5% invests the full $220,000 instead of the $191,800 left after a $28,200 tax bill. At 7% a year, payments total about $258,200 against $225,100 from selling first. The estimated deduction is $220,000 × 0.9520 = about $78,900. The cost: about $305,200 goes to charity at the end, instead of about $266,100 staying in the family.

Going furtherAnnuity trust or unitrust?

A CRAT's payment never changes, which suits someone who wants certainty, but no new contributions can be added and inflation erodes the payment. A CRUT's payment rises and falls with the investments and can accept additional gifts. The IRS publishes sample trust documents for both (Rev. Procs. 2003-53 to 2003-60 for CRATs, listed on its charitable remainder trust page). Trusts file Form 5227 every year and send beneficiaries a Schedule K-1.

Charity first, family later

Charitable lead trusts

A charitable lead trust (CLT) turns the remainder trust inside out: the charity receives the annual payments for a term of years, and whatever is left at the end goes to family members. It is mostly a tool for families who expect to owe estate or gift tax.

Charitable lead trust
What it is
A trust that pays a charity either a guaranteed annuity (a CLAT) or a fixed percentage of the trust's value each year (a CLUT), with the remainder passing to non-charitable beneficiaries. The tax code allows a deduction for the charity's lead interest only in one of those two forms (IRC 170(f)(2)(B), 2055(e)(2)(B)).
Used for
Reducing the taxable value of a gift or bequest to heirs: the taxable gift is only the present value of the remainder, computed at the IRS's section 7520 rate.
What it means for you
If the trust's investments earn more than the 7520 rate, the excess passes to heirs free of gift or estate tax. For October 2026 that rate is 5.6% (Rev. Rul. 2026-19).
Annuity factor a = [1 − (1 + i)−n] ÷ i
Taxable remainder = value − annuity × a
Zeroed-out annuity = value ÷ a
(i = the 7520 rate, n = years)

For a $1,000,000 ten-year CLAT at 5.6%, the annuity factor is 7.502, so an annuity of about $133,305 a year to charity makes the taxable remainder zero. If the trust earns exactly 5.6%, nothing is left for the family. If it earns 8%, about $227,800 passes to the children with no gift tax.

Practitioner depthGrantor or non-grantor

A grantor CLT gives the donor an up-front income-tax deduction for the charity's lead interest, but the donor then pays tax on the trust's income every year, and if the donor stops being treated as owner, part of the deduction is recaptured (IRC 170(f)(2)(B)). A non-grantor CLT gives no up-front deduction to the donor; the trust itself deducts its payments to charity. Which is better depends on the donor's income and the trust's investments. These are specialist tools for estates near or above the $15 million exemption.

A family giving vehicle

Donor-advised funds and private foundations

Some families want more than a one-time bequest: a vehicle that keeps giving, possibly with children or grandchildren advising. The two main choices differ in cost, control and rules.

Donor-advised fund (DAF)
What it is
An account held by a public charity called a sponsoring organization. Once you contribute, the sponsor has legal control; you, or someone you name, keep advisory privileges over grants and investments (IRS, Donor-advised funds).
Used for
Taking a deduction in one year and granting over many; giving appreciated shares; naming successor advisors so children continue the family's giving.
What it means for you
Simple and inexpensive, but grants cannot go to individuals or pay for personal benefits; a taxable distribution triggers a 20% excise tax on the sponsor (IRC 4966). Policies on successors and inactive accounts vary by sponsor.
Private foundation
What it is
A separate charitable organization, usually funded by one family, run by its own board. It must generally pay out about 5% of its investment assets each year (the minimum investment return in IRC 4942) and pays a 1.39% excise tax on net investment income (IRC 4940).
Used for
Full control: hiring staff, running programs, choosing grantees, involving family on the board for generations.
What it means for you
Costlier and more regulated: annual Form 990-PF filings, strict self-dealing rules, and lower deduction limits for lifetime gifts (generally 30% of AGI for cash and 20% for appreciated property, IRC 170(b)(1)(B) and (D)).
Going furtherWhich one for a legacy?

For most families with less than several million dollars to give, a donor-advised fund achieves the goal of a family giving vehicle at a fraction of the cost: it can be named as an IRA beneficiary, successor advisors can be named, and there is no annual filing for the family. A foundation makes sense when the family wants to employ people, run its own programs, or make grants the sponsor would not allow. Either way, the IRS notes it examines arrangements promoted as donor-advised funds that deliver impermissible benefits to donors; a legitimate DAF gives up control for good.

Real law, real numbers

Three charitable legacy stories

Case study 1Estate of Atkinson v. Commissioner (11th Cir. 2002)
What happened
On August 9, 1991, Melvine Atkinson created a trust intended to be a charitable remainder annuity trust, funded with about $4 million of stock, to pay her $200,000 a year for life and then annuities to four other people, with the remainder to charity. No annuity payments were actually made to her from the trust before her death, and the trust was left exposed to the estate's taxes. The IRS denied the estate's charitable deduction; the Tax Court agreed, and on October 16, 2002, the Eleventh Circuit affirmed. The estate owed $2,654,976.
What it teaches
A charitable remainder trust qualifies only if it complies with the rules throughout its existence. Good intentions and a charitable remainder are not enough: the payments must actually be made, on time, in the right amounts.
Source
Estate of Atkinson v. Commissioner, 309 F.3d 1290 (11th Cir. 2002).
Case study 2Ruth's church and Anna's inheritance (illustration)
The setup
Ruth wants to leave $50,000 to her church. Her will could say so, paid from the CDs or the house sale; or her IRA beneficiary form could name the church for $50,000 or a matching percentage.
The result
Through the will, Anna inherits $50,000 more of IRA money and, at a 24% rate, pays $12,000 of income tax on it. Through the IRA form, Anna receives $50,000 more of assets with a stepped-up basis and owes about nothing. The church receives $50,000 either way.
What it teaches
The choice of asset is free and can be made with a single form. Before 2031 Ruth can also give through QCDs once she reaches 70½, which reduces her own taxes.
Source
Computed; rules from IRC 1014(c) and IRS Publication 590-B; QCD limits from Notice 2025-67.
Case study 3The Harpers' tech stocks in a unitrust (illustration)
The setup
At retirement, the Harpers put $220,000 of low-basis tech stock into a 20-year, 5% charitable remainder unitrust and have the trustee diversify.
The result
No $28,200 tax at the sale; about $258,200 of payments over 20 years at 7% returns; a deduction of roughly $78,900; about $305,200 to their chosen charities at the end.
What it teaches
A CRT is a way to diversify and give, not a way to enrich heirs. It fits only if the family genuinely wants the charitable remainder.
Source
Computed with the INV-093 unitrust calculator; rules from IRC 664 and the IRS charitable remainder trust page.

Decisions

Choosing the asset and the vehicle

Scenario 1 · Ruth

Ruth's will leaves $50,000 to her church and everything else to Anna. What change, if any, should she make?

Keep the will giftIt works, but Anna then inherits more IRA money and owes income tax on it: about $12,000 at 24%.
Use the IRA formRight. The church pays no income tax on IRA money; Anna inherits stepped-up assets instead. Coordinating the will and form avoids giving twice.
The principle: give charity pre-tax money and family stepped-up money.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers

The Harpers want to reduce their tech concentration and support their university, and they will need income in retirement. Which vehicle fits best?

FoundationToo costly and administrative for their scale, and it pays no income back to them.
CRUT plus DAFRight, if they genuinely want to leave the remainder to charity. The CRUT diversifies without an up-front gains tax and pays them for years; the DAF takes low-basis shares for current giving.
Sell and give cashPays up to 18.8% on the gain first; giving the shares themselves avoids that.
The principle: give appreciated assets, not the cash left after selling them.

What a charitable legacy means for each household

A contingent beneficiary. If Maya supports a cause, naming it as a contingent beneficiary on her 401(k) costs nothing and avoids the account falling to her estate if no named person survives.
Family first, charity as backup. With young children, the Riveras' priority is the children's trust. A small percentage to charity can sit on the 401(k)s as a contingent beneficiary.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A parent leaves $50,000 to charity. If it comes from the IRA rather than from stepped-up stock, how much income tax does the heir avoid at a 24% rate?

The heir inherits $50,000 of stock instead of $50,000 of IRA money: $50,000 × 0.24 = $12,000 less tax.
Exercise 2

Estimate the charitable deduction for $200,000 placed in a 20-year unitrust paying 5% at the start of each year, using (1 − payout)n.

0.9520 = 0.3585; $200,000 × 0.3585 = $71,697.
Exercise 3

What annual annuity to charity "zeroes out" a $1,000,000 ten-year lead annuity trust when the 7520 rate is 5.6%?

Annuity factor = [1 − 1.056−10] ÷ 0.056 = 7.5016. $1,000,000 ÷ 7.5016 = $133,305 a year.
Exercise 4

Roughly how much must a private foundation with $2,000,000 of investment assets distribute each year?

The minimum investment return is 5%: about $100,000 (the exact distributable amount is reduced by certain taxes).
