Running a Rental

Owning a rental is not a passive investment unless you pay someone to make it one. This module covers the work and the law: screening tenants fairly, the Fair Housing Act, leases and deposits, repairs and reserves, landlord insurance, and whether to hire a property manager or spend your own hours.

The question this module answersWhat does it take to run a rental well and legally, and what is my time worth in it?

Start here

A rental is a part-time job with legal duties

If you have rented an apartment, you have seen the landlord's side from the outside: the application, the lease, the deposit, the maintenance request, the move-out inspection. Plenty of individual owners handle all of it well, often with one or two properties, for years.

What most first-time landlords underestimate is not the money but the obligations. Federal law governs how you advertise and whom you may refuse. State and local law governs deposits, notices, repairs and evictions. A mistake on a spreadsheet costs a few hundred dollars; a mistake under fair housing law can cost far more. This module maps the landlord's year and the rules at each step.

Choosing a tenant

Screen with written criteria, applied the same way to everyone

Good screening is the single biggest lever a landlord has over results: a tenant who pays on time and treats the home well is worth more than a few dollars of extra rent. The discipline is to decide your standards before you meet anyone, write them down, and apply them identically.

Tenant screening report
What it is
A consumer report on an applicant, prepared by a screening company, which can include credit history, eviction records and criminal records.
Used for
Checking the facts behind an application: whether the applicant pays debts, has been evicted, and has the income they claim (verified separately with pay stubs or employer contact).
What it means for you
Because it is a consumer report, the federal Fair Credit Reporting Act applies. Reports can contain errors, such as another person's eviction under a similar name, so a report is a starting point, not a verdict.
Adverse action notice
What it is
The notice a landlord must give when it rejects an applicant, or asks for a higher deposit or rent, based on information in a screening report.
Used for
Letting the applicant find and fix errors. The Federal Trade Commission says the notice must include the screening company's name, address and phone number and explain the applicant's rights to a free copy of the report within 60 days and to dispute inaccurate information; the company must investigate a dispute within 30 days.
What it means for you
Build the notice into your process. It can be given in writing, electronically or orally; a written copy protects you.
Going furtherCriteria that commonly cause trouble

Blanket rules can have effects that fall more heavily on a protected group, and some states and cities restrict specific criteria, such as the use of certain criminal records or refusing tenants who pay with housing vouchers. Before adopting a rule, check your state and local law, and ask whether the rule actually predicts whether someone will pay rent and care for the home.

The federal floor

The Fair Housing Act, in plain language

Fair Housing Act42 U.S.C. 3601 et seq.
What it is
A federal law enacted in 1968 that bans discrimination in renting, selling and financing housing. The Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988 added disability ("handicap" in the statute) and familial status. It is enforced by the Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) and the Department of Justice.
Used for
Protecting people from being refused housing, offered worse terms, steered, or harassed because of a protected characteristic.
What it means for you
It applies to most landlords, including small ones. Violations can bring HUD complaints, private lawsuits and Justice Department actions with damages and penalties.

What the Act prohibits (42 U.S.C. 3604) includes refusing to rent or negotiate because of a protected characteristic; setting different terms, conditions or privileges; publishing any notice or advertisement that indicates a preference, limitation or discrimination; and falsely telling someone a unit is not available. For people with disabilities, it requires landlords to permit reasonable modifications of the premises at the tenant's expense (for a rental, the landlord may require restoration at move-out, reasonable wear and tear excepted) and to make reasonable accommodations in rules, policies, practices or services.

Going furtherThe narrow exemptions, and the one rule with no exemption

Section 3603(b) exempts (1) a single-family house sold or rented by an owner who owns no more than three such houses, without a real estate broker or agent and without discriminatory advertising, and (2) rooms or units in a building with living quarters for no more than four families, if the owner lives in one of them. The advertising ban in section 3604(c) applies even to these exempt owners. Religious organizations and qualifying housing for older persons have separate exemptions. State and local laws often have narrower exemptions or none.

Practitioner depthSex, sexual orientation and gender identity

In February 2021, HUD announced that it would interpret and enforce the Act's ban on sex discrimination to include sexual orientation and gender identity (HUD news release 21-021). HUD has since withdrawn that guidance, effective September 17, 2025 (Federal Register, April 6, 2026, 91 FR 17291), so check HUD's current position. Many states and cities list sexual orientation and gender identity expressly in their own fair housing laws; where they do, those protections apply regardless of federal guidance.

The paperwork that protects you both

Leases, security deposits and lead disclosure

A written lease sets the rent, due date, term, who may live there, pets, who pays which utilities, how repairs are requested, and how either side ends the tenancy. Much of what a lease may say is limited by state and local law: notice periods, late fees, entry rules and deposit handling. A lease clause that conflicts with the law is generally unenforceable.

Security deposit
What it is
Money the tenant pays at move-in that the landlord holds against unpaid rent or damage beyond normal wear and tear.
Used for
Covering losses at the end of a tenancy, with an itemized accounting of any deductions.
What it means for you
States cap the amount and set deadlines. Arizona, for example, caps deposits at one and one-half months' rent and requires an itemized list of deductions with any refund within 14 days, excluding weekends and holidays, after the tenancy ends, the tenant returns possession and the tenant asks (A.R.S. § 33-1321). Other states differ.
Going furtherLead-based paint disclosure

For housing built before 1978, federal rules require landlords to disclose any known lead-based paint or hazards, give tenants the EPA pamphlet Protect Your Family From Lead in Your Home, include a Lead Warning Statement in the lease, provide available records and reports, and keep signed disclosures for three years after the lease begins. Exemptions include leases of 100 days or less with no possible renewal or extension, zero-bedroom units and housing for the elderly or disabled (in both of those cases unless a child under six lives or is expected to live there), and housing certified lead-free by a certified inspector (EPA).

Keeping it running

Repairs, reserves and the right insurance

Most state landlord-tenant laws require a rental to be kept fit to live in, typically including working heat, plumbing and electrical systems and a weatherproof building; check your state's code for the exact list. Beyond the legal floor, prompt repairs keep good tenants, and good tenants are the most valuable thing a small landlord has.

Repairs, improvements and capital reserves
What it is
Repairs keep the property in working order; improvements make it better, restore it or adapt it to a new use, and must be capitalized and depreciated for tax purposes (IRS Publication 527). A capital reserve is money set aside each month for large replacements.
Used for
Budgeting the costs that arrive in lumps: roof, heating and cooling, water heater, appliances, flooring.
What it means for you
Fund the reserve from the rent every month, in a separate account, so a $12,000 replacement is an expected expense rather than a crisis.
Landlord insurance
What it is
A policy for a property you rent to others. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) describes a landlord policy as covering the home, other structures, property contents such as appliances and furniture, lost rental income due to damage, legal fees and liability claims.
Used for
Protecting the building and the owner. A homeowners policy is written for a home you live in; the NAIC warns that homeowners policies usually exclude or limit coverage for business activity in the home.
What it means for you
Tell your insurer before you rent out a home. Require tenants to carry renters insurance for their own belongings, and consider an umbrella policy for extra liability protection (INV-023).

Hire it out, or do it yourself

What a property manager costs, and what your hours are worth

Property manager
What it is
A person or company paid to market the unit, screen tenants, collect rent, arrange repairs, handle notices and, if necessary, run an eviction.
Used for
Turning a rental into a more passive investment, managing property from a distance, and bringing legal and local know-how.
What it means for you
Fees take many forms: a percentage of collected rent, a leasing fee for each new tenant, renewal fees, and sometimes markups on repairs. Compare the total annual cost of real quotes, and read the management agreement's termination terms.

With the illustrative defaults — a 10% monthly fee, a leasing fee of half a month's rent, and a new tenant every two years — a manager costs about $2,869 a year, 12.2% of collected rent. Doing it yourself at 4 routine hours a month plus 20 hours per turnover takes about 58 hours a year, so self-managing pays you about $49 an hour before taxes. That can be a good wage, or poor pay for someone whose time is scarce, and one bad tenant or emergency can multiply the hours.

Going furtherThe costs that don't show up in the fee

A self-managing owner carries the legal risk personally: a fair housing complaint over a poorly worded ad, a deposit returned a week late, an entry without proper notice. A good manager's value is partly in avoiding those mistakes. A poor manager, on the other hand, can let vacancies drag and repairs pile up. Ask for references from other owners, the manager's average days to lease and turnover rate, and how they handle maintenance approvals.

Practitioner depthTime, taxes and real estate professional status

Hours spent on rentals matter for tax purposes too. Rental losses are generally passive and limited, with a special allowance for active participants that phases out at higher incomes; people who qualify for real estate professional status by spending more than 750 hours and more than half their working time in real property businesses can treat rental losses differently. INV-068 covers these rules; keep a contemporaneous log of your hours either way.

Real law, real cases

Three cases every landlord should know

Case study 1United States v. Sterling (2006–2009)
What happened
In August 2006 the Justice Department sued Los Angeles landlord Donald T. Sterling and related defendants, alleging discrimination on the basis of race, national origin and familial status at apartment buildings they owned and managed. In November 2009 the defendants agreed to pay $2.725 million to settle the allegations — a $100,000 civil penalty and $2.625 million for people harmed — which the department called the largest monetary payment it had ever obtained in settling a case of housing discrimination in apartment rentals.
What it teaches
The suit alleged refusals to rent, misrepresenting availability, worse treatment, and statements and advertisements expressing preferences. Each is a separate prohibited act under section 3604. Scale does not change the rules much: apart from the narrow exemptions in section 3603(b), the same prohibitions apply to a small landlord, and the advertising ban applies to everyone.
Source
U.S. Department of Justice, press releases of August 7, 2006 and November 3, 2009.
Case study 2The eviction moratoriums and Alabama Association of Realtors v. HHS (2021)
What happened
The CARES Act of March 2020 imposed a 120-day eviction moratorium for properties in federal assistance programs or with federally backed loans. When it expired, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) imposed a nationwide moratorium covering all residential properties, with criminal penalties, later extended by Congress and by the CDC. On August 26, 2021, the Supreme Court vacated a stay, leaving in force a ruling that the CDC had exceeded its authority.
What it teaches
Rent collection can be interrupted by events far outside a landlord's control. An owner with a thin cash cushion and a mortgage to pay was exposed for many months. Reserves are protection against legal and political risk as well as broken water heaters.
Source
Supreme Court of the United States, No. 21A23, per curiam opinion (August 26, 2021).
IllustrationThe assistance-animal request
The setup
A fictional landlord with a "no pets" rule receives an application from a tenant with a disability who asks to keep an assistance animal.
The law
The Fair Housing Act requires reasonable accommodations in rules and policies when necessary to give a person with a disability equal opportunity to use and enjoy a dwelling (42 U.S.C. 3604(f)(3)(B)). An assistance animal is not a pet for this purpose.
What it teaches
A neutral rule applied to everyone can still violate the Act if the landlord refuses a reasonable accommodation. Evaluate the request; do not apply the pet rule or a pet deposit automatically. When unsure, get advice before refusing.

Decisions

Three landlord decisions

Scenario 1 · Ruth

If Ruth moves near Anna, she could keep the Pittsburgh house and rent it out rather than sell it. What should she weigh?

Self-manage remotelyAt 68, several hours away, every leak and late payment becomes a phone call and possibly a trip. The fee saving is real; the hours and the legal risk are too.
Compare selling and managed rentingRight. Converting a home to a rental also affects the home sale exclusion, which requires living in it two of the last five years (INV-063, INV-068). For many retirees, selling and simplifying beats becoming a long-distance landlord.
The principle: count your hours and your stage of life, not only the rent.
Scenario 2 · A screening choice (illustration)

Suppose the Riveras had bought the rental from INV-065. Two applicants meet their written criteria. One has two young children; the other is a single professional who "seems quieter." What should they do?

Choose the quieter applicantPreferring an applicant because the other has children is familial-status discrimination under the Fair Housing Act, exactly the kind of practice alleged in the Sterling case.
Criteria and a neutral tie-breakerRight. Decide by the published standards; if two applicants qualify, use a rule set in advance, such as order of completed applications, and document it.
The principle: choose tenants by written, lawful criteria applied the same way to everyone.
Scenario 3 · Denise's future duplex (illustration)

Years from now, Denise buys a duplex and lives in one unit. A friend says that because she lives there, fair housing law does not apply and she can advertise "ideal for a single professional." Is the friend right?

Yes, exemptThe Act exempts some owner-occupied buildings of up to four units from parts of section 3604, but section 3604(c), the advertising ban, still applies. An ad signaling a preference against families with children is a violation.
NoRight. Colorado and Denver may have their own rules. Write neutral ads that describe the unit, not the tenant.
The principle: describe the property, never the person you want.

What running a rental means for each household

Start with the rules, not the property. If Denise someday house-hacks a duplex (INV-070), she becomes a landlord at home. Neutral ads and written criteria from day one matter even for an owner-occupant.
Two careers and two children leave few spare hours. If they ever buy a rental, budget a manager's fee in the analysis; if the deal only works self-managed, it is paying them for a second job.
Her spare hours are already spoken for. A full-time job and caring for Eli leave little time for tenant calls and turnovers, and a landlord's emergencies do not wait. If she ever owns a rental, the analysis should carry a manager's fee from day one; a deal that only works self-managed does not fit her life.
Plan for retirement time and travel. A rental bought in their 50s is managed in their 60s and 70s. Choose a manager-friendly property or budget the fee now.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

Under Arizona's rule, what is the largest security deposit a landlord may charge on a $2,100-a-month rental, in dollars?

One and one-half months' rent: 1.5 × $2,100 = $3,150 (A.R.S. § 33-1321). Other states set different limits.
Exercise 2

Rent is $1,800 a month. A manager charges 9% of collected rent and a full month's rent as a leasing fee for each new tenant. Vacancy is 5% and a new tenant arrives once every two years. What is the manager's annual cost, in dollars?

Collected rent = $1,800 × 12 × 0.95 = $20,520. Monthly fees = 9% × $20,520 = $1,846.80. Leasing fee = $1,800 × 0.5 turnovers a year = $900. Total ≈ $2,747.
Exercise 3

A manager would cost $2,700 a year. Self-managing takes 60 hours a year. What hourly pay does self-managing effectively earn you, in dollars?

$2,700 ÷ 60 = $45 an hour, before taxes and before any value you place on avoiding the legal risk and the late-night calls.
Exercise 4

You set aside 8% of a $2,100 monthly rent as a capital reserve. How much goes into the reserve in a year, in dollars?

$2,100 × 12 × 0.08 = $2,016 a year ($168 a month). A $12,000 roof takes about six years of deposits, which is why the reserve starts on day one.
