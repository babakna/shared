Couples and Families

Money decisions get harder, and more important, when other people share them. This module covers how couples combine finances, how a one-income household protects itself, the legal basics of marital property, prenuptial agreements and beneficiaries, the special risks in blended families, and how to raise children who handle money well.

The question this module answersHow do we organize money as a couple or family so that it is fair, protected, and passes to the people we intend?

Start here

Two people, one plan — or two plans that fit together

Most couples sort out money without a formal system: whoever pays the rent pays the rent, and it works well enough. Plenty of long, happy partnerships run that way, and there is no single right structure.

But a household with shared goals, children or property eventually needs clear answers to three questions. How do we pay for shared life? What happens if one income stops? Who owns what, and who receives it if one of us dies or we separate? The first is about fairness and habit. The second is about protection. The third is partly law, and the law often decides it for you unless the paperwork says otherwise — above all, each account's beneficiary designation.

Fair and workable

Splitting shared costs

Once a couple decides which costs are shared (typically housing, utilities, groceries, childcare, insurance and joint goals), the next question is who pays how much. There are three common rules, and they give very different results when incomes differ.

Joint account
What it is
A bank or brokerage account owned by two or more people, each of whom can usually deposit and withdraw. Many joint accounts pass automatically to the surviving owner.
Used for
Paying shared bills and saving for shared goals from one place.
What it means for you
Either owner can typically withdraw everything, so a joint account is an act of trust. How it is titled decides who owns it at a death (INV-085).

With the default numbers — partner A takes home $5,300 a month, partner B $3,800, and shared costs are $6,000 (66% of combined pay) — a 50/50 split leaves A with $2,300 and B with $800. Proportional splitting (58% and 42%) has A pay about $3,495 and B $2,505, leaving $1,805 and $1,295. Equal leftover gives each $1,550. None is objectively right; the question is what the couple considers fair, and the calculator makes the trade-off concrete.

Going furtherA monthly money meeting

Whatever the structure, couples who review money regularly tend to avoid surprises. A short monthly check covers: last month's shared spending against the plan, progress on each goal (INV-024), upcoming large bills, and any change in either person's job or health. A yearly session reviews insurance, beneficiaries and the investment policy (INV-028). Agreeing in advance on a threshold ("we discuss any purchase over $500") prevents many arguments.

Practitioner depthPooling and retirement fairness

Even fully pooled couples hold retirement accounts in one person's name, and tax rules treat them individually. A couple can equalize by directing the higher earner's extra saving into their own 401(k) while funding a spousal IRA or Roth IRA for the other. Balancing the two partners' account values also balances what each would control if the relationship ended, which is fairer in practice even though divorce courts may divide marital property differently.

Single-income households

When one partner earns and the other does not

Many families choose, for a while or for good, to live on one income while a partner cares for children, a relative or a home. It concentrates risk: if the earner's pay stops, so does the household's. It also creates a gap in the non-earner's own retirement savings, because workplace plans require a paycheck. The tax code offers one important fix.

Spousal IRAofficially the Kay Bailey Hutchison Spousal IRA
What it is
An IRA for a spouse with little or no pay, funded on the strength of the other spouse's taxable compensation. It is an ordinary traditional or Roth IRA in the lower earner's own name.
Used for
Letting a non-earning or low-earning spouse keep saving for retirement in tax-advantaged accounts.
What it means for you
If you file a joint return, each spouse can contribute up to the annual limit, as long as the couple's combined contributions do not exceed the taxable compensation on the joint return. For 2026 the limit is $7,500 per person, or $8,600 at age 50 or older.
Spousal IRA limit (2026) = the smaller of $7,500 ($8,600 if 50+) and [joint compensation − the other spouse's traditional and Roth IRA contributions for the year]

Rule: IRS, Retirement topics: IRA contribution limits and Publication 590-A, "Kay Bailey Hutchison Spousal IRA Limit." Roth contributions are also subject to income limits (INV-032).

Four protections for a one-income household

  1. Term life insurance on the earner sized to replace the income until the children are grown, and on the at-home partner too: replacing childcare and household work costs real money (INV-023).
  2. Disability coverage on the earner. A long illness or injury stops the income just as surely as a death, and it is easy to overlook.
  3. A larger emergency fund, six months or more, because there is no second paycheck to fall back on (INV-019).
  4. Social Security's spousal benefit. A spouse can receive a spousal benefit of up to 50% of the worker's primary insurance amount, reduced if claimed before full retirement age (INV-078).
Going furtherThe career-break cost

Stepping out of paid work costs more than the lost salary: it also pauses employer matches, Social Security earnings credits and career progression. A couple deciding on a career break can price it with the tools in this course: lost income, lost match (INV-022) and the future value of retirement contributions not made. Funding a spousal IRA each year of the break closes part of that gap.

Going furtherOne parent, one income

The four protections apply, with changes, to a single parent such as Monica Walker, 58, who supports her son Eli, 13, on her $92,000 salary. There is no second adult to fall back on, so life insurance is sized for Eli rather than a partner, and because Eli, who has special needs, will likely need support for life, it should be payable to a third-party special needs trust rather than to him (INV-023, INV-102). Disability coverage matters even more, because her pay is the whole household income. Her emergency-fund target is six months of essentials plus $3,000, or $34,200 (INV-019); she holds $30,000. There is no spousal IRA or spousal benefit, but Eli may qualify for a child's benefit on her Social Security record once she claims, as a disabled adult child (INV-078, INV-102). Filing as head of household gives her a larger standard deduction and wider brackets than a single filer (INV-029).

Law you should know

Marital property, prenuptial agreements and beneficiaries

Marriage changes ownership. The rules depend on the state you live in, and in particular on whether it follows community property rules, and they matter at three moments: while married (who controls what), at divorce (how property is divided), and at death (who inherits). This is general information, not legal advice; a family-law or estate attorney in your state should review any agreement or plan.

Community property
What it is
A system in which most property and income acquired during marriage belong equally to both spouses. The IRS lists nine community property states: Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington and Wisconsin.
Used for
Dividing property at divorce or death and, for spouses filing separately, splitting income for federal tax (IRS Publication 555).
What it means for you
The Riveras live in Arizona, so a paycheck earned by either spouse during the marriage is generally community property. Property owned before marriage, or received by gift or inheritance, is generally separate property. The other states use common-law rules, where title and state divorce law decide.
Prenuptial agreementalso premarital agreement
What it is
A contract made before marriage that sets rules for property, support and other matters. Under the Uniform Premarital Agreement Act (1983), a model law many states have adopted in some form, it must be in writing and signed by both parties, and it takes effect on marriage.
Used for
Protecting a business, premarital savings or an inheritance; providing for children from an earlier marriage; settling in advance what would happen on divorce or death.
What it means for you
Under the model act, an agreement cannot adversely affect a child's right to support, and it is unenforceable if not signed voluntarily, or if it was unconscionable and the challenging party did not receive fair disclosure of the other's finances. Full financial disclosure and separate lawyers protect both partners. State law varies.
Going furtherYour spouse's rights in a 401(k)

Federal law gives spouses special rights in many workplace plans. Pension-type plans must pay married participants a qualified joint and survivor annuity unless both spouses consent in writing to another form, and the spouse's consent must be witnessed by a plan representative or a notary. Many 401(k) and other profit-sharing plans are exempt from that requirement if they offer no life annuity and pay the full death benefit to the surviving spouse unless the spouse has consented to a different beneficiary (IRS, Retirement topics: Qualified joint and survivor annuity). IRAs have no such federal rule, which is one reason IRA beneficiary forms deserve extra care.

Practitioner depthCommunity property details

Community property states differ among themselves. The IRS notes, for example, that income from separate property is separate income in Arizona, California, Nevada, New Mexico and Washington, but community income in Idaho, Louisiana, Texas and Wisconsin. Registered domestic partners in California, Nevada and Washington generally follow those states' community property rules. Alaska, South Dakota and Tennessee have laws allowing couples to elect community property treatment, which Publication 555 does not address. Moving between states can change the character of property, so couples who relocate should review titles and agreements.

Second marriages and stepchildren

Blended families: providing for everyone you intend

A blended family includes children from a previous relationship of one or both partners. Blended families face the ordinary questions plus a sharp one: if one spouse dies, how is money shared between the surviving spouse and the children of the first marriage? The default answer, set by beneficiary forms and titles, is often "everything to the new spouse," and that may not be what anyone intended.

Five steps for blended families

  1. Update every beneficiary form after divorce and remarriage. The Case studies tab shows what happens when a former spouse is still named.
  2. Decide the split explicitly between the spouse and each child, and write it into beneficiary forms, titles and the will so they agree.
  3. Consider life insurance for the children of the first marriage, which lets retirement accounts go to the spouse without disinheriting the children.
  4. Use a trust when control matters, for example to support a spouse for life and then pass the remainder to one's own children (INV-087).
  5. Consider a prenuptial or postnuptial agreement that records the plan and the disclosure behind it.
Going furtherStepchildren and default rules

State default inheritance rules, which apply when there is no will or beneficiary, generally favor a spouse and legal children, and a stepchild who was never adopted may receive nothing under them. The rules vary by state. The practical lesson is the same everywhere: if you want a stepchild to inherit, name them.

The next generation

Teaching children about money, age by age

Children learn money habits long before they earn money. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's research-based model describes three building blocks of adult financial capability: executive function (planning, delaying gratification, focus), financial habits and norms, and financial knowledge and decision-making skills. Each develops at a different age.

Custodial Roth IRA
What it is
A Roth IRA opened for a minor by a parent or other adult acting as custodian. There is no minimum age for a Roth IRA, but contributions cannot exceed the child's taxable compensation for the year (and the annual limit, $7,500 in 2026).
Used for
Turning a teenager's summer-job or babysitting earnings into decades of tax-free growth. The limit depends on what the child earned; families often give the child the money to make the contribution.
What it means for you
$1,000 a year from ages 15 to 18 grows to about $4,300 by 18 and, left alone at a 5% real return, about $42,700 in today's dollars by 65. It also teaches ownership of a real account.
Going furtherAccounts for children, compared

A custodial UTMA/UGMA account holds any gift for a child and becomes the child's property at the age set by state law; its investment income may be subject to the kiddie tax. A 529 plan is for education and stays under the owner's control (INV-034). A custodial Roth IRA requires the child's own earnings and is aimed at retirement, though contributions can come back out. Each teaches something different; none replaces conversations about money.

Real law

Two Supreme Court cases every family should know

Case study 1Egelhoff v. Egelhoff (2001)
What happened
David Egelhoff named his wife as beneficiary of his employer's life insurance policy and pension plan, both governed by the Employee Retirement Income Security Act (ERISA). Shortly after they divorced, he died without a will. His children from a previous marriage relied on a Washington statute that automatically revokes a spouse's beneficiary designation on divorce. The Supreme Court held that ERISA pre-empts the state statute; the plans had to be paid according to the plan documents, to the former wife.
What it teaches
For employer plans governed by ERISA, a state law you may be counting on to fix an outdated form may not apply. In a blended family, the children of the first marriage can be the ones left out. Change the form yourself.
Source
Egelhoff v. Egelhoff, 532 U.S. 141 (2001). Legal Information Institute, Cornell Law School.
Case study 2Kennedy v. Plan Administrator for DuPont (2009)
What happened
William Kennedy named his wife Liv as beneficiary of his employer savings plan and named no contingent beneficiary. Their divorce decree divested Liv of her interest in the plan, but William never filed a new beneficiary form. When he died, the plan paid Liv. His daughter, as executrix of his estate, sued. The Supreme Court held that the administrator did its duty under ERISA by paying according to the plan documents.
What it teaches
Even a divorce decree in which the former spouse gives up the account did not override the beneficiary form on file. The only reliable fix is a new form, filed with the plan, after every divorce, marriage, birth or death.
Source
Kennedy v. Plan Administrator for DuPont Savings and Investment Plan, 555 U.S. 285 (2009). Legal Information Institute, Cornell Law School.

Decisions

Family money choices

Scenario 1 · The Riveras

Elena earns $60,000 and Marcus $85,000. They have always split shared bills 50/50 from separate accounts, and Elena is left with little to save. With two children, what structure fits best?

Keep 50/50It worked before children. Now shared costs are larger, so 50/50 leaves Elena far less to save and makes her retirement lag. In Arizona, wages earned during the marriage are generally community property anyway.
Hybrid, proportional or pooledRight. The family's goals are shared, so funding them in proportion to pay, or from one pot, matches reality. Equal retirement saving rates keep both partners' futures on track.
The principle: revisit the structure when the family changes.
Scenario 2 · Monica

Monica divorced Eli's father years ago; he is not involved and pays no support. Suppose her 401(k) beneficiary form still names him, and a coworker suggests she simply switch every form to Eli. What should Monica do?

NothingThis is the Kennedy case. A divorce decree did not override the form on file; her former husband could be paid.
Name Eli directlyWell meant, and a common mistake. Money left to Eli outright counts as his resource and can cost him SSI and Medicaid, and a 13-year-old cannot manage an inheritance, so a court may have to become involved.
A guardian, a trust, and new formsRight. Money held in a properly drafted third-party special needs trust does not count toward the SSI resource limit, and the trustee can spend it on what benefits do not cover (INV-102). The will names who raises Eli if she dies while he is a minor. How retirement accounts pay out to a trust is in INV-091.
The principle: when a child will need lifelong support, name a trust, not the child, and write it into every form.
Scenario 3 · The Harpers

Ethan, 20, earned $4,000 from a summer job. Tom and Rachel want to help him start investing. What is the best first step?

Technology stocksTeaches concentration, the opposite of the lesson in INV-004, and holds the money outside a tax-advantaged account.
A Roth IRA he ownsRight. He earned $4,000, so he can contribute up to $4,000. Tax-free growth for 45 years and hands-on ownership are the best teacher.
An account in their namesKeeps control but teaches little, and the money remains legally theirs.
The principle: give young adults real accounts, real ownership and a simple, diversified start.

Apply it

Family money for each household

Start clean. Jordan should name beneficiaries on her 401(k) and any life insurance through work, and revisit them if she marries. If she moves in with a partner, a written agreement about shared costs avoids trouble later.
Protect and restructure. Wills naming guardians, term life on both, a hybrid or pooled account structure, and money lessons for Sofia (habits: allowance, saving for a goal) and Leo (waiting, counting, needs versus wants).
One income, and a son who will need support for life. Monica's will should name a guardian for Eli while he is a minor, and before he turns 18 she should choose between guardianship and a less restrictive option such as supported decision-making (INV-102). A third-party special needs trust should be the beneficiary of her 401(k), Roth IRA and a 20-year term life policy, with a letter of intent that tells the trustee and guardian about Eli's routines, care and preferences. Money lessons should fit Eli's abilities: small, regular amounts of his own, and an Ohio STABLE account (Ohio's ABLE program), where the first $100,000 does not count toward the SSI resource limit.
Adult child and a joint retirement. Help Ethan open his own Roth IRA; confirm each spouse's 401(k) beneficiary and Ethan as contingent; and make sure both spouses can manage the money if one cannot.
Widowhood paperwork. Ruth's IRA beneficiary form should name Anna (or the heirs she chooses), not Walter. Powers of attorney and a trusted contact make it easy for Anna to help if needed (INV-098).

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

Partner A takes home $7,000 a month and partner B $3,000. Shared costs are $5,000. Under proportional splitting, how much does A pay each month?

A's share is 7,000 ÷ 10,000 = 70%. 0.70 × 5,000 = $3,500.
Exercise 2

Same couple, equal-leftover rule. How much spending money does each partner keep each month?

Combined take-home $10,000 minus $5,000 of shared costs leaves $5,000, or $2,500 each. A pays (7,000 − 3,000 + 5,000) ÷ 2 = $4,500 and B pays $500.
Exercise 3

A married couple filing jointly, both under 50, has one earner paid $70,000. What is the most they can contribute to IRAs in total for 2026, including a spousal IRA?

Each spouse may contribute up to $7,500, and $15,000 is well below their $70,000 of joint compensation: $15,000. (Deductibility and Roth eligibility depend on income and workplace plan coverage.)
Exercise 4

A spousal IRA receives $7,500 at the end of each year for 20 years at a 5% real return. What is it worth at the end, in today's dollars?

1.0520 = 2.65330. 7,500 × (2.65330 − 1) ÷ 0.05 = $247,995.
