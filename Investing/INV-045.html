Active Management

The previous two modules made the case for indexing. This one makes the strongest honest case for the alternative: where active management has a better chance, what it can do that an index fund cannot, how to judge a manager, and how to spot a fund that charges active fees for index-like results.

The question this module answersWhen, if ever, is it worth paying someone to manage my money actively — and how would I tell a good manager from a lucky or expensive one?

Start here

Markets need active investors; the question is whether you should pay one

Every price you pay for an index fund was set by someone doing research and trading. Without active investors, there would be no prices to index. Active management is not a mistake; it is a service, and like any service it can be worth its price or not.

INV-043 showed that, on average, active funds must trail index funds after costs, and that over 20 years about nine in ten did. But averages hide a range. Some categories give managers more room to add value, some investors need things an index fund cannot do, and some managers really are skilled. This module is about those exceptions — and about not paying exception prices for ordinary results.

What this module covers

  1. What counts as active management, and how active a fund really is (active share).
  2. Where active has a better chance: less efficient niches.
  3. What active can do that an index fund cannot: tax management, direct indexing and customization.
  4. Fees versus alpha, and why skill is so hard to prove (information ratio).
  5. A checklist for evaluating a manager.
  6. Closet indexing: paying active fees for index-like results.

Definitions

A spectrum, not a switch

Active investing
What it is
Any approach that departs from holding the market in proportion to value: picking individual stocks or bonds, rotating among sectors, timing the market, tilting toward factors such as value, or managing a bond portfolio's interest-rate and credit risk.
Used for
Trying to beat a benchmark, reduce risk, manage taxes or express preferences an index does not.
What it means for you
"Active" describes what a manager does, not whether it works. The two questions to ask are how different the fund is from its index, and what you pay for the difference.
Active share
What it is
The percentage of a portfolio that differs from its benchmark. Computed as half the sum of the absolute differences between each holding's weight in the fund and in the index. 0% is an index fund; 100% shares nothing with it.
Used for
Measuring how much actual management a fund delivers, separately from how its returns turned out.
What it means for you
A fund with a 0.85% fee and 30% active share charges you roughly 2.7% a year for the part that is actually managed (Closet indexing tab).
Active share = ½ × Σ | weight in fund − weight in index |
Tracking error
What it is
The standard deviation of the difference between a fund's return and its benchmark's, year by year. A fund with 5% tracking error typically beats or trails the index by up to about 5 points in a given year.
Used for
Measuring how far results can wander from the benchmark; it is the "noise" against which any skill must be detected.
What it means for you
High tracking error means you will spend long stretches behind the index even if the manager is good. Ask yourself whether you would stay through them.
Practitioner depthDoes active share predict returns?

Martijn Cremers and Antti Petajisto (2009), studying US equity funds from 1980 to 2003, found that funds with the highest active share outperformed their benchmarks before and after expenses, and that non-index funds with the lowest active share underperformed. Andrea Frazzini, Jacques Friedman and Lukasz Pomorski (2016), using the same sample, found that active share correlates with benchmark returns but "does not predict actual fund returns" within a benchmark. The fair summary: active share reliably tells you how different a fund is, and therefore whether its fee buys anything; it does not reliably tell you whether the difference will pay.

Where the odds are better

Small companies, foreign markets and bonds

The argument for active management is strongest where fewer analysts follow the securities, information is harder to get, trading is costly, or the benchmark itself is awkward to own. Three areas are usually named: small-company stocks, international and emerging-market stocks, and bonds. The data give each some support over shorter periods.

Two patterns stand out. First, in these categories there are stretches of three to five years when most managers win — much more often than in large US stocks. Second, over 20 years most still lose. International small-cap funds are the best long-run case in the table, with about one in five beating the benchmark over 20 years; that is a real opportunity, but it still means picking the right fund from five.

Going furtherWhy bond managers often look better

Bond indexes contain thousands of issues, many of which trade rarely, and they weight issuers by how much debt they have. Active bond managers can hold bonds that are riskier than the index — lower credit quality, longer maturities — and in calm markets that extra risk usually pays, so they "beat" the index. When credit markets fall, the same bets hurt. SPIVA notes that in the first half of 2026 reaching for riskier long-term credit was rewarded. Before paying for an active bond fund, compare its credit quality and duration with its benchmark's; some of the "alpha" may simply be risk (INV-009, INV-010).

What an index fund cannot do

The clearest case for "active": your taxes and your constraints

Some of the most valuable "active" work has nothing to do with beating the market. An index fund treats all its owners the same. It cannot know that you have a large gain elsewhere to offset, that you work for a bank and already carry banking risk, or that you would rather not own tobacco companies. A separately managed account can.

Direct indexing
What it is
Owning the individual stocks of an index in your own account, instead of a fund that holds them. FINRA describes it as a way to "purchase many or all the stocks in a specified index."
Used for
Tax-loss harvesting stock by stock, excluding companies or sectors, and gradually transitioning a concentrated portfolio.
What it means for you
FINRA warns that once you deviate from the index your returns "may be different from the index's returns," and that fees may be higher than a typical passive portfolio. The benefit is mostly tax, and only in taxable accounts.
Tax-loss harvesting
What it is
Selling an investment that is below what you paid, to realize a capital loss for tax purposes, and replacing it with something similar so your market exposure barely changes.
Used for
Offsetting capital gains and some ordinary income, deferring taxes.
What it means for you
The wash-sale rule disallows the loss if you buy substantially identical securities within 30 days before or after the sale — including purchases in your own IRA or Roth IRA, or by your spouse (IRS Publication 550). INV-037 covers the mechanics.
Going furtherHarvesting defers tax; it rarely erases it

When you sell a stock at a loss and buy a replacement, the replacement starts with a lower cost basis. When you eventually sell it, the gain is larger. So harvesting mostly defers tax, which is valuable (the deferred tax compounds for you), and it can become permanent if the shares are held until death and receive a step-up in basis (INV-093) or are donated to charity (INV-039). Harvesting has little or no value inside IRAs and 401(k)s, where gains and losses are not taxed each year.

Practitioner depthThe fine print on direct indexing

Harvesting opportunities are largest in the first years, when many lots are near their cost, and shrink as the portfolio's gains grow; later contributions renew them. Each replaced stock creates tracking error. Excluding sectors (for example, an employee excluding their own industry) changes the portfolio's risk, not just its taxes. And the service fee, charged every year, must be smaller than the tax value created. Compare the all-in fee with a broad index ETF at 0.03% before signing up.

The hurdle

A skilled manager still has to clear the fee — and prove it

Suppose a manager really is skilled, with a true edge (alpha) of 1% a year before fees. If the fund charges 0.75% more than an index fund, you keep 0.25%. And because results bounce around the benchmark by several points a year, even that real 0.25% is nearly invisible in the record. The tool shows how these three numbers — edge, fee and noise — decide your odds.

Information ratio
What it is
A manager's average excess return over the benchmark divided by the tracking error: edge per unit of noise.
Used for
Comparing managers whose bets are of different sizes, and estimating how long it would take to tell skill from luck.
What it means for you
An information ratio of 0.5 after fees would be exceptional. At 0.1, even 20 years of results cannot separate skill from chance.
Information ratio = (fund return − benchmark return) ÷ tracking error     Years needed for t = 2: (2 ÷ information ratio)²

With the default inputs — a 1% edge, a 0.75% fee and 5% tracking error — the edge after fees is 0.25%, the information ratio 0.05, and the chance of being ahead of the index after 10 years only about 56%. To confirm the edge with the usual statistical standard would take (2 ÷ 0.05)² = 1,600 years. Set the fee above the edge and the line turns down: holding longer makes trailing more likely.

Going furtherWhy skill may not reach investors

Jonathan Berk and Richard Green (2004) offered an explanation that does not require managers to be unskilled. In their model, investors chase past performance, so skilled managers attract money; because active management has decreasing returns to scale, the growing fund's edge shrinks until returns to investors match passive benchmarks. Past performance then cannot predict future returns, even though skill exists. For you, the implication is the same as the scorecards': a skilled manager's edge tends to be absorbed by size and fees before it reaches you.

Due diligence

Eight questions to ask before you pay for active

Where to find the answers: the prospectus fee table and annual report (cost, turnover), the fund's fact sheet (benchmark, holdings, manager tenure), independent fund-research sites (tracking error, style), and the SPIVA report for the category's base rate. For a separately managed account or an advisor, the firm's Form ADV Part 2 brochure lists fees and conflicts (INV-103).

Style drift
What it is
A fund moving away from the kind of securities it was sold to hold — a "small value" fund gradually buying larger or growthier stocks.
Used for
Checking whether a fund still fills the slot you bought it for in your allocation.
What it means for you
Drift can make a record look better (the manager bought what was working) and can quietly change your overall mix. Over 20 years, only 18.18% of surviving mid-cap value funds stayed in their style category (SPIVA, Mid-Year 2026).

Paying for what you do not get

Active fees, index-like portfolios

Closet indexing
What it is
A fund that is marketed and priced as actively managed but whose holdings are so close to its benchmark that it behaves much like an index fund.
Used for
Describing the worst combination for investors: the fee of active management with almost none of its potential.
What it means for you
A closet index fund is nearly certain to trail its benchmark by roughly its extra fee. Check active share, or at least compare the fund's top holdings with the index's.

A useful way to see the problem: think of a fund with 30% active share as 70% index fund plus 30% genuine bets. You could buy the 70% for the price of an index fund. So the whole fee, less what the index part should cost, is really the price of the 30%.

Cost of the active part = [fund fee − (1 − active share) × index fee] ÷ active share

For a 0.85% fund with 30% active share, compared with a 0.05% index fund: [0.85 − 0.70 × 0.05] ÷ 0.30 = 2.72% a year for the part that is actually managed.

Going furtherLarge funds and style boxes

Closet indexing tends to appear in very large active funds, which must spread billions across the same big companies that dominate the index, and in funds whose managers are judged on short-term relative results, which punishes being different. It is not illegal; it is a question of value. The Riveras' six overlapping large-cap funds (INV-004) are worth checking on exactly this point.

Real history and law

Three cases

Case study 1The "stock picker's market" of 2026 that wasn't
What happened
The first half of 2026 had many of the conditions active managers say they need: stock-level dispersion (how differently stocks moved) was the highest since 2008, leadership shifted away from the largest companies, and mid and small caps beat the S&P 500. Still, 67% of large-cap, 74% of mid-cap and 69% of small-cap active funds trailed their benchmarks. Results by style varied enormously: 85% of large-cap growth funds trailed, but only 29% of large-cap value funds.
What it teaches
Favorable conditions raise the possible reward for skill; they do not change the arithmetic of costs. And a manager's result in any short period often reflects a style tilt versus the benchmark more than stock selection.
Source
S&P Dow Jones Indices (2026). SPIVA U.S. Scorecard, Mid-Year 2026.
Case study 2Two layers of fees: the funds-of-funds in Buffett's bet
What happened
In Warren Buffett's ten-year bet (INV-043), the five funds-of-funds owned stakes in more than 200 hedge funds. Investors paid fees to both layers; Buffett estimated fixed fees averaged about 2.5% of assets a year, before performance fees. The best fund-of-funds gained 87.7% over ten years against 125.8% for the S&P 500 index fund.
What it teaches
Every layer of management — a fund of funds, an advisor who picks active funds, a wrap account — adds a fee that the underlying skill must overcome. Layers multiply the hurdle.
Source
Berkshire Hathaway Inc. (2018). 2017 letter to shareholders.
Case study 3The wash-sale rule, as the IRS applies it
The rule
You cannot deduct a loss on stock or securities sold in a wash sale: one where, within 30 days before or after the sale, you buy substantially identical stock or securities, acquire an option to buy them, or acquire them for your IRA or Roth IRA. A purchase by your spouse or a corporation you control also counts. The disallowed loss is added to the cost basis of the new shares.
What it teaches
Tax management is a genuine service, but a technical one. A harvested loss can be lost entirely because a reinvested dividend, an automatic IRA purchase or a spouse's account bought the same fund within the window. Coordinating across every account is part of what a tax-aware manager is paid for.
Source
Internal Revenue Service. Publication 550, Investment income and expenses, "Wash Sales."

Decisions

When active might earn its keep

Scenario 1 · The Harpers

The Harpers' $220,000 taxable account holds 25 technology stocks, some with large gains and a few below cost. Their advisor proposes moving it into a direct-indexing account tracking the whole US market, for an added 0.35% a year, selling the losers now and trimming winners over several years. What should they weigh?

Sell everything nowSimple and it ends the concentration, but realizes every gain in one year, likely at 15% plus the 3.8% net investment income tax, since their income exceeds the $250,000 threshold for married couples.
Weigh the serviceRight. Direct indexing is well suited to exactly this: a taxable account, embedded gains, and a need to diversify over time. The questions are whether the harvested losses and staging are worth 0.35% on top of their 1.25% advisory fee ($770 a year on $220,000), and for how many years.
Keep the stocksPast gains are what created the tax problem, not a reason to keep the risk. A single sector is one large bet (INV-004).
The principle: pay for active management when it solves a problem an index fund cannot, at a price below the value it creates.
Scenario 2 · Ruth

Ruth's IRA holds an active intermediate bond fund charging 0.65% that has beaten the Bloomberg U.S. Aggregate over the last three years. The same company offers a bond index fund at 0.03%. Should she switch?

Trust three yearsThree years is short, and bond funds' short-run wins often come from taking more credit risk, which tends to fail exactly when stocks also fall.
Check the risk, then decideRight. Inside the IRA, switching has no tax cost. If the fund's quality and duration match the index, it might be worth keeping; if not, the index fund gives her a cleaner, cheaper safety cushion.
The principle: in the safe part of a portfolio, judge a manager first by the risk taken, then by the return.
Scenario 3 · Maya

Maya works in health care, so her paycheck already depends on that industry. A brokerage offers to move her $40,000 taxable account into a direct-indexing account that would hold the total US market excluding health-care companies and harvest tax losses along the way, for 0.40% a year. Is that worth it?

Pay for the exclusionThe logic is sound: human capital counts as part of the portfolio (INV-004). But on $40,000, 0.40% is $160 a year against about $12 at 0.03%, for a modest cut in industry exposure, and the account adds tracking error. Selling her current funds to switch could also realize gains.
Fix the big risk cheaplyReasonable. Health care is only one sector of a total-market fund, and her largest risk is not in the portfolio at all: Eli has no special needs trust, and her only life insurance is a 1× salary group policy (INV-087, INV-102). The exclusion makes more sense for larger balances.
The principle: solve the largest risk first with the cheapest tool; buy refinements only when they are worth their cost.

Test the claims

Six claims about active management

Apply it

Where active might fit for each household

No reason yet. Denise's money is in a 401(k), where taxes are deferred and the plan's index or target-date fund is cheapest. Active management offers her nothing an index fund does not.
Check for closet indexing. If any of their six large-cap funds are active, compare their holdings and fees with the plan's index fund. Six overlapping funds are likely a lot of index exposure at active prices.
A small taxable account, a small case. Maya's $40,000 taxable account already holds broad index funds, which rarely distribute capital gains. Tax-aware management might help in a year with losses, but on that balance the fee is hard to recoup; her $290,000 401(k) and $40,000 Roth IRA are sheltered, so harvesting does nothing there.
The strongest case. A large taxable account with embedded gains, a concentration to unwind and high income: tax-aware management can be worth paying for, if its price is judged separately from the 1.25% advisory fee.
Keep the safe part simple. Ruth's money is in a traditional IRA and CDs, so harvesting is irrelevant. For her bonds, an index fund's predictability is worth more than a manager's chance of a small win.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A manager's edge is 1.5% a year before fees, the fee is 1.0% above an index fund, and tracking error is 4%. How many years of results would be needed to prove the after-fee edge at a t-statistic of 2?

Net edge = 1.5 − 1.0 = 0.5%. IR = 0.5 ÷ 4 = 0.125. Years = (2 ÷ 0.125)² = 16² = 256.
Exercise 2

A fund charges 0.85% and has 30% active share; an index fund costs 0.05%. What do you effectively pay for the actively managed part, in percent per year?

The index-like 70% should cost 0.70 × 0.05 = 0.035%. The rest of the fee, 0.85 − 0.035 = 0.815%, buys the 30%: 0.815 ÷ 0.30 = 2.72%.
Exercise 3

An index holds stocks A, B and C at 25%, 25% and 50%. A fund holds A and B at 50% each and no C. What is its active share, in percent?

Differences: |50 − 25| = 25, |50 − 25| = 25, |0 − 50| = 50. Sum = 100; half = 50%.
Exercise 4

An investor harvests $20,000 of capital losses and uses them to offset $20,000 of long-term gains that would have been taxed at 15%. How much tax is deferred this year, in dollars?

20,000 × 0.15 = $3,000. It is deferred rather than erased, because the replacement shares have a lower cost basis.
