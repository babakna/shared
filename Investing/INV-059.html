Record Keeping and Consolidation

The least glamorous module in the course may save your family the most trouble. Old 401(k)s get forgotten, beneficiary forms go out of date, and nobody else knows where the accounts are or how to reach them. This module shows how to move old accounts safely, simplify what you own, audit your beneficiaries, and build the file your family will need.

The question this module answersIf something happened to me tomorrow, could my family find everything, and would it go where I intend?

Start here

Every job leaves an account behind

Americans change jobs many times over a career, and each workplace retirement plan stays behind unless you move it. Add a bank account here, an old brokerage app there, an IRA opened years ago, and by middle age many people have accounts they seldom look at and one or two they have forgotten.

Scattered accounts are not a disaster; the money is still yours. But they cost you in four ways: higher fees for small, forgotten balances; an allocation you cannot see as a whole; beneficiary forms that were filled in years ago and never updated; and, if something happens to you, a scavenger hunt for your family. Congress thought the problem large enough to order the Department of Labor to build a national search tool for lost retirement accounts (Case studies tab).

The fix has three parts: consolidate what can sensibly be combined, audit who inherits each account, and record where everything is in one place your family can reach.

What this module covers

  1. Your options for an old 401(k), and the rules for rolling one over: direct versus indirect, the 60-day rule and 20% withholding, as the IRS states them.
  2. Consolidating accounts, and finding lost ones.
  3. A beneficiary audit, and why the form usually beats the will.
  4. The family financial file, a letter of instruction, and planning digital access.
  5. How long to keep records, from the IRS's own guidance.

When you leave a job

Four things you can do with an old workplace plan

When you leave an employer, your vested 401(k), 403(b) or governmental 457(b) balance is yours. You generally have four choices, and only one of them is usually a mistake.

Eligible rollover distribution
What it is
A payment from a retirement plan or IRA that the tax law lets you roll into another plan or IRA without tax. Most pre-retirement payouts qualify.
Used for
Moving retirement money between accounts while keeping it tax-deferred.
What it means for you
Some payments cannot be rolled over, including required minimum distributions, hardship distributions, loans treated as distributions and a series of substantially equal payments (IRS). An old plan's balance at a job change normally can be.

Small balances can move without you

If you leave an employer and do not choose, the plan may act on its own for small accounts. According to the IRS, if your account is $1,000 or less, the plan may pay it to you, usually with 20% withheld, without your consent; if it is between $1,000 and $5,000, the plan may deposit it into an IRA in your name. The SECURE 2.0 (Setting Every Community Up for Retirement Enhancement) Act of 2022 permits plans to raise that $5,000 involuntary cash-out limit to $7,000 (IRS Notice 2024-3), so the upper limit depends on your plan. Either way, a small old account can end up in an IRA you did not open, at an institution you did not pick — one more reason to decide yourself.

How the money moves

Direct rollover, trustee-to-trustee transfer, or a check to you

Direct rollover
What it is
The plan pays your balance straight to the new plan or IRA — electronically, or by a check made payable to the new account. The IRS: "No taxes will be withheld from your transfer amount."
Used for
Moving a workplace plan balance with no withholding, no deadline and no tax.
What it means for you
When you leave a job, ask the old plan for a direct rollover and give it the receiving account's details. This is almost always the right method.
Trustee-to-trustee transfer
What it is
The IRA version of a direct rollover: one IRA custodian sends the money directly to another. Because the money is never paid to you, the IRS does not treat it as a rollover at all.
Used for
Consolidating IRAs, or moving an IRA to a cheaper provider.
What it means for you
You can do as many as you like; the one-rollover-per-year limit below does not apply.
Indirect (60-day) rollover
What it is
The money is paid to you, and you deposit it into a plan or IRA yourself. The IRS: "You have 60 days from the date you receive an IRA or retirement plan distribution to roll it over to another plan or IRA."
Used for
Rarely by choice; usually it happens because a check was made out to the account owner.
What it means for you
From a workplace plan, 20% is withheld even if you intend to roll it over, and you must replace that 20% from other money to roll over the full amount.

The 20% withholding trap, in the IRS's own example

The IRS illustrates the rule with a 42-year-old who receives a $10,000 eligible rollover distribution from a 401(k); the employer withholds $2,000. If the $8,000 received is rolled over but the $2,000 is not, then $2,000 is taxable income, and the 10% additional tax on early distributions applies to it unless an exception does. To roll over the full $10,000, the person must add $2,000 from other savings within 60 days; then the entire distribution is tax-free, and the $2,000 withheld is reported as taxes paid.

Not rolled over = distribution − (amount received + amount replaced)
Cost ≈ not rolled over × (tax rate + 10% if under 59½)

At the default settings — a $60,000 payout at age 45, a 27% combined tax rate and nothing replaced — $12,000 is withheld and only $48,000 reaches the new account. The missing $12,000 becomes taxable income: about $3,240 of income tax plus a $1,200 additional tax, $4,440 in all. Miss the 60-day deadline entirely and the whole $60,000 is taxable, about $22,200. A direct rollover costs nothing.

The one-rollover-per-year rule

Since 2015 you can make only one IRA-to-IRA rollover in any 12-month period, counting all your IRAs together — traditional, Roth, SEP (Simplified Employee Pension) and SIMPLE (Savings Incentive Match Plan for Employees) IRAs (IRS Announcements 2014-15 and 2014-32). The IRS states that the limit does not apply to conversions from traditional to Roth IRAs, trustee-to-trustee transfers, IRA-to-plan rollovers, plan-to-IRA rollovers, or plan-to-plan rollovers. A second IRA-to-IRA rollover within 12 months is taxable, may owe the 10% additional tax, and if deposited may be treated as an excess contribution taxed at 6% a year while it remains.

Going furtherIf you miss the 60 days

The IRS may waive the 60-day requirement when the deadline was missed because of circumstances beyond your control. There are three routes: an automatic waiver in certain cases where a financial institution made an error; self-certification, using the model letter in Revenue Procedure 2016-47 (as updated), if one of the reasons listed in that letter kept you from finishing in time; and a private letter ruling, in which the IRS weighs factors such as death, disability, hospitalization, incarceration, serious illness, restrictions imposed by a foreign country and postal error. The IRS can waive only the 60-day requirement, not the one-rollover-per-year rule. A self-certified rollover must be completed as soon as practicable, usually within 30 days after the obstacle ends, and the IRS stresses that self-certification is not a waiver by the IRS: an audit can still find that you did not qualify.

Practitioner depthIRA payouts are withheld differently

The 20% mandatory rate applies to eligible rollover distributions paid to you from workplace plans. An IRA distribution paid to you is subject to 10% withholding unless you elect out or choose a different amount (IRS). Two further points: rolling pre-tax plan money to a Roth IRA is permitted but taxable as a conversion; and employer stock distributed from a 401(k) can qualify for special treatment of its net unrealized appreciation (IRS Publication 575), which depends on how the shares leave the plan, so check with a tax professional before rolling over a plan that holds appreciated company shares.

Fewer, better accounts

What to combine, what to keep separate, and how to find lost accounts

Account consolidation
What it is
Combining accounts of the same tax type — several old 401(k)s and IRAs into one rollover IRA, several brokerage accounts into one — by direct rollover or transfer.
Used for
Lower fees, one clear view of your allocation, fewer beneficiary forms and simpler withdrawals.
What it means for you
You cannot merge different tax types without tax consequences: pre-tax, Roth and taxable money stay in separate accounts. The goal is one account of each type, not one account.

With the defaults — $150,000 across five accounts at a 0.55% average expense ratio and $40 a year in account fees each — costs are about $1,025 a year. Consolidated into two accounts using 0.08% index funds, about $200. After 20 years at 6% the consolidated portfolio is about $46,600 larger. Roughly $42,000 of that comes from the cheaper funds and only about $4,000 from the account fees, which is the usual pattern: consolidation pays mostly because it is a chance to fix the investments.

Consolidation also simplifies retirement withdrawals

The IRS rules for required minimum distributions reward having fewer workplace plans. An IRA owner "must calculate the RMD separately for each IRA they own but can withdraw the total amount from one or more of the IRAs." But RMDs from 401(k) and 457(b) plans "must be taken separately from each of those plan accounts." Ruth, with one IRA, has one calculation and one withdrawal. Someone with four old 401(k)s at 73 would face four.

Finding accounts you have lost

  • The Department of Labor's Retirement Savings Lost and Found (lostandfound.dol.gov) searches for private-sector pension and 401(k)-type plans linked to your Social Security number. Sign-in uses Login.gov.
  • Old employers and their plan administrators. Your last statement names the recordkeeper; a merged or closed employer's plan usually moved to a successor.
  • State unclaimed-property offices hold abandoned bank and brokerage balances; each state runs its own search.
  • Your own records. Old statements, and tax forms such as a Form 1099-R from a past distribution, name institutions you may have forgotten.

Who inherits

The form usually beats the will

Retirement accounts, life insurance and many bank and brokerage accounts pass by a beneficiary designation or a transfer-on-death registration, not by your will. The institution pays whoever is named on its form. If the form is out of date, your will cannot fix it.

Primary and contingent beneficiaries
What it is
The primary beneficiary inherits first. A contingent (secondary) beneficiary inherits only if no primary beneficiary survives you or all primaries disclaim.
Used for
Making sure an account always has a living, intended recipient, so it does not fall back to your estate.
What it means for you
Every account should name both. Ruth names Anna as primary; she should name a contingent too, such as Anna's children or a trust (INV-085, INV-087).
Spousal rights in workplace plans
What it is
Under federal law most 401(k) plans avoid the annuity rules only by paying the death benefit in full to the surviving spouse unless the spouse has consented to another beneficiary (IRS). Plans must require a plan representative or notary to witness a spouse's consent.
Used for
Protecting spouses from being disinherited from workplace retirement savings.
What it means for you
If you are married and want to name someone other than your spouse on a 401(k), your spouse must sign. IRAs follow different rules, and state law may matter.

A ten-minute beneficiary audit

  1. List every account and policy: workplace plans (current and old), IRAs, Roth IRAs, health savings accounts (HSAs), life insurance, annuities, bank and brokerage accounts with transfer-on-death or payable-on-death registrations.
  2. Log in, or call, and read the beneficiary actually on file for each. Do not rely on memory.
  3. Check each name against your life today: marriages, divorces, births, deaths.
  4. Name a contingent everywhere. Avoid naming a minor directly; a child cannot manage the money, and a court may have to appoint someone (INV-085).
  5. Make sure the forms and your will point the same way, then save a copy or screenshot of each confirmation in your family file.

Make it findable

A family financial file, and a plan for your digital life

If you were unable to manage your affairs tomorrow, someone would need to know what you own, where it is, whom to call, and how to get in. The family financial file answers those questions in one place. It can be a binder, an encrypted digital folder, or both; what matters is that it is complete, current, and that the right person knows where it is.

Letter of instruction
What it is
An informal, plain-language letter to your family or executor that explains where things are and what you want done. It is not a legal document and does not replace a will.
Used for
Listing accounts, advisors, passwords' location, bills on autopay, funeral wishes and anything a will does not cover.
What it means for you
It is the cover page of your family file. Update it at each annual review (INV-058).
Section of the fileWhat goes in it
1. PeopleExecutor, agents under your power of attorney and health care directive, attorney, tax preparer, advisor, insurance agents, with contact details
2. AccountsEvery bank, brokerage, retirement, HSA and 529 account: institution, type, last four digits, beneficiary on file
3. Income and benefitsEmployer and pension contacts, Social Security, annuities, and how each pays
4. InsuranceLife, disability, long-term care, home, auto and umbrella policies, with policy numbers
5. Property and debtsDeed, mortgage, vehicle titles, loans, credit cards
6. Legal documentsWill, trust, powers of attorney, health care directive (or where the originals are kept)
7. TaxesRecent returns and the records needed to support them (next tab)
8. Digital accessWhere the password manager or password list is, and which accounts have legacy settings

Digital assets: who can get in, legally

Email, photos, online-only bank and brokerage accounts, and even crypto wallets are "digital assets," and the companies that hold them are "custodians." The Uniform Law Commission's summary of the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act explains that access is usually governed by a terms-of-service agreement rather than property law. The Act, which states adopt individually, sets a three-tier order of priority when instructions conflict:

  1. An online tool comes first. If a company offers a tool, separate from its general terms, to name someone to access or delete your data, the Act makes your online instructions legally enforceable.
  2. Then your documents. Without an online tool, directions in a will, trust, power of attorney or other written record are enforceable.
  3. Then the terms of service. If you gave no instructions, the account's terms decide, and the Act's default rules fill any gaps. By default a fiduciary may never see the content of your messages without your consent, though they may get a catalogue (who sent what, and when).

Two widely used online tools: Apple's Legacy Contact lets you choose someone who can request access to certain data after your death using an access key you generate plus a death certificate; it does not include purchased media or iCloud Keychain passwords (Apple). Google's Inactive Account Manager lets you set an inactivity period and name up to 10 people to be notified and, if you choose, to download data you select (Google). Because the online tool ranks first, setting these up is one of the most effective ten-minute tasks in estate planning.

⚠️

Do not write passwords into your will. A will can become a public record in probate. Put credentials in a password manager or a sealed, secure list, and put only its location and how to open it in the family file.

Keep, then shred

Record retention, from the IRS's own rules

The IRS ties record keeping to the period of limitations: the time during which you can amend a return to claim a refund, or the IRS can assess more tax. Keep records that support a return until that period ends. The IRS's guidance:

SituationKeep records for
General rule (none of the situations below)3 years
You file a claim for credit or refund after filing3 years from filing or 2 years from paying the tax, whichever is later
You claim a loss from worthless securities or a bad-debt deduction7 years
You fail to report income that is more than 25% of the gross income on your return6 years
You do not file a return, or file a fraudulent oneIndefinitely
Employment tax records (if you employ someone)At least 4 years after the tax is due or paid, whichever is later
Records connected to propertyUntil the period of limitations expires for the year you dispose of the property

Source: Internal Revenue Service, How long should I keep records? (last reviewed June 30, 2026).

What the property rule means for investors

Investments are property. The records that prove your cost basis — purchase confirmations, reinvested dividends, records of inherited or gifted shares — must be kept until the period of limitations ends for the year you sell. For shares bought in 2010 and sold in 2030, that is roughly 2034. Brokers report basis for "covered securities," which the IRS says are generally securities acquired after 2010, with certain exceptions (IRS Publication 550). For anything older, or moved between firms, your own records may be the only proof.

By the same logic, keep the forms that prove money in an IRA was already taxed — such as the Form 8606 filed for nondeductible contributions — until the account has been fully withdrawn and the limitations period for that last year has passed. Without them you may pay tax twice on the same dollars.

Going furtherBeyond the IRS minimum

Many people keep copies of filed tax returns themselves (not every receipt) much longer than three years, because a return is a compact record of income, IRA contributions and carryforwards. Other documents have no IRS clock at all: wills, trusts, deeds, marriage and divorce records, and beneficiary confirmations should be kept until they are replaced. Scanning to an encrypted, backed-up folder lets you shred paper safely.

Real law, real consequences

Three cases every account owner should know

Case study 1Kennedy v. Plan Administrator for DuPont Savings and Investment Plan (2009)
What happened
William Kennedy named his wife, Liv, as beneficiary of his employer savings plan. Their divorce decree divested Liv of her interest in the plan, but William never changed the beneficiary form. When he died, his daughter Kari, as executrix of his estate, claimed the money. The plan paid Liv. The Supreme Court unanimously upheld the payment on January 26, 2009, holding that the administrator had properly paid according to the plan documents.
What it teaches
For workplace plans, the beneficiary form on file controls. A divorce decree, a will or anyone's intentions may not override it. The only reliable fix is to change the form.
Source
Supreme Court of the United States, Kennedy v. Plan Administrator for DuPont Savings and Investment Plan, 555 U.S. 285 (2009) (syllabus, Legal Information Institute).
Case study 2Bobrow v. Commissioner (2014) and the one-rollover-per-year rule
What happened
For decades the IRS's own publication had read the once-a-year limit on IRA rollovers as applying to each IRA separately. In 2014 the Tax Court held that a taxpayer cannot make a non-taxable IRA-to-IRA rollover if he has already made one from any of his IRAs in the preceding year (Bobrow v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2014-21). The IRS adopted that reading for distributions beginning in 2015.
What it teaches
Moving IRA money by sending checks to yourself is risky even for sophisticated people. A trustee-to-trustee transfer is not a rollover and is not limited.
Source
Internal Revenue Service, Rollovers of retirement plan and IRA distributions; IRS Announcements 2014-15 and 2014-32.
Case study 3The Retirement Savings Lost and Found (2024)
What happened
The SECURE 2.0 Act of 2022 directed the Department of Labor to create an online, searchable database of retirement plans by December 29, 2024. The Employee Benefits Security Administration (EBSA) began collecting data from plans in November 2024. The public site lets workers search for private-sector pension and 401(k)-type plans linked to their Social Security number. EBSA reports that since 2017 its enforcement efforts have recovered more than $7 billion for missing participants and beneficiaries.
What it teaches
Lost retirement accounts are common enough to need a national search tool. Consolidating old accounts, and keeping a list in your family file, means neither you nor your heirs will need it.
Source
U.S. Department of Labor, Employee Benefits Security Administration, news release (November 18, 2024) and Retirement Savings Lost and Found database.

Decisions

Three record-keeping decisions

Scenario 1 · Jordan's old 401(k)

Jordan has left their employer with $84,000 in the 401(k), including no company stock (the employer shares were bought separately through the stock purchase plan). The old plan's funds are expensive. What should Jordan do?

Check to JordanThe plan must withhold 20%, $16,800. Unless Jordan replaces it from savings within 60 days, that $16,800 becomes taxable, plus the 10% additional tax at 45.
Direct rolloverRight. No withholding, no deadline, no tax, and a chance to choose low-cost index funds. The new account's beneficiary form starts blank: fill it in the same day.
Cash outThe full $84,000 would be taxable and hit with the 10% additional tax; at a 27% combined rate that is about $31,000 gone, and the retirement plan loses 60% of its savings.
The principle: move retirement money institution-to-institution, never through your own hands.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers' second rollover

Rachel received a $40,000 check from one of her IRAs to move it to a cheaper provider, and plans to deposit it within 60 days. Eight months ago she did the same with her other IRA. What is the risk?

Inside 60 daysThe 60-day rule is not the only rule. The one-per-year limit counts all her IRAs together.
Second rollover problemRight. The $40,000 would be taxable income, and deposited amounts could be taxed at 6% a year as an excess contribution while they remain. She should talk to a tax professional at once, and use trustee-to-trustee transfers from now on.
The principle: for moving IRAs, ask the new custodian to pull the money; do not take a check.
Scenario 3 · Ruth's audit

Walter died in 2023. Ruth's IRA beneficiary form names "my spouse, Walter" as primary and Anna as contingent. Her email, photos and online bank log-ins exist only in her head. What should she do first?

Leave itAnna probably inherits, but the form is out of date and names no living contingent after her. If Anna died first, the IRA could fall to Ruth's estate, with slower access and fewer options.
Update and documentRight. A current form, a living contingent, online legacy tools (which rank first under the uniform digital-assets act) and a letter of instruction take an afternoon and spare Anna months.
The principle: after every death, marriage or divorce, re-read every beneficiary form.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

Jordan's old plan sends an $84,000 eligible rollover distribution as a check made out to Jordan. How much must the plan withhold, in dollars?

The mandatory rate is 20%: 0.20 × 84,000 = $16,800. Jordan receives $67,200.
Exercise 2

Jordan deposits only the $67,200 within 60 days. With a 27% combined tax rate and the 10% additional tax (Jordan is 45), what is the tax cost on the part not rolled over, in dollars?

$16,800 is not rolled over, so it is taxable: 16,800 × 0.27 = $4,536, plus 16,800 × 0.10 = $1,680. Total $6,216. The $16,800 withheld is credited against it at filing.
Exercise 3

You file your 2026 tax return on April 15, 2027. None of the IRS's special situations apply. Under the general three-year rule, until what year must you keep the records that support it? (Enter the year.)

Three years from filing in April 2027 runs to April 2030. Records of the cost basis of anything you still own are different: keep those until three years after the return for the year you finally sell.
Exercise 4

$150,000 sits in funds with an average expense ratio of 0.55%. How much do the fund expenses cost per year, in dollars?

150,000 × 0.0055 = $825 a year. At 0.08% it would be $120.
