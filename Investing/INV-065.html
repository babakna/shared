Analyzing a Rental Property

A rental is a small business with one product, housing, and a long list of costs. This module walks through the income statement a lender would build, the four ratios investors use to judge a deal, why the popular 1% rule misleads, and a full analyzer that projects cash flow, equity and return year by year.

The question this module answersWill this property actually make money, and how much, for the cash I put in?

Start here

The listing says it cash-flows. Does it?

Rental property has built real wealth for many American families, and a well-bought, well-run rental can pay its owner for decades. It is also the investment where the gap between the sales pitch and the arithmetic is widest.

A typical listing or seminar example takes the rent, subtracts the mortgage payment, property tax and insurance, and calls what is left "cash flow." On the illustrative Phoenix house used throughout this module — $300,000, renting for $2,100 a month, 25% down at 7.5% — that shortcut shows about +$252 a month. Add the costs every landlord eventually pays — empty months, repairs, replacing the roof and water heater, and a manager or your own time — and the same house loses about $427 a month. This module teaches you to build the full picture before you buy.

What this module covers

  1. Building net operating income (NOI): rent, vacancy and every operating expense.
  2. Cap rate: what the property earns on its price, and how it sets value.
  3. Returns on your cash: cash flow, cash-on-cash, debt service coverage and the internal rate of return (IRR).
  4. The 1% rule, and why passing it proves little.
  5. A rental analyzer with a year-by-year projection.

The income statement

Net operating income, line by line

Gross potential rent and vacancy
What it is
Gross potential rent is twelve months of full rent from every unit. Vacancy and credit loss are the rent you do not collect: empty weeks between tenants, and tenants who do not pay.
Used for
Subtracting vacancy (and adding any other income, such as pet or laundry fees) gives effective gross income, the rent you can actually expect.
What it means for you
One empty month a year is 1 ÷ 12 = 8.3% of rent. The national rental vacancy rate was 7.3% in the second quarter of 2026 (Census Bureau); your market and your property may differ.
Operating expenses
What it is
Everything it costs to run the property: property tax, landlord insurance, repairs and maintenance, capital reserves for big replacements, management, any utilities the owner pays, homeowners association (HOA) dues, legal and accounting costs, and leasing costs.
Used for
Subtracted from effective gross income to get NOI. Loan payments, depreciation and income taxes are not operating expenses; they depend on how you finance and who you are, not on the property.
What it means for you
New investors most often underestimate this line, especially reserves: a roof, an air-conditioning system or a water heater is certain to need replacing; only the year is unknown.
NOI = gross potential rent − vacancy and credit loss + other income − operating expenses

Going furtherRepairs versus improvements

The IRS distinguishes repairs, deductible in the year paid, from improvements, which must be capitalized and depreciated. An expense is an improvement if it results in a betterment to the property, restores it, or adapts it to a new use (IRS Publication 527). For cash-flow analysis both are real costs; for taxes they are treated differently. INV-068 covers depreciation, which spreads a residential rental building's cost over 27.5 years.

What the property earns

Cap rate: the property's yield before financing

Capitalization rate (cap rate)
What it is
NOI divided by the property's price or value. Our example: $13,760 ÷ $300,000 = 4.59%.
Used for
Comparing properties on equal terms, regardless of how each buyer finances them, and estimating value: value = NOI ÷ market cap rate.
What it means for you
It is the return you would earn in the first year if you paid all cash, before any change in value. Compare it with what safe money earns: a 4.59% cap rate is below the 5.18% yield on 10-year Treasuries on September 24, 2026.
Cap rate = NOI ÷ price     Value = NOI ÷ cap rate
Going furtherWhy cap rates move with interest rates

A cap rate is an income yield, so investors compare it with bond yields and borrowing costs. When interest rates rise, buyers demand higher cap rates, which, for the same NOI, means lower prices; when rates fall, the reverse. Rents also change, so the link is loose, but it is why rising rates can hurt property values even while rents rise. INV-066 shows the same force through a lender's eyes.

Practitioner depthCap rates on single-family homes

Single-family houses are mostly priced by owner-occupants, not investors, so their prices often imply cap rates well below those of apartment buildings. A house that is expensive as a rental may be fairly priced as a home. That is why many single-family rentals in high-priced markets show thin or negative cash flow, and why their investors are, in effect, betting on appreciation.

Add the loan

Cash flow, cash-on-cash, coverage and IRR

Cash-on-cash return
What it is
Annual cash flow (NOI minus loan payments) divided by the cash you put in: down payment, closing costs and initial repairs. Our example: −$5,118 ÷ $84,000 = −6.1%.
Used for
Measuring the cash yield on your own money after financing.
What it means for you
A negative number means you feed the property every month. It ignores principal repayment and price changes, so it is not the whole return, but it is the part that hits your budget.
Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR)
What it is
NOI divided by annual loan payments. Our example: $13,760 ÷ $18,879 = 0.73.
Used for
Lenders' main test of whether a property can carry its loan. Below 1.0, the rent does not cover the mortgage even before you pay yourself anything.
What it means for you
Each lender sets its own minimum, and lenders that underwrite on property income generally want coverage comfortably above 1.0 (INV-066). A 0.73 means the loan is too big for the income.
Total return and IRR
What it is
A rental pays you four ways: cash flow, principal paydown (tenants' rent repays your loan), appreciation, and tax effects such as depreciation (INV-068). The internal rate of return (IRR) combines the timing of all cash in and out into one annual rate.
Used for
Comparing a rental's whole return with other investments, such as an index fund or a REIT (INV-069, INV-071).
What it means for you
Check how much of the IRR depends on appreciation. If the deal only works when prices rise, it is a leveraged bet on prices, not an income investment.

A shortcut, examined

Why passing the 1% rule proves little

The 1% rule
What it is
A screen that says monthly rent should be at least 1% of the purchase price: a $200,000 house should rent for $2,000 or more.
Used for
Quickly discarding listings that cannot possibly work, before spending time on a full analysis.
What it means for you
Failing the rule is a warning; passing it is not a green light. The rule ignores the expenses that decide the answer: property tax, insurance, the age and condition of the building, and the interest rate.

Three more reasons to treat the rule with suspicion. It ignores the loan rate: at 7.5%, even a property that passes can lose money monthly. It says nothing about the neighborhood, which drives vacancy, tenant quality and appreciation. And properties that pass it in expensive regions are often the ones with problems the price already reflects.

Going furtherBetter quick screens

If you want a fast filter, estimate NOI with a realistic expense ratio (operating expenses as a share of effective rent for that age and type of property in that market) and compute the cap rate and DSCR at today's loan rate. Two minutes with those numbers tells you more than the 1% rule, and the analyzer in the next tab does it for you.

Run the numbers

A rental analyzer with a multi-year projection

Enter a property. The analyzer builds year-1 NOI, computes the four ratios, then projects each year: rent and expenses grow, the loan amortizes, value changes, and at the end it sells, pays selling costs and the loan, and computes your IRR and the cash you get back per dollar invested.

At the defaults: NOI $13,760, cap rate 4.59%, cash flow −$427 a month, cash-on-cash −6.1%, DSCR 0.73, rent at 0.70% of price, and a 10-year IRR of about 5.4% with $1.82 back per dollar. Three experiments are worth running:

  1. Set growth to zero (rent, expenses and value). The IRR falls to about −5.7%: without appreciation, this deal loses money.
  2. Pay all cash (100% down). The IRR rises to about 6.7% and the cash-on-cash return to 4.45%, because the loan costs more than the property earns. This is negative leverage, explained in INV-066.
  3. Drop vacancy to 0% and reserves to 0%. The numbers improve sharply — which is how a listing's pro forma is often made to look better.
Practitioner depthWhat the analyzer assumes

Repairs and reserves are a percentage of potential rent; management is a percentage of collected rent; taxes, insurance and fixed costs grow at their own rate. Sale proceeds = value × (1 − selling cost) − loan balance. IRR is computed on annual cash flows, before income taxes, with no refinancing. Depreciation typically reduces taxes during ownership and is partly recaptured at sale (INV-068). For comparison with owning index funds instead, try Babak's Stock vs Real Estate Calculator.

Real data and a labelled illustration

Three lessons about rental numbers

Case study 1Vacancy through the cycle
What happened
The national rental vacancy rate averaged 10.6% in 2009, after the housing bust, and 5.8% in 2022, when rentals were scarce. In the second quarter of 2026 it was 7.3%, up from 7.0% a year earlier.
What it teaches
Vacancy is cyclical. A deal underwritten in the tight market of 2022 with 3% vacancy would have understated its risk. Use a vacancy figure that survives a bad year, not the best one.
Source
U.S. Census Bureau, Housing Vacancies and Homeownership (release of July 28, 2026; annual averages via FRED).
Case study 2Rents lagged prices for 25 years
What happened
From 2000 to 2025, national home prices rose about 3.1-fold and rents about 2.4-fold (Figure 65.6). A house that earned 8% of its price in gross rent in 2000 would, at the same rent growth, earn about 6% of its 2025 price.
What it teaches
When yields compress, new buyers get less income per dollar and more of their return must come from further appreciation. Past investors' results partly reflect prices rising faster than rents, which cannot continue indefinitely.
Source
FRED, series CSUSHPINSA and CUSR0000SEHA (2026); ratio computed for this module.
IllustrationThe pro forma that left things out
The setup
A fictional listing for our $300,000 house advertises "$252 a month positive cash flow": $2,100 rent minus the $1,573 loan payment, $150 of tax and $125 of insurance.
The full analysis
Adding 7% vacancy ($147 a month), repairs and reserves at 8% of rent each ($336) and management at 10% of collected rent ($195) turns +$252 into −$427 a month, a $679 swing.
What it teaches
Every line left out of a pro forma is a cost the buyer pays later. Rebuild every seller or promoter projection yourself.

Decisions

Two households consider a rental

Scenario 1 · The Riveras

A coworker tells the Riveras to use their $41,000 of idle cash to buy a $300,000 rental house in Phoenix. With 15% down and closing costs they would need about $54,000, and at 7.5% the house would lose about $636 a month at the start. What should they do?

Buy itPhoenix prices fell 56% from 2006 to 2011 and about 5% from mid-2022 to mid-2026. At 15% down the 10-year return ranges from a loss of about 13% a year to a gain of about 11.5%, depending only on growth, and a $636 monthly shortfall would strain a family with two children.
Pass for nowThe sound choice. Their base is not finished: no 529 plans yet and modest retirement savings. A rental that needs feeding each month adds risk they do not need. If they want real estate exposure, a low-cost REIT index fund is liquid and diversified (INV-069).
Borrow against their homeThis stacks leverage on leverage: a variable-rate HELOC on their own house to fund a money-losing rental. If Phoenix prices fell, both properties would lose value at once (INV-066).
The principle: buy a rental that works on realistic numbers today, with money you can afford to tie up; do not buy one that needs appreciation to rescue it.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers

The Harpers consider selling part of their $220,000 taxable portfolio of technology stocks to buy a $250,000 Raleigh condo for cash, as a rental "for retirement income." What should they weigh?

Rent is saferA single condo is one tenant, one building and one HOA. Vacancy, a special assessment or a bad tenant can wipe out a year's income. It swaps one concentration (tech stocks) for another.
Compare carefullyRight. An all-cash condo returns its cap rate plus or minus value changes; if that is 4% to 5% before their time and taxes, it may not beat a diversified bond-and-stock mix. Selling appreciated stock has a tax cost (INV-035, INV-037), which applies whichever way they diversify.
The principle: judge a rental against the alternatives on the same, after-cost, after-time basis.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A house rents for $1,800 a month. Vacancy is 5% and operating expenses are $7,200 a year. What is the NOI, in dollars?

Potential rent $21,600; less 5% vacancy = $20,520 effective income; less $7,200 = $13,320.
Exercise 2

The same house costs $240,000. What is its cap rate, in percent?

$13,320 ÷ $240,000 = 0.0555 = 5.55%.
Exercise 3

A property's NOI is $14,000. Its $180,000, 30-year loan at 7% costs $1,197.54 a month. What is the DSCR? (Two decimals.)

Annual debt service = $1,197.54 × 12 = $14,370.53. DSCR = $14,000 ÷ $14,370.53 = 0.97: the rent does not quite cover the loan.
Exercise 4

You invest $60,000 of cash (down payment and closing costs). NOI is $16,500 and loan payments are $13,800 a year. What is your cash-on-cash return, in percent?

Cash flow = $16,500 − $13,800 = $2,700. $2,700 ÷ $60,000 = 4.5%.
