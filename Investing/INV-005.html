How Markets and Brokers Work

When you press "Buy," a chain of firms and rules moves your order, finds a seller, transfers the shares and holds them safely for you. Knowing that chain helps you pay less to trade, choose the right kind of order, and understand exactly what is protected if a firm fails.

The question this module answersWhat actually happens to my money and my shares, and who protects them?

Start here

A marketplace, not a store

In a store, the price is on the tag and the store is the seller. A stock market is different: it is a marketplace where buyers and sellers meet, and the price is simply wherever the most recent buyer and seller agreed to trade.

You cannot walk onto a trading floor yourself. Instead, a licensed firm called a broker carries your order to the market, a trading venue matches it with someone on the other side, and a clearinghouse makes sure both sides deliver. Figure 5.1 shows the full path.

Where shares come from

New issues and the market for used shares

A company raises money by selling new shares or bonds to investors. That first sale happens once, in the primary market — for stocks, often through an initial public offering (IPO). After that, investors trade those same shares with each other in the secondary market. Almost every trade an individual makes is secondary: the company is not involved and receives nothing.

Going furtherIPOs for individual investors

Most IPO shares are allocated to large institutional clients of the underwriting banks. Individuals usually buy on the first trading day in the secondary market, often after the price has already jumped. Academic research over several decades (for example, work by Jay Ritter on long-run IPO performance) has found that newly public companies have, on average, lagged comparable established companies over the following years. Buying popular IPOs on day one is closer to speculation than investing.

Your gateway

Brokers and the account types they hold

Brokerage account
What it is
An account at a broker-dealer registered with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and a member of FINRA (the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority), through which you buy, sell and hold investments.
Used for
Holding stocks, bonds, funds and cash; the same firm can hold several account types for you.
What it means for you
The account type decides the tax treatment; the investments inside decide the risk. An individual retirement account (IRA) is not an investment — it is a tax wrapper you put investments into.

What brokers charge now

Since 2019, most large US brokerages have charged no commission for online trades of US stocks and exchange-traded funds (ETFs), and many offer fractional shares, letting you invest by the dollar rather than the share. "No commission" does not mean "no cost," though: brokers can earn from the bid-ask spread, from payment for order flow, from lending out securities, and from the interest on your uninvested cash.

💰

Check your cash. Uninvested cash in many brokerage accounts is "swept" into a bank deposit or money market fund. Some sweep programs pay far less than a money market fund you could choose yourself. Look up your account's sweep rate; on a large cash balance the difference can be hundreds or thousands of dollars a year.

How prices form

Every stock has two prices at once

At any moment there is a highest price someone is willing to pay (the bid) and a lowest price someone is willing to accept (the ask, or offer). Buy immediately and you pay the ask; sell immediately and you receive the bid. The gap between them is the bid-ask spread — a small cost you pay on every round trip.

Liquidity
What it is
How easily something can be bought or sold quickly, in size, without moving its price. Liquid markets have narrow spreads and deep order books.
Used for
Judging trading costs, and how quickly you could raise cash from an investment.
What it means for you
Large, popular index funds and big-company stocks are extremely liquid. Small stocks, some bonds, real estate and private investments are not — you may wait, or accept a discount, to sell.
Practitioner depthMarket makers, wholesalers and payment for order flow

Many retail orders never reach an exchange. Brokers route them to wholesale market makers, which fill them from their own inventory, often at a price slightly better than the best displayed bid or ask ("price improvement"), and pay the broker a small fee per order — payment for order flow. Supporters point to zero commissions and price improvement; critics point to the conflict of interest in routing. The SEC has studied the practice repeatedly, including in its October 2021 staff report on early-2021 market events. Brokers must disclose their routing practices in quarterly reports under SEC Rule 606.

Tell the broker what you want

Market, limit, stop and stop-limit orders

An order tells your broker what to do and under what conditions. The trade-off is always the same: certainty of getting filled versus certainty of price. Run each order type through the simulated trading day below.

OrderWhat it doesGuaranteesMain risk
MarketBuy or sell now at the best available price.A fill (in liquid securities)The price, especially in fast markets or thin stocks
LimitBuy at or below, or sell at or above, a price you set.Your price or betterNo fill at all if the price never reaches your limit
Stop (stop-loss)Becomes a market order once the price reaches the stop.That the order is triggeredA fill well below the stop in a sudden drop ("gap")
Stop-limitBecomes a limit order once the stop is reached.No fill worse than the limitNo fill if the price falls straight through the limit
💡

A practical default. For a long-term investor buying a broad ETF during regular hours, a market order and a limit order set slightly above the ask usually end up nearly identical. Limit orders matter most for thinly traded securities, large orders, volatile moments and anything traded outside regular hours.

When and how trades complete

Hours, settlement and circuit breakers

Settlement (T+1)
What it is
The actual exchange of cash and securities after a trade. Since May 28, 2024, the standard US settlement cycle for most securities is T+1: one business day after the trade date, down from two.
Used for
Knowing when sale proceeds are truly available, and when you legally own purchased shares.
What it means for you
Sell on a Monday and the cash settles Tuesday. In a cash account, buying and then selling with unsettled funds can trigger trading restrictions; your broker's rules explain the details.
Market-wide circuit breakers
What it is
Automatic trading halts across all US stock markets when the S&P 500 falls sharply from the prior day's close: 7% (Level 1) or 13% (Level 2) halts trading for 15 minutes if hit before 3:25 p.m.; 20% (Level 3) halts trading for the rest of the day.
Used for
Giving investors time to absorb information during extreme panics.
What it means for you
In March 2020, Level 1 halts were triggered on four days: March 9, 12, 16 and 18. A halt is not a sign to act; it is a pause for everyone.

If a firm fails

SIPC and FDIC protect different things

Two protections created by federal law are often confused. FDIC insurance protects bank deposits if a bank fails. SIPC protection restores missing securities and cash if a brokerage fails. Neither protects you from investments losing value.

Case studyLehman Brothers Inc., 2008–2022
What happened
When Lehman Brothers collapsed in September 2008, its US brokerage, Lehman Brothers Inc., entered a liquidation under the Securities Investor Protection Act on September 19, 2008 — the largest brokerage liquidation in US history. A pre-negotiated sale of its US brokerage business to Barclays let a large share of customers move to a solvent broker, and most customer claims were satisfied within weeks. When the liquidation closed in 2022, customers had received $106 billion, fully satisfying all 111,000 customer claims, without the use of government or SIPC funds.
What it teaches
Customer securities at a US broker-dealer are held separately from the firm's own assets. When a large broker fails, the usual outcome is a transfer of accounts, not a loss. The market value of what you own is still your risk.
Source
SIPC, "Lehman Brothers Inc.'s 14-year liquidation successfully concludes" (news release, September 28, 2022).

Borrowing to invest

Margin multiplies gains and losses

A margin account lets you borrow from your broker using your investments as collateral. Under the Federal Reserve's Regulation T, you can generally borrow up to 50% of the purchase price of eligible stocks. FINRA's rules then require your equity to stay at least 25% of the account's value; many brokers set higher "house" requirements. Fall below it and you face a margin call: deposit cash or have positions sold, often at the worst moment.

margin call when: (value − loan) ÷ value < maintenance  →  value < loan ÷ (1 − maintenance)    e.g. $10,000 ÷ 0.75 = $13,333
Short selling
What it is
Borrowing shares and selling them, hoping to buy them back later at a lower price and return them.
Used for
Betting on a decline, or hedging another position.
What it means for you
A buyer's loss is limited to what they paid; a short seller's loss has no upper limit, because a price can keep rising. January 2021's GameStop episode, when heavily shorted shares rose many times over in a few weeks, showed how fast that can happen.

Real history

When the plumbing made the news

Case study 1January 2021: when brokers restricted buying
What happened
A surge of buying by individual investors, amid bullish enthusiasm on social media, drove GameStop and a handful of other heavily shorted stocks up by many multiples in a few weeks; GameStop rose about 2,700% from its intraday low on January 8 to its intraday high on January 28. At the end of January, several retail brokers temporarily prohibited customers from buying certain of those stocks and options.
Why
The SEC staff report found that "volatility combined with settlement risks led some firms to temporarily restrict trading." Brokers must post deposits with the clearinghouse, and the largest part of that requirement rises with volatility. The SEC listed the "forces that may cause a brokerage to restrict trading" as an area for further study.
What it teaches
Your broker's relationship with the clearing system can, in extreme conditions, affect what you are allowed to do. It is one more reason not to rely on being able to trade a speculative position at the exact moment you want.
Source
U.S. SEC (October 2021), Staff report on equity and options market structure conditions in early 2021; SEC press release 2021-212.
Case study 2March 2020: four circuit breakers in eight trading days
What happened
As COVID-19 spread, the S&P 500 fell 7% below the prior day's close during trading on March 9, 12, 16 and 18, 2020, each triggering a 15-minute market-wide halt. Markets reopened each time through an auction.
What it teaches
The system worked as designed: trading paused, resumed, and markets stayed open. Stocks ended 2020 up 18.0% for the year. Investors who sold during those March days locked in some of the year's lowest prices.
Source
NYSE, "Assessing NYSE model performance during 2020 crash"; Investor.gov, "Stock market circuit breakers"; Damodaran, NYU Stern (2020 return).

Decisions

Put the plumbing to work

Scenario 1 · Maya opens her first brokerage account

Maya wants to invest $200 a month outside her 401(k), in a broad index ETF. One app advertises free trades and a "free stock" for signing up; a large brokerage offers free trades and fractional shares. How should she choose?

The free stockA one-time bonus worth a few dollars matters far less than the ongoing features. Apps that make trading feel like a game can also encourage the frequent trading that research links to lower returns (INV-060).
Compare what mattersRight. For a $200-a-month index investor, automatic recurring purchases and fractional shares do most of the work. Check that the firm is a SIPC member (sipc.org has a member list) and that FINRA BrokerCheck shows no serious problems.
The principle: choose a broker for the next 30 years, not for the sign-up bonus.
Scenario 2 · Jordan and margin

Jordan's brokerage offers margin at 11% interest. Jordan is confident a particular stock will rise and wants to borrow $20,000 to buy more. What are the risks?

Low riskThe broker is protected by your collateral and its right to sell it; you are not. That is why the broker can lend readily.
High riskRight. Borrowing to buy a single stock combines leverage with concentration, the two ingredients most associated with permanent losses. As a single parent with one income, Jordan has little room for a forced sale.
The principle: leverage turns a temporary fall into a permanent one, because it can force you to sell.

Ruth's $780,000 IRA at a brokerage is SIPC-protected against firm failure only up to $500,000, so she should confirm the firm carries excess coverage or consider splitting it; her bank CDs are FDIC-insured up to $250,000 per bank, per ownership category. Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A stock's bid is $49.95 and its ask is $50.05. You buy 200 shares and immediately sell them. Ignoring commissions, what did the round trip cost, in dollars?

You pay 200 × $50.05 = $10,010 and receive 200 × $49.95 = $9,990: a cost of $20, the spread times the shares.
Exercise 2

You buy $20,000 of stock with $10,000 of your own money and a $10,000 margin loan. With a 25% maintenance requirement, below what account value does a margin call begin, in dollars?

Equity must be at least 25% of value: V − 10,000 ≥ 0.25V, so V ≥ 10,000 ÷ 0.75 = $13,333 — a fall of one-third from $20,000.
Exercise 3

In that same margin account, the stock falls 20%. What percentage of your own $10,000 have you lost? (Ignore interest.)

$20,000 × 20% = $4,000 lost. Your equity falls from $10,000 to $6,000: −40%. Leverage doubled the loss.
