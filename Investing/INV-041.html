State Taxes

Your state decides how much of your wages, individual retirement account (IRA) withdrawals, Social Security and investment income it taxes, and at what rate. The five states our households live in take five different approaches — which is why "retirement-friendly" depends entirely on what kind of income you will have.

The question this module answersHow does my state tax my investments and retirement income, and would moving change that?

Start here

The tax you pay where you live

Federal income tax is the same wherever you live. State income tax is not. Each state writes its own rules: what counts as income, which deductions apply, what rate is charged, and whether retirement income is taxed at all.

For most working households, the state tax is a modest, predictable share of income and there is little to plan. It matters more at three moments: when you decide where to live in retirement, when you plan large one-time income such as Roth conversions or big capital gains, and when you move between states in the middle of a year. The building blocks

Starting point, deductions, rate

Almost every state income tax can be described by three choices.

Starting point
What it is
Most states begin from a line on your federal return: federal adjusted gross income (North Carolina, Arizona, Ohio) or federal taxable income (Colorado). Pennsylvania is unusual: it builds its own income classes (compensation, interest, dividends, net gains and others) from scratch.
Used for
Borrowing the federal definition of income, then adding and subtracting items the state treats differently.
What it means for you
Anything that changes your federal AGI, such as a Roth conversion or a harvested gain, usually changes your state tax too.
Subtractions and exclusions
What it is
Income the state chooses not to tax, such as Social Security benefits, some pensions, or interest on US Treasury securities (exempt from all state and local income taxes, IRS Publication 550).
Used for
Favoring retirees, government workers, or particular kinds of income.
What it means for you
This is where states differ most for retirees. A state with a higher rate can still tax a retiree less.
Rate structure
What it is
A flat tax applies one rate to all taxable income; a graduated tax has brackets like the federal system; a few states have no broad income tax.
Used for
Raising revenue with more or less progressivity.
What it means for you
Under a flat tax, the timing of income (a conversion this year or next) matters less for state tax than under a graduated one.
Going furtherState and local taxes on your federal return

If you itemize, state and local income (or sales) and property taxes are deductible on your federal return up to a cap. Public Law 119-21 set the cap at $40,000 for 2025 and $40,400 for 2026, rising 1% a year through 2029 and returning to $10,000 from 2030; it is reduced by 30% of modified AGI above $505,000 for 2026, but not below $10,000. Most households take the standard deduction and get no federal benefit from state taxes paid.

Where retirees feel the difference

Social Security, IRAs and pensions by state

All five states exempt Social Security for their older residents, at least in part. The differences are in IRA and 401(k) withdrawals, the income most retirees rely on besides Social Security. Pennsylvania does not tax them after retirement age; Colorado exempts up to $24,000 per person at 65 (less any Social Security it subtracts); Ohio gives a small credit; North Carolina and Arizona tax them at their flat rates after their standard deductions.

Try the presets. For Ruth, the range is $74 (Pennsylvania) to $784 (North Carolina). For the Harpers in retirement, $399 (Pennsylvania) to $1,895 (North Carolina). For Denise's $62,000 salary, where retirement exclusions do not apply, the order changes: Arizona is lowest at $1,159 and Colorado highest at $2,024. The Maya preset uses her $80,960 of wages after 401(k) deferrals and one dependent, Eli, whose exemption only the Ohio calculation uses: Arizona is again lowest at $1,433 and Colorado highest at $2,500, with Ohio, where she lives, at $1,738 before Columbus's city tax.

Going furtherRoth conversions and state tax

A conversion is a distribution from a traditional IRA, so in a state that taxes IRA income it adds state tax at that state's rate. The Harpers converting $133,000 at 62 would owe about $4,289 to North Carolina (3.99% after the NC standard deduction) under the calculator's simplified rules. A household that plans to move in retirement should compare converting before and after the move (INV-038), and a state's treatment of conversions should be confirmed with its revenue department.

Which state gets to tax you

Domicile, statutory residence and part-year rules

Domicile
What it is
Your true, permanent home. Pennsylvania's definition is typical: "the place where an individual, by present and voluntary intention, establishes his true, fixed and permanent home ... the place where an individual intends to return whenever absent." You can have several residences but only one domicile.
Used for
Deciding which state can tax all of your income.
What it means for you
Moving your domicile takes more than a new address: Pennsylvania lists intent to leave, intent to make the new place your base, and actual physical presence and abode there.
Statutory resident
What it is
Someone domiciled elsewhere whom a state taxes as a resident anyway. In Pennsylvania: a person who "has a permanent place of abode in Pennsylvania; and spends more than 183 days (midnight to midnight) of the taxable year in Pennsylvania."
Used for
Taxing people who keep a home and spend most of the year in the state while claiming another domicile.
What it means for you
Snowbirds with homes in two states must count days and keep records. It is possible to be a resident of two states in the same year.

Moving mid-year

In the year you move, you usually file part-year returns in both states, each taxing what you earned while a resident plus its own source income. Plan large, voluntary income — Roth conversions, big capital gains, bonuses you can time — for the part of the year, or the year, when you live in the lower-tax state. Keep evidence of the move: driver's license, voter registration, vehicle registration, bank accounts and where you spend your days, all factors Pennsylvania lists.

Going furtherLocal income taxes

Some states let cities, counties or school districts tax income too. Ohio municipalities levy income taxes on wages, but Ohio law bars them from taxing intangible income (such as interest, dividends and capital gains; a few cities that taxed it in 1988 and won voter approval are grandfathered) and "social security benefits, railroad retirement benefits, unemployment compensation, pensions, retirement benefit payments, payments from annuities" (ORC §718.01). For Maya Walker in Columbus, that means city tax on her wages but not on the interest, dividends or capital gains from her savings and taxable account.

Using the rules

Where state tax changes the decision

  1. Where to retire. Compare total tax on your expected income mix, not headline rates. A retiree living mainly on IRA withdrawals pays nothing to Pennsylvania on them and 3.99% after the standard deduction to North Carolina.
  2. Roth conversions. In a state that taxes IRA income, conversions cost state tax now; if you will move to a state that exempts it, converting after the move saves that tax. If you will move to a higher-tax state, converting before may help.
  3. Capital gains. Every state here taxes capital gains as ordinary income. Large sales can be timed for a move, and Pennsylvania's lack of loss carryovers makes harvesting less valuable there (INV-037).
  4. Bond choices. Treasury interest is exempt from all state income taxes, which helps in taxable accounts in higher-rate states. States differ in whether they tax municipal bond interest, especially interest on other states' bonds — check your state's rules before buying.
  5. Property and sales taxes. These are not covered here but can outweigh income-tax differences; a complete comparison includes them.

Law and history

Three rules with real consequences

Case study 1North Carolina's Bailey decision
What happened
After the North Carolina Supreme Court's decision in Bailey v. State of North Carolina, the state may not tax certain retirement benefits of state, local and federal government retirees. Before the trial court ruled on remaining issues, the state settled the class action; the court approved the settlement on October 7, 1998, and it required the state to appropriate $799,000,000 for refunds to retirees who had paid tax on those benefits for 1989 through 1997. Today, distributions from a qualifying retirement plan in which the retiree was vested as of August 12, 1989 are exempt from North Carolina income tax.
What it teaches
State tax treatment can depend on your employer and your history, not just your income. Government retirees moving to or living in North Carolina should check whether they qualify.
Source
North Carolina Department of Revenue, "Bailey Decision Concerning Federal, State and Local Retirement Benefits"; NCDOR Directive PD-99-1 (March 4, 1999).
Case study 2The federal limit on taxing former residents
What happened
Federal law provides that "No State may impose an income tax on any retirement income of an individual who is not a resident or domiciliary of such State," with retirement income defined to include qualified plans, IRAs and many deferred-compensation arrangements.
What it teaches
A retiree who earned a pension in one state and retires to another owes the pension's income tax only to the new home state. Getting the domicile change right is what makes this protection work.
Source
4 U.S.C. §114, Limitation on State income taxation of certain pension income.
Case study 3Pennsylvania and the lost loss
What happened
Pennsylvania's Personal Income Tax Guide says the state "has no provisions for the carryover of losses from one tax year to another year" and "does not allow an offset of loss against gain from one class of income to another or between two taxpayers (i.e., spouses)."
What it teaches
A Pennsylvanian who harvests a large capital loss in a year without gains gets a federal carryover but no Pennsylvania benefit that year or later. State rules can change the value of a federal strategy.
Source
PA Department of Revenue, PA Personal Income Tax Guide, "Net Gains (Losses) from the Sale, Exchange, or Disposition of Property."

Decisions

Moving for taxes?

Scenario 1 · Ruth

Anna has suggested that Ruth move near her in Arizona (an illustrative detail). A friend says Arizona's 2.5% flat tax is lower than Pennsylvania's 3.07%, so the move would cut Ruth's taxes. Is that right?

Lower rate, lower taxRates compare only taxable income. Ruth's taxable income in Pennsylvania is just her $2,400 of interest.
Look at what is taxedRight. In Arizona her $30,000 IRA withdrawal becomes taxable after the standard deduction and age-65 exemption. The difference is a few hundred dollars a year — small enough that being near Anna can easily be worth more.
The principle: compare state taxes on your actual income mix, then let the tax be a tie-breaker, not the reason.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers

The Harpers plan large Roth conversions after retiring and are thinking about moving from North Carolina to a state without an income tax in about five years. How should the move and the conversions fit together?

Convert now in NCAdds 3.99% state tax to every converted dollar. On $133,000 a year that is roughly $4,300 a year that could be avoided after a move.
Model both ordersRight. The federal window from 62 to 74 is long enough to place most conversions after a move. Any conversions before it should be those that fill the lowest federal brackets.
Never convertLeaves the federal problem — large required minimum distributions (RMDs) at 75 and a big pre-tax balance for their son — unsolved (INV-038).
The principle: time voluntary income for the lowest combined federal and state rate.

Exercise 1

A North Carolina resident has $50,000 of North Carolina taxable income in 2026. How much state income tax is due, in dollars?

0.0399 × $50,000 = $1,995.
Exercise 2

An Ohio resident has $40,000 of nonbusiness income after exemptions in 2026. Using the statute's schedule, what is the tax before credits, in dollars?

$332 + 0.0275 × $13,950 = $332 + $383.63 = $715.63.
Exercise 3

A Colorado resident aged 70 subtracts $10,000 of taxable Social Security and has $30,000 of IRA distributions. How much of the IRA income can they subtract, in dollars?

The pension subtraction is $24,000 minus the Social Security subtracted, or the pension income if smaller: $24,000 − $10,000 = $14,000.
Exercise 4

A person domiciled in Florida keeps a permanent home in Pennsylvania. What is the fewest number of days in Pennsylvania that would make them a Pennsylvania statutory resident?

The test is a permanent place of abode plus "more than 183 days," so 184 days.
