Withdrawal Strategies

Saving for retirement is a question of how much to put in. Retirement itself is the reverse: how much can you take out each year without running dry? This module tells the real story of the 4% rule, tests it on every retirement since 1928, and compares the flexible alternatives: guardrails, variable percentages and buckets.

The question this module answersHow much can I spend each year from my portfolio, and what rule should decide it?

Start here

Why you cannot simply spend the average return

If a portfolio of 60% stocks and 40% bonds has earned about 5% a year after inflation over the long run, it seems natural to spend 5% a year and leave the principal intact. That reasoning is the single most common retirement-income mistake.

The average is real. From 1928 to 2025, a 60/40 mix of the S&P 500 and 10-year Treasuries, rebalanced every year, compounded at 8.3% a year before inflation and 5.1% after it (computed from Damodaran's data in inv-data.js). But nobody retires into an average. You retire into one particular sequence of years, and if the bad years come first, withdrawals taken from a shrunken portfolio cannot be earned back. Figure 76.1 shows a retiree who started in 1966 and spent a fixed, inflation-adjusted amount.

William Bengen opened his 1994 paper with exactly this trap: a planner who "carefully computed their rates of withdrawal based on average rates of returns" and watched clients' money run out. His answer, the safe withdrawal rate, and the answers that followed it are the subject of this module.

  4. Variable percentage and amortization methods that can never run out.

Where the number came from

Bengen 1994 and the Trinity study

Fixed real withdrawalalso called the constant-dollar or inflation-adjusted withdrawal
What it is
You withdraw a percentage of the starting portfolio in the first year; every year after, you take the same dollar amount raised by inflation, whatever the markets do. The percentage is used once, at the start.
Used for
The method behind the "4% rule": a $1 million portfolio pays $40,000 in year one, then $40,000 plus inflation, forever unchanged in buying power.
What it means for you
It gives the steadiest possible income, which is why it was studied first, but it is also the most fragile rule, because it never adjusts spending to a falling portfolio.

Bengen, 1994

William Bengen, a financial planner in El Cajon, California, used Ibbotson Associates' data on large-company stocks, intermediate-term Treasury notes and inflation from 1926 onward. For each starting year he measured "portfolio longevity": how long a portfolio lasted with a fixed real withdrawal. His conclusions, in his words and numbers (the 4% and 3% findings are for his illustrative 50% stock, 50% intermediate-Treasury portfolio):

  • A first-year withdrawal of 4%, adjusted for inflation afterward, "should be safe": "In no past case has it caused a portfolio to be exhausted before 33 years."
  • A 4.25% rate "could exhaust a portfolio in as little as 28 years."
  • About 3% (up to roughly 3.5%) was "absolutely safe": never less than 50 years.
  • An initial 5% rate was "risky; six percent or more is 'gambling.'"
  • The best starting allocation was between 50% and 75% stocks; below 50% was "counterproductive."

The Trinity study, 1998

Philip Cooley, Carl Hubbard and Daniel Walz, finance professors at Trinity University, whose paper became known as the Trinity study, published a broader test in the AAII Journal of the American Association of Individual Investors (AAII) in February 1998. They used S&P 500 returns and long-term, high-grade corporate bonds from 1926 to 1995, withdrawal rates from 3% to 12% (raised each year by the Consumer Price Index, CPI, in the version shown here), and payout periods of 15 to 30 years, and they reported "success rates": the share of past periods in which the portfolio lasted.

The Trinity authors drew a conclusion that is often forgotten: "Retirees who demand CPI-adjusted withdrawals during their retirement years must accept a substantially reduced withdrawal rate from the initial portfolio." They also called 3% and 4% rates from stock-dominated portfolios "exceedingly conservative behavior" that would likely leave large estates to heirs, which means many rigid 4% retirees under-spent.

Going furtherWhat the rule is not

The 4% rule is not "withdraw 4% of whatever the balance is each year"; that is a constant-percentage rule, which never runs out but makes income swing with markets. It is not a promise: both studies describe what would have happened in US history, one of the most successful stock markets in the world. And it was never meant to include fees or taxes: both studies used index returns with no costs.

Every retirement since 1928

Test the fixed real withdrawal on 98 years of data

The tool below runs the same experiment on this course's data set: every retirement starting in 1928 through the last year that still has a full period of data. For a 30-year retirement, that is 69 starting years, 1928 to 1996. Change the rate, the mix and the length and watch which years fail.

What the default shows

At 4% with 60% stocks, using a fixed real withdrawal, the money lasted 30 years in 65 of 69 retirements (94%). The four failures all began in the late 1960s (1965, 1966, 1968 and 1969), when high inflation and poor stock returns in the 1970s arrived early; the worst, 1966, ran out in its 26th year. The highest rate that survived every one of the 69 starts was 3.73%, set by 1966. At the other extreme, a 1982 retiree could have sustained over 10%. The median retiree ended 30 years with about $1.39 million in start-year dollars, more than they began with; 43 of the 69 ended with at least their starting real balance.

💡

Why this differs from Bengen's "never before 33 years." Bengen used intermediate-term Treasuries and data from 1926 through the early 1990s; this data set uses 10-year Treasuries, which lost more in the 1970s inflation, and runs through 2025. Small changes in data and timing move the "safe" rate by a few tenths of a percent, which is itself a lesson: 4% is a neighborhood, not a precise line.

Practitioner depthHow the backtest works
Bt+1 = (Bt − W) × (1 + rt), in real dollars, where rt = (1 + s·Rstock + (1 − s)·Rbond) ÷ (1 + CPI) − 1

Working in real (start-year) dollars with a constant W is identical to raising a nominal withdrawal by inflation each year. A retirement "fails" in the first year the balance is smaller than the withdrawal. The maximum sustainable rate for each start year is found by bisection. With only 98 years of data, the 69 thirty-year windows overlap heavily (two adjacent windows share 29 years), so they are far from 69 independent experiments.

Flexible spending, part 1

The Guyton–Klinger decision rules

The fixed real withdrawal is rigid by design. Almost nobody actually spends that way: people trim a vacation after a crash and splurge after a boom. Jonathan Guyton and William Klinger asked what starting rate becomes safe if spending follows a few explicit rules.

Guardrailsthe Guyton–Klinger decision rules
What it is
A starting withdrawal plus rules that cut spending when the current withdrawal rate drifts well above the starting rate, and raise it when the rate drifts well below.
Used for
Starting with a higher income than the fixed 4% rule allows, in exchange for accepting occasional cuts.
What it means for you
You trade certainty of income for a higher starting income and a much lower chance of running out. It works only if you will actually make the cuts.

In their March 2006 Journal of Financial Planning paper, Guyton and Klinger used Monte Carlo simulation (INV-077) based on 1973–2004 and 1928–2004 data, tested 50%, 65% and 80% equity portfolios, and required the money to last at least 40 years. Their rules:

  • Withdrawal rule: raise withdrawals by inflation each year, except that there is no increase after a year in which the portfolio's total return was negative, and no later make-up.
  • Capital preservation rule: if the current withdrawal rate has risen more than 20% above the initial rate (for example, above 6% for a 5% start), cut this year's withdrawal by 10%. The rule stops applying in the final 15 years of the planning horizon.
  • Prosperity rule: if the current rate has fallen more than 20% below the initial rate, raise this year's withdrawal by 10%.
  • Portfolio management rule: fund withdrawals from asset classes that have grown above their target; do not sell stocks after a negative year if cash and bonds can cover the withdrawal.

They concluded that initial withdrawal rates of 5.2–5.6% were sustainable at a 99% confidence standard for portfolios with at least 65% in equities, falling to as low as 4.6% with 50% equities, and that consistently applying the two new rules "effectively eliminates the risk of exhausting retirement assets."

Now try four rules on one real retirement, including a simplified version of guardrails. The default is the worst kind of start, 1966, at a 5% initial rate: exactly the case where the fixed rule runs out.

In 1966 at 5% and 60/40, the fixed real withdrawal paid $50,000 a year in 1966 dollars for 18 years and then ran out. The guardrails never ran out and ended with about $659,000, but they paid for that safety: five 10% cuts, plus inflation freezes in the high-inflation 1970s, reduced real spending to about $20,600 by the 15th year, 59% below where it started. Across all 69 starts at 5%, the guardrails never ran out, but in the worst start (1968) real spending fell to 38% of its first-year level at its low point.

⚠️

This tool is a simplified version. It applies the withdrawal, capital preservation and prosperity rules to a rebalanced two-asset portfolio; it does not model the portfolio management rule (the 2006 paper had already dropped the 6% cap on yearly inflation raises used in Guyton's earlier work, and so does the tool), and it runs on actual history rather than simulation. Deep cuts in an inflationary decade are a real feature of rules that freeze inflation raises, not a bug in the tool.

Flexible spending, part 2

Rules that can never run out

A rule that withdraws a percentage of the current balance can never empty the portfolio: a percentage of something is always something. The price is that income moves with the market. Two versions are common.

Constant-percentage withdrawal
What it is
Withdraw the same percentage, such as 4% or 5%, of the balance at the start of each year.
Used for
A simple rule with no chance of depletion; some endowments use smoothed versions of it.
What it means for you
A 30% market fall means a 30% pay cut the next year. If your fixed bills are covered by Social Security and pensions, that may be tolerable; if not, it is not.
Variable percentage withdrawalVPW, an amortization-based method
What it is
Each year, withdraw the payment that would use up the portfolio over your remaining planning horizon at an assumed return, like a mortgage payment in reverse. Because the remaining horizon shrinks, the percentage rises with age.
Used for
Spending more of the portfolio during your lifetime, deliberately, while still adapting to markets. The method, VPW, was popularized by the Bogleheads investor community.
What it means for you
Income is higher on average and never depletes before the end of the horizon, but it varies year to year and shrinks after bad markets. Pick a horizon beyond your likely lifespan (for example, 100) so the late-life percentages do not strip the account.
Withdrawal rate at age a = r ÷ [ (1 − (1 + r)−n) × (1 + r) ], with n = years left in the horizon and r = assumed real return
Going furtherSmoothing the ride

Pure percentage rules pass every market swing straight through to your income. Common fixes include basing the withdrawal on an average of the last few year-end balances, capping the change in spending to a set percentage each year, or covering essential spending with guaranteed income so that only discretionary spending varies. Each fix reduces volatility of income at the cost of some risk of lower spending later.

Practitioner depthWhy the amortization rule spends more

The fixed real rule was calibrated to survive the worst historical start, so in a typical history it leaves a large balance: the median 60/40 retiree in this course's backtest ended 30 years with 1.39 times the starting real balance. An amortization rule targets zero at the horizon, so it spends that surplus during life. In the 1982 start, for example, amortization spending over 30 years totaled about $4.07 million per $1 million invested (in 1982 dollars), versus $1.5 million for a 5% fixed real withdrawal. Whether that is desirable depends on bequest goals.

A way to organize the money

“Bucket strategy” is a family of different plans

Bucket strategy
What it is
An umbrella term for plans that separate near-term spending from long-term growth. Some use two buckets, some three or more, and some label money by purpose rather than time. The refill rule matters as much as the labels.
Used for
Making withdrawals understandable, reducing forced sales of volatile assets, and helping a retiree stay invested through a crash.
What it means for you
Buckets are a way of implementing an asset allocation, not a separate source of return. A plan is incomplete until it states how much belongs in each bucket, which account holds it, what triggers a refill, and what happens after a prolonged decline.
VersionHow it worksStrengthTradeoff or failure mode

Lucia “Buckets of Money”Time-based buckets intended to mature or fund successive retirement periods, historically described with safe, income, and growth segments.Strong behavioral story and spending visibility.Product choices and timing rules can be complex; labeling does not remove investment risk.
Purpose-based reserveHold conservative assets for a defined income gap or liability, sometimes roughly five years of planned portfolio withdrawals, while investing the rest for longer goals.Sizes safety assets from the actual gap after Social Security, pensions and other income.“Five years” is still a planning judgment, not a guarantee; multiple purposes complicate the map.
Income floor + growth: 2 bucketsCover essentials with Social Security, pensions, cash, bonds or an annuity; invest the remainder for flexible spending and legacy.Directly protects essential spending instead of an arbitrary number of years.Guarantees can reduce liquidity or leave less upside; product costs and insurer strength matter.
Total return + rebalancingKeep one diversified portfolio, withdraw from cash flows and overweight assets, then rebalance to targets.Fewer moving parts and usually less idle cash.Can feel unsafe in a bear market even when the economics resemble a bucket plan.

The strongest claim for buckets is behavioral, not magical: seeing near-term spending protected can make it easier not to panic. The counterargument, emphasized by Rob Berger, is that disciplined rebalancing already sells the asset that is above target; after a stock crash that is generally bonds, not stocks. Large cash reserves can lower return, and “refill only after stocks rise” can leave cash depleted in a long bear market. Research comparisons therefore depend heavily on the chosen asset mix and refill rules; a label alone cannot be backtested.

💡

Context decides the version. Ruth can size a reserve from her $25,200 annual gap after Social Security, rather than from her full $60,000 spending. Daniel and Priya Shah need more than a standard 30-year retirement bucket: Daniel may retire while Priya is still working, Leena's support horizon can extend beyond both parents, and taxable, tax-deferred, Roth and third-party trust money have different tax and legal jobs. A purpose-based map — near-term retirement, Priya's later retirement, and Leena's lifetime support — is more informative than assigning the entire $3.2 million to years 1–30.

Going furtherBuckets and the account you hold them in

Buckets need not be separate accounts. Many retirees hold the cash bucket in a taxable account or an individual retirement account (IRA) money market fund, bonds in the IRA, and stocks spread across Roth and taxable accounts for tax reasons (INV-036, INV-042). Required minimum distributions from IRAs (INV-081) can be taken from whichever holdings you choose, which makes them a natural refill point for the cash bucket.

The research record

Three findings that shaped the field

Case study 1Bengen's worst years were the inflationary ones
What happened
Bengen found that the 1973–1974 "Big Bang" damaged portfolios that began withdrawals "as much as 20 or more years earlier," and that it was "not a deflationary period like the Depression that is to be truly feared, but rather an inflationary period."
What it teaches
Rising prices force a fixed real withdrawal to grow just as the portfolio shrinks. The same pattern shows up in Figure 76.7: the lowest sustainable rates belong to retirees who started in 1965–1969.
Source
Bengen, W. P. (1994). Determining withdrawal rates using historical data. Journal of Financial Planning (October 1994).
Case study 2Trinity's warning about rigid inflation increases
What happened
The Trinity authors showed that without inflation adjustments, withdrawal rates of 3–4% were "extremely unlikely to exhaust any portfolio," but that retirees who "demand CPI-adjusted withdrawals" must accept a substantially lower starting rate. They added that for retirees with significant fixed costs and those who spend less as they age, CPI adjustments are likely to cause "a suboptimal exchange of present consumption for future consumption."
What it teaches
The famous success rates depend on one assumption: spending never adjusts. Relaxing that assumption, even partly, changes the answer more than tinkering with the asset mix.
Source
Cooley, P. L., Hubbard, C. M., & Walz, D. T. (1998). Retirement savings: Choosing a withdrawal rate that is sustainable. AAII Journal (February 1998).
Case study 3Decision rules raise the starting rate
What happened
Guyton and Klinger found that with their capital preservation and prosperity rules, initial rates of 5.2–5.6% were sustainable over 40 years at a 99% confidence standard for portfolios with at least 65% equities, and that the 1973–2004 and 1928–2004 data periods gave "virtually identical results."
What it teaches
A willingness to cut spending by 10% in bad stretches is worth more than a point of starting income. The cost, as the tool shows for 1966, can be a long run of reduced real spending.
Source
Guyton, J. T., & Klinger, W. J. (2006). Decision rules and maximum initial withdrawal rates. Journal of Financial Planning (March 2006).

Decisions

Choosing a rule

Scenario 1 · The Harpers

The Harpers expect $1.69 million at retirement and a $61,000 gap before Social Security fully starts. Tom likes certainty; Rachel would rather travel more early and cut back later if needed. Which rule fits?

Fixed real at 4%It covers their $61,000 gap with room to spare, and in 94% of historical 30-year periods it would have lasted. But it also would have left the median retiree with more real money than they started with: a lot of unspent travel.
Guardrails with a cut listA strong fit for a couple with a mix of essential and discretionary spending. Their $80,000 floor is mostly Social Security once both claim, so a 10% cut falls on travel, not groceries. Writing the cut list now makes it much more likely they will follow the rule later.
Constant 5%It can never run out, but a 2008-style year would cut their income by about a third the next year. With a large discretionary budget that may be acceptable; it is the least comfortable for Tom.
The principle: the best rule is one you will follow; flexibility is worth the most when a large share of spending is discretionary.
Scenario 2 · Ruth

Ruth needs about $25,200 a year from her $780,000 IRA, a 3.2% rate. After a bad year, a neighbor tells her to "cut back to be safe." Should she?

Cut 20%Unnecessary at her rate. In this course's backtest, a 3.2% fixed real withdrawal from a 50/50 portfolio lasted in all 74 historical 25-year periods, which is about her horizon to the mid-90s.
Keep spending; use the cushionRight. Her $60,000 of CDs and savings is a natural cash bucket. Spending from it after a loss, and refilling it later from the IRA, avoids selling stocks low. Her required minimum distributions (RMDs) from 73 (INV-081) will likely exceed her spending, so the cushion refills itself.
The principle: a low withdrawal rate is itself the safety margin; do not add panic on top of it.
Scenario 3 · Maya's budget meets the research

Maya, 58, reads that "4% is dead" and "5% is fine" in the same week. Her budget (INV-075) needs about $38,300 a year from savings at 67, and she projects about $841,000 with catch-up contributions: a 4.6% first-year rate. Almost all of it is essential spending for herself and Eli. What should she take from the research?

Take the 5%The higher rates in the research come with spending cuts after bad years. In this course's backtest a rigid 4.6% from a 60/40 portfolio lasted 30 years in 58 of 69 historical periods (84%), and Maya has little discretionary spending to cut. Without the catch-up her rate would be 5.2% (45 of 69, 65%).
About 4%, and close the gap nowRight. A rigid 4% lasted in 65 of 69 periods (94%). At 4%, $841,000 supports about $33,600 a year, so she has about $4,700 a year to find: more catch-up saving (from 60 to 63 the limit is $11,250), a later Social Security claim if her health allows (INV-078), and the end of her mortgage payment at 70.
The principle: ask "safe under which rule?" before asking "how much is safe?", and remember that essential spending cannot follow a flexible rule.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

You retire with $1,200,000 and use a 4% fixed real withdrawal. Inflation in your first year is 3%. How much do you withdraw in year two, in dollars?

Year one: 4% × $1,200,000 = $48,000. Year two: $48,000 × 1.03 = $49,440, whatever the portfolio did.
Exercise 2

Guardrails, 5% initial rate on $1,000,000. After a bad year the balance is $800,000, and this year's inflation-adjusted withdrawal would be $51,500. What do you actually withdraw, in dollars?

Current rate = $51,500 ÷ $800,000 = 6.44%. The upper guardrail is 1.2 × 5% = 6%, so the capital preservation rule applies: $51,500 × 0.9 = $46,350. (If the previous year's return was negative, the withdrawal rule would also have frozen the inflation raise first.)
Exercise 3

An amortization (VPW-style) rule at age 70, with a horizon to age 100 (31 payments) and a 3% real return, gives a withdrawal rate of 4.854%. What is the withdrawal from a $600,000 balance, in dollars?

0.03 ÷ [(1 − 1.03−31) × 1.03] = 0.04854. $600,000 × 0.04854 = $29,126. Next year the rate will be a little higher and the balance will have moved with the market.
Exercise 4

If a portfolio earned exactly 0% after inflation every year, how many years would a 4% fixed real withdrawal last?

With no real growth, 25 withdrawals of 4% use up 100%: 25 years. Every year the rule lasts beyond 25 is paid for by real returns, which is why a 30-year retirement needs a portfolio that earns something after inflation.
