Federal Estate and Gift Tax

The federal government taxes large transfers of wealth, whether made during life as gifts or at death, under one unified system. For 2026 the first $15 million per person is excluded, so only a few thousand estates a year owe anything. This module explains exactly how the tax is computed, why gifts and estates share one exemption, how a surviving spouse can inherit the unused exemption, and what forms are needed even when no tax is due.

The question this module answersWill my estate or my gifts owe federal transfer tax, and what do I have to file either way?

Start here

A tax almost no one pays, and why it still matters

Most people have heard of the "death tax" and many worry about it. For the ordinary family, the worry is misplaced: the federal estate tax applies in 2026 only to the part of an estate above $15 million, and a married couple can shelter $30 million.

The IRS's own statistics show how rare the tax is. In 2024, executors filed 7,195 federal estate tax returns, and 2,663 of them owed tax, a total of $23.3 billion (IRS Statistics of Income). Most of those returns were for people who died in 2023, a year in which 3,090,964 US residents died (CDC). Roughly one death in 1,160 produced a taxable estate.

So why study it? Because the rules still reach ordinary families in three ways: gifts above $19,000 a person a year require a gift tax return even though no tax is due; a surviving spouse may need an estate tax return to preserve the first spouse's unused exemption; and the exemption has changed often enough — it was scheduled to be cut roughly in half in 2026 before Public Law 119-21 raised it instead — that plans should be built to survive the next change.

The computation

From gross estate to tax due

Gross estate
What it is
The value at death of everything the person owned or controlled for tax purposes: "cash and securities, real estate, insurance, trusts, annuities, business interests and other assets" (IRS), including retirement accounts, life insurance they owned, revocable trusts and their share of joint property.
Used for
The starting point of the estate tax calculation and the Form 706 filing test.
What it means for you
It is larger than the probate estate. Ruth's gross estate includes her IRA, even though the IRA avoids probate.

The IRS lists the deductions: "mortgages and other debts, estate administration expenses, property that passes to surviving spouses and qualified charities." What is left is the taxable estate. The law then adds back adjusted taxable gifts — lifetime gifts above the annual exclusions, made after 1976 — computes a tentative tax on the total using the rate schedule in 26 U.S.C. § 2001(c), and subtracts the unified credit.

Going furtherWhy add lifetime gifts back?

Adding adjusted taxable gifts back into the base puts lifetime gifts and death transfers on one schedule, so a person cannot escape the higher brackets by giving half away before death. Gifts that were within the annual exclusions, paid for tuition or medical care directly, or made to a spouse or charity are not added back. The value added back is the gift's value when it was made, so later growth on gifted assets escapes estate tax — the main tax reason large estates give early.

Public Law 119-21

$15,000,000 per person, and the unified credit

Basic exclusion amountoften called the lifetime exemption
What it is
The amount each person can transfer, by lifetime taxable gifts and at death combined, before federal gift or estate tax is due. For 2026 it is $15,000,000 (IRS, Rev. Proc. 2025-32), set by section 70106 of Public Law 119-21, which amended 26 U.S.C. § 2010(c)(3). It is adjusted for inflation for 2027 and later years.
Used for
Determining the unified credit and whether a Form 706 is required.
What it means for you
Unless your estate plus lifetime taxable gifts exceeds $15,000,000 (or $30,000,000 for a married couple using portability), you will owe no federal transfer tax in 2026.
Unified creditthe "applicable credit amount"
What it is
A dollar-for-dollar credit equal to the tentative tax on the applicable exclusion amount. For 2026 the credit on $15,000,000 is $5,945,800 (IRS, Instructions for Form 706).
Used for
Offsetting gift tax during life and estate tax at death; it is "unified" because both draw on the same credit.
What it means for you
Every taxable gift uses part of the credit and leaves less for your estate. The two are one budget.

With the calculator's defaults — a $20,000,000 gross estate, $300,000 of debts and expenses, no spouse or charity — the tax is $1,880,000: 40% of the $4,700,000 by which the $19,700,000 taxable estate exceeds the exclusion. Leave $5,000,000 to charity and the tax disappears.

Going furtherThe "clawback" that did not happen

The 2017 tax law doubled the exclusion for 2018 through 2025, and the IRS explained that in 2026 it was "due to revert to its pre-2018 level of $5 million, as adjusted for inflation." People worried that large gifts made under the higher exclusion would be "clawed back" into their estates. Final regulations released on November 26, 2019 said no: an estate can compute its credit using the greater of the exclusion used for lifetime gifts or the exclusion at death. The IRS's example: a person who made a $9 million taxable gift in 2018 and died in 2026 could still base the estate tax on the $9 million used. Public Law 119-21 then made the question moot for 2026 by raising the exclusion to $15,000,000.

Practitioner depthCredits, deductions and the 40% bracket

Because the credit equals the tentative tax on the exclusion, and the exclusion is well above the $1,000,000 where the 40% bracket begins, every dollar of taxable estate above the applicable exclusion is taxed at exactly 40%. Deductions (marital, charitable, debts, the state death tax deduction) and credits for foreign death taxes reduce this. The 2026 Rev. Proc. also sets related figures, such as a $1,460,000 limit on the reduction in value from special-use valuation of farm or business real property under § 2032A, and $1,940,000 as the amount used for the "2-percent portion" of estate tax deferred on a closely held business under § 6166.

Giving during life

The annual exclusion, gift splitting and Form 709

Annual gift exclusion
What it is
The amount you can give each person each year without using any of your lifetime exclusion or filing a gift tax return: $19,000 per recipient for 2026 (IRS). See annual gift exclusion.
Used for
Moving money to children and grandchildren tax-free every year; funding 529 plans; paying life insurance premiums held in trusts.
What it means for you
A gift above $19,000 to one person is not "taxed"; it is reported on Form 709 and reduces your $15,000,000 lifetime exclusion. Tax is due only after the lifetime exclusion is used up.

The IRS lists the gifts that are not taxable at all: gifts within the annual exclusion; tuition or medical expenses you pay directly for someone; gifts to your spouse; and gifts to a political organization for its use. Gifts to qualifying charities are deductible. For a spouse who is not a US citizen, the annual exclusion is $194,000 for 2026 instead of unlimited (Rev. Proc. 2025-32). The gift tax is generally paid by the donor, not the recipient.

Gift splitting
What it is
An election by a married couple to treat a gift made by either spouse as made half by each, so each spouse's annual exclusion applies.
Used for
Letting a couple give $38,000 per recipient for 2026 ($19,000 each), even if the money comes from one spouse's account.
What it means for you
Electing it requires filing Form 709, even when no gift exceeds the doubled exclusion.

Form 709

Form 709 is due "no earlier than January 1, but not later than April 15, of the year after the gift was made"; extending your income tax return extends it automatically, or Form 8892 gives six months (IRS, Instructions for Form 709). You must file if you gave any one person more than the annual exclusion (other than your spouse), gave any gift of a future interest (such as most gifts to trusts) regardless of amount, or elect gift splitting. A special election lets you treat a contribution to a 529 plan of up to five years' exclusions as made evenly over five years.

Going furtherGifts carry the donor's basis

A gift of appreciated stock carries the donor's cost basis to the recipient, while the same stock inherited at death usually gets a step-up to its value at death (INV-090). For families far below the estate tax exclusion, giving appreciated assets can therefore cost more income tax than holding them. Gifts of cash, or of assets with little gain, avoid the problem. INV-092 covers family gifting strategy.

Married couples

Inheriting a spouse's unused exclusion

Portabilityof the deceased spousal unused exclusion (DSUE)
What it is
An election that lets a surviving spouse add the deceased spouse's unused basic exclusion to their own. It applies to spouses who died after 2010 (IRS, Instructions for Form 706). See portability.
Used for
Letting a couple shelter up to two exclusions even if the first spouse left everything to the survivor.
What it means for you
It requires filing a complete, timely Form 706 at the first death, even when no tax is due. Skipping that return can forfeit up to $15,000,000 of exclusion.
Form 706
What it is
The United States Estate (and Generation-Skipping Transfer) Tax Return, filed by the executor.
Used for
Reporting the gross estate, deductions and tax; electing portability; allocating GST exemption.
What it means for you
For deaths in 2026 it is required if the gross estate plus adjusted taxable gifts exceeds $15,000,000, or if the executor elects portability. It is due nine months after death; Form 4768 gives an automatic six-month extension.

Missed the deadline?

Executors of estates that were not otherwise required to file can still elect portability by filing Form 706 "on or before the fifth anniversary of the decedent's death," stating at the top that it is "Filed Pursuant to Rev. Proc. 2022-32 to Elect Portability under section 2010(c)(5)(A)" (IRS, Instructions for Form 706). For Ruth, whose husband Walter died in 2023, that window runs until the fifth anniversary of his death in 2028.

Rules that trip people up

  • Last deceased spouse. A survivor can use only the DSUE of the "most recently deceased person who was married to the surviving spouse at the time of that person's death." Remarriage followed by the new spouse's death can replace a large DSUE with a small one.
  • Fixed amount. The DSUE is figured on the first spouse's return as the lesser of the basic exclusion then in effect or the unused amount; it does not grow afterward.
  • Examination. When the survivor uses the DSUE, the IRS "may examine any return of a predeceased spouse" to verify it, even after the first spouse's own assessment period has closed.
  • Non-citizens. A nonresident surviving spouse who is not a US citizen generally cannot use a DSUE amount, except as a treaty allows.
Going furtherPortability or a bypass trust?

Portability is simple and keeps a step-up in basis on everything at the second death. A bypass trust (INV-087) shelters later growth, protects assets for children and can use state estate-tax exemptions that are not portable. For most couples far below $30,000,000, portability (or nothing at all) is enough; the bypass model in INV-087 shows where the balance tips.

Gifts to grandchildren

The generation-skipping transfer tax, briefly

Without a special rule, a wealthy family could pay estate tax once and then leave money directly to grandchildren, skipping a whole generation of tax. The generation-skipping transfer (GST) tax closes that gap. It is a separate tax, applied on top of gift or estate tax, to transfers to "skip persons."

Generation-skipping transfer taxGST tax
What it is
A federal tax on transfers to people two or more generations below the transferor, such as grandchildren, or to trusts held only for them. The tax is computed at the maximum federal estate tax rate — 40% — on transfers above the GST exemption.
Used for
Preventing wealth from skipping a generation's estate tax.
What it means for you
For 2026 the GST exemption equals $15,000,000 per person (Rev. Proc. 2025-32). Annual exclusion gifts and tuition paid directly are generally outside it. Only very large gifts to grandchildren or long-term "dynasty" trusts are affected.

Allocation of the GST exemption happens on Form 709 or Form 706. Many allocations are automatic, but trusts that could benefit both children and grandchildren need careful elections. Unlike the basic exclusion, the GST exemption is not portable between spouses, which is one reason large estates still use trusts.

Practitioner depthDirect skips, taxable terminations and taxable distributions

The GST tax recognizes three events. A direct skip is a transfer straight to a skip person, including to a trust held only for skip persons. A taxable termination occurs when a trust interest held by a non-skip person ends and only skip persons remain. A taxable distribution is a distribution from a trust to a skip person that is not a direct skip or termination. A trust with an "inclusion ratio" of zero, meaning fully covered by allocated exemption, can make all three free of GST tax.

Real cases and data

Who pays, and what goes wrong

Case study 1Estate of Sower v. Commissioner (2017)
What happened
Frank Sower died in 2012 and his estate elected portability, passing an unused exclusion to his wife, Minnie, who died in 2013. When the IRS examined Minnie's estate, it also examined Frank's return and reduced his DSUE amount by lifetime taxable gifts he had made. The Tax Court held on September 11, 2017 that the IRS may examine a predeceased spouse's return to determine the DSUE, and that a letter accepting Frank's return as filed was not a closing agreement.
What it teaches
A portability return is not "just a formality." It must be accurate and complete, including lifetime gifts, because it can be examined years later when the survivor dies.
Source
Estate of Sower v. Commissioner, 149 T.C. 279 (2017); IRS, Instructions for Form 706.
Case study 2The 2026 "sunset" that became an increase
What happened
Under the 2017 tax law, the doubled exclusion was scheduled to end after 2025, and the IRS explained that the exclusion was "due to revert to its pre-2018 level of $5 million, as adjusted for inflation" in 2026. Many families made large gifts in 2024 and 2025 to use the higher amount before it expired. Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025, instead set the 2026 exclusion at $15,000,000, up from $13,990,000 in 2025.
What it teaches
Estate-tax law is political and changes. Irrevocable gifts made to beat a deadline cannot be undone if the deadline disappears. Plans for families near the line should be flexible: disclaimer options, portability elections and trusts with adjustable terms.
Source
IRS, Estate and gift tax FAQs; IRS, What's new — estate and gift tax; Rev. Proc. 2025-32.
Case study 3Anti-clawback: the IRS's own example
The example
In 2018, when the exclusion was $11.18 million, a person makes a $9 million taxable gift and uses $9 million of exclusion to reduce the gift tax to zero. They die in 2026, when (under the law as it then stood) the exclusion was expected to be lower. Under the final regulations of November 26, 2019, the estate "can still base its estate tax calculation on the higher $9 million" used in 2018.
What it teaches
Exclusion used during life is protected if the exclusion later falls. That makes lifetime gifts a way to lock in a high exclusion — for people who can afford to give that much.
Source
IRS, Estate and gift tax FAQs.

Decisions

Gifts, forms and a portability question

Scenario 1 · The Harpers

Ethan's tuition is $42,000 this year. Tom wants to write Ethan a check for $42,000 so Ethan can pay the college. Is there a better way?

Check to EthanA $42,000 gift to one person exceeds the $19,000 exclusion. It is not taxed, but Tom must file Form 709 and uses $23,000 of lifetime exclusion. (The exclusion covers tuition paid to the school, not a check to the student; 26 U.S.C. § 2503(e).)
Pay the college directlyRight. Tuition paid directly to the school is excluded under 26 U.S.C. § 2503(e), with no return and no annual exclusion used; room, board and books do not qualify. They can still give Ethan $38,000 of other gifts this year with gift splitting.
Two checks$21,000 from each still exceeds $19,000 each, so both would file Form 709. Paying tuition directly is cleaner.
The principle: pay tuition and medical bills directly; give cash within the annual exclusion.
Scenario 2 · Ruth

Ruth wants to give Anna $50,000 toward a house. She fears a "gift tax bill." What happens?

40% taxNo. Tax is due only after lifetime taxable gifts exceed the exclusion. Ruth is more than $13 million away.
A return, no taxRight. $50,000 − $19,000 = $31,000 of taxable gift, reported and absorbed by the exclusion. Pennsylvania has no gift tax, but gifts within one year of death can be pulled into its inheritance tax (INV-089).
Anna's income taxGifts are not income to the recipient (IRS Publication 559). Ruth should also consider her own cash needs before giving.
The principle: for most families, a gift above the exclusion means paperwork, not tax.
Scenario 3 · Ruth again

No Form 706 was filed when Walter died in 2023. Should Ruth's family file a late one now to elect portability?

Always fileA complete Form 706 is real work and cost. For an estate so far below the exclusion, the DSUE would almost certainly never be used.
Probably not neededA reasonable judgment for Ruth. For a younger survivor, one who might receive an inheritance, or one near the exclusion, the five-year late election under Rev. Proc. 2022-32 is valuable insurance.
The principle: portability is insurance; buy it when the risk is real.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

Using the rate schedule, what is the tentative tax on $15,000,000 — the 2026 unified credit — in dollars?

Over $1,000,000: $345,800 + 40% of the excess. $345,800 + 0.40 × $14,000,000 = $345,800 + $5,600,000 = $5,945,800, matching the IRS figure.
Exercise 2

A single person dies in 2026 with a $12,000,000 taxable estate, having made $5,000,000 of adjusted taxable gifts during life (no gift tax paid). What is the estate tax, in dollars?

Base = $12,000,000 + $5,000,000 = $17,000,000. Tentative tax = $345,800 + 0.40 × $16,000,000 = $6,745,800. Minus the $5,945,800 credit = $800,000, or 40% of the $2,000,000 above the exclusion.
Exercise 3

Ruth gives Anna $50,000 in 2026. How much is a taxable gift reported on Form 709, in dollars?

$50,000 − the $19,000 annual exclusion = $31,000. No tax is due; her remaining exclusion falls to $14,969,000.
Exercise 4

A widow dies in 2026 with a $24,000,000 taxable estate. Her husband's executor did not elect portability. What is the federal estate tax, in dollars?

Only her own $15,000,000 exclusion: 0.40 × $9,000,000 = $3,600,000. With a $15,000,000 DSUE the tax would be zero.
