Target-Date Funds and Glide Paths

If you were automatically enrolled in a 401(k), your money may well have gone into a fund with a year in its name. That one fund holds thousands of stocks and bonds and slowly shifts from growth to safety as the year approaches. This module opens the box: how the shift works, why funds with the same year hold very different mixes, what they cost, and when a single fund really is all you need.

The question this module answersIs a target-date fund a good home for my retirement money, and how do I choose between two funds with the same year on the label?

Start here

An autopilot for your retirement mix

You already know the idea of an autopilot: you set the destination and the system adjusts the controls along the way. A target-date fund is an autopilot for your investment mix. You pick the year you expect to retire, and the fund gradually turns down the risk as that year approaches — without you having to rebalance or remember to become more conservative at 55.

For many people that is exactly right. A low-cost target-date fund gives broad diversification, automatic rebalancing and a sensible path toward safety in one purchase, which is why target-date funds are one of the default categories the Labor Department's rules recognize for workers who do not pick an investment themselves. The catch is in the details. "2050" tells you the destination but not the route, and different fund families fly very different routes. Figure 50.1 shows the route one large family publishes in its own prospectus.

Inside the box

A fund of funds on a schedule

Target-date fund
What it is
A single mutual fund (or, in some workplace plans, a collective trust) named for an approximate retirement year, holding a mix of stock and bond funds that becomes more conservative over time.
Used for
One-decision retirement investing; the most common default in workplace plans.
What it means for you
Pick the year closest to when you expect to start withdrawals. Everything else — diversification, rebalancing, de-risking — is done inside the fund.
Fund of funds
What it is
A fund whose holdings are other funds. Vanguard's target-date funds, for example, own a handful of Vanguard index funds rather than individual stocks and bonds.
Used for
Building a diversified mix from a few broad building blocks.
What it means for you
The cost you pay includes the underlying funds' expenses. Prospectuses show them as "acquired fund fees and expenses."
Glide path
What it is
The pre-set schedule by which a target-date fund's stock share falls as the target year approaches, and sometimes after it.
Used for
Reducing the damage a market crash can do just before and after retirement, when a large loss is hardest to recover from (sequence risk, INV-078).
What it means for you
The glide path is the product. Two funds with the same year can differ by 15 or more percentage points in stocks at the moment you retire.

A target-date fund's year assumes a retirement age. T. Rowe Price's prospectus, for example, describes its 2050 fund as designed for someone retiring at about 65 in or near 2050 and warns that it may not suit an investor retiring earlier or later even with the same target date. If you expect to retire at 60, a fund five years earlier than your 65th-birthday year is closer to your real timeline.

Going furtherActive tilts inside a "passive" product

Some families let managers move away from the glide path. T. Rowe Price calls its schedule the "neutral" allocation and allows target allocations to differ from it by up to plus or minus 5 percentage points based on its market outlook, and its underlying funds include actively managed funds. Vanguard's and BlackRock's LifePath Index funds hold index funds. Fidelity offers both an index series (Freedom Index) and actively managed series. Read the "principal investment strategies" section to see which kind you own.

Practitioner depthMutual fund or collective investment trust?

Some workplace plans offer target-date strategies in forms other than mutual funds, such as collective trusts, which can differ in fees and in the documents that describe them. Vanguard's workplace materials, for example, describe a Target Retirement Income and Growth Trust that lets a retiring participant freeze the glide path's equity allocation. Whatever the form, your plan's annual fee disclosure (INV-007) lists the option's cost.

What happens after the target year

"To" versus "through" retirement

The biggest design difference between families is what happens after the date on the label. Some funds reach their most conservative mix at the target year and stay there. Others keep de-risking for years afterward, on the theory that a 65-year-old will spend down the account over two or three decades, not withdraw it all on their birthday.

"To" glide path
What it is
A glide path that reaches its final, most conservative allocation at the target year, then holds it steady.
Used for
Investors who may move money out of the fund at retirement — to buy an annuity, roll over or hand it to an advisor.
What it means for you
Lower stock exposure in the years just before and at retirement; less growth for a long retirement if you stay in.
"Through" glide path
What it is
A glide path that keeps reducing stocks after the target year, reaching its final mix years or decades later.
Used for
Investors who will stay in the fund and draw it down gradually through retirement.
What it means for you
More stocks at retirement, which raises both long-run growth and the size of a loss in a crash right around your retirement date.

BlackRock's prospectus shows a classic "to" design: 40% in equity funds at zero years until retirement, after which a fund's allocation "is expected to be similar to that of LifePath Index Retirement Fund," into which it may be merged. Vanguard says each fund's allocation should become similar to its Target Retirement Income Fund (about 30% stocks) within seven years after the year in its name. Fidelity's funds reach an allocation similar to its Freedom Index Retirement Fund "approximately 15 to 20 years after" the target year. T. Rowe Price's glide path provides a 55% neutral equity allocation at the target date and continues to decline until about 30 years after it; its 2005 fund, 21 years past its date, still held about 40% in stocks in August 2026.

Going furtherWhich design is "right"?

Neither is right for everyone. A "to" path protects the person who retires in a crash and then leaves the fund; a "through" path serves the person who stays for 25 years and needs growth to keep up with inflation. What matters is matching the design to what you will actually do at retirement — and knowing, before a crash, how much you hold in stocks.

Practitioner depthWhy the replay is only a proxy

Real funds in 2008 held international stocks (which fell further than US stocks), and many held TIPS, short-term bonds and cash rather than only 10-year Treasuries. The proxy isolates one thing — the stock share — so you can see how much the glide path choice alone matters. For a fund's actual history, read the calendar-year returns in its prospectus or annual report.

What you pay

Same idea, very different prices

Because a target-date fund is meant to be held for 40 years or more, its fee compounds for 40 years. The four families' own fee tables show a wide range, and the share class your plan offers changes the price of the same fund.

On $400,000 growing at 6% a year before fees for 20 years, the difference between 0.08% and 0.62% is about $130,000: roughly $1.26 million against $1.13 million. Costs are not the only thing that matters — an actively managed series may add or subtract return — but they are the only difference you know in advance (INV-007). T. Rowe Price's prospectus also notes that its management fee declines over time on a contractual schedule, and that some Investor-class accounts pay a $20 annual account fee.

💡

Check two numbers in your plan's fee disclosure. The target-date option's total expense ratio, and whether a cheaper index-based target-date series or collective trust exists in the same plan. Your employer chose the menu; you choose from it.

Why your 401(k) picked it

The QDIA rule

When a plan invests money for a worker who never chose an investment, the employer is acting as a fiduciary. A Department of Labor regulation, 29 CFR 2550.404c-5, gives employers relief from liability for the results of that default investment if it is a "qualified default investment alternative" and the plan follows certain conditions.

Qualified default investment alternative (QDIA)
What it is
A default investment that meets the Labor Department's regulation. The regulation lists a life-cycle or "targeted-retirement-date" fund, a balanced fund, a professionally managed account, and — only for up to 120 days after a worker's first elective contribution — a capital-preservation product.
Used for
Investing automatically enrolled workers' money sensibly without making the employer liable for market losses.
What it means for you
The default is chosen to be reasonable for a typical worker of your age, not for you. The regulation says a target-date QDIA's allocation is not required to take into account your own risk tolerance, other investments or preferences.

Notice what the regulation does not do. It does not require the cheapest fund, a particular glide path, or any guarantee. A plan can choose a 0.08% index series or a far more expensive active series and both can be QDIAs. The regulation's own wording of the target-date category is revealing: such a fund is "designed to provide varying degrees of long-term appreciation and capital preservation through a mix of equity and fixed income exposures based on the participant's age, target retirement date … or life expectancy," becoming more conservative "with increasing age."

Going furtherWhy defaults matter so much

A default is what happens when a worker does nothing, so it shapes the savings of everyone who never gets around to choosing. That is why the choice is made carefully by plan fiduciaries, and why the QDIA rule's notice and transfer requirements exist: anyone who does want to choose can do so easily.

Using it well

One fund, held alone

A target-date fund is designed to be your whole portfolio for retirement. It works best when you let it be.

The most common mistake is mixing a target-date fund with other funds. If Maya holds 50% in a 2065 fund and 50% in an S&P 500 fund, her real stock share is about 96% and her international share drops from about 40% of stocks to about 19% — she has undone the design without meaning to. If you want a different risk level, the simpler fix is to choose a different year: an earlier year for less risk, a later one for more. Vanguard's prospectus says exactly this: investors who want less (or more) risk can select funds with earlier (or later) target dates.

Taxable accounts are the other caution. A target-date fund holds bonds, whose interest is taxed each year as ordinary income, and it rebalances internally, which can distribute capital gains. In an IRA or 401(k) that does not matter. In a taxable account, holding separate stock and bond funds can let you place each where it is taxed least (INV-036, asset location).

Going furtherTarget-date funds as a benchmark

Even if you build your own portfolio, a low-cost target-date fund for your year is a useful yardstick. If your hand-built mix has trailed it for five years, ask whether your extra effort, cost or concentration is paying for itself (INV-057 on measuring performance).

Real documents, real changes

Three lessons from the fund documents

Case study 1Glide paths change: BlackRock and Fidelity, 2026
What happened
BlackRock's April 30, 2026 LifePath Index prospectus supplement announced that, effective on or about June 1, 2026, certain funds would begin to implement a revised glide path with a higher equity allocation starting 30 years from the target date, raising equity exposure by up to about 6 percentage points depending on the vintage; the allocation at retirement stayed at 40%. Fidelity's May 30, 2026 Freedom Index prospectus described a revised asset allocation strategy expected to take effect by the end of the first quarter of 2027.
What it teaches
A glide path is a manager's policy, not a law of nature. Families revise them, usually gradually and with notice in the prospectus. Reread the "principal investment strategies" section every few years.
Source
BlackRock Funds III, Form 485BPOS filed April 30, 2026; Fidelity Aberdeen Street Trust, Form 485BPOS, prospectus dated May 30, 2026 (SEC EDGAR).
Case study 2Retiring into 2008: why the "to" versus "through" debate began
What happened
In 2008 the S&P 500 returned −36.6% while 10-year Treasuries gained 20.1% (Damodaran). A fund holding 40% stocks and 60% Treasuries would have lost about 2.6%; one holding 56% stocks about 11.6%. Investors who expected a "2010" fund to be safe two years before its date discovered how much its stock share mattered.
What it teaches
Know your fund's stock share at and around retirement before a crash. If a 10% loss at 63 would change your plans, a "to" design or an earlier target year may fit you better.
Source
Illustration computed from Damodaran, NYU Stern, 2008 returns; not the returns of any actual fund.
Case study 32022: when every glide path fell together
What happened
Stocks returned −18.0% and 10-year Treasuries −17.8% in 2022. In the proxy, a fund with 91% stocks and one with 40% stocks lost almost exactly the same amount.
What it teaches
Glide paths reduce stock risk; they do not remove interest-rate risk. That is one reason some families now add short-term TIPS near retirement, as Vanguard's workplace materials describe beginning around age 60.
Source
Computed from Damodaran, NYU Stern; Vanguard Workplace Solutions, "Target-date fund glide path."

Decisions

Target-date choices in real lives

Scenario 1 · Maya

Maya's new employer automatically enrolled her in its target-date fund. She will turn 65 in 2067, and the plan offers 2065 and 2070 funds. A coworker says she should also add an S&P 500 fund "for more growth." What should she do?

Keep it simpleAt her age the 2065 and 2070 funds are nearly identical (about 91–92% stocks in Vanguard's series). Her real decisions are elsewhere: contribute at least 6% to get the full 50% match, and deal with her 6.8% loans.
Split 50/50Raises her stock share only from about 91% to about 96% while cutting her international exposure roughly in half, and she would have to rebalance herself. Little gain for more complexity.
All S&P 500Defensible for a 24-year-old in theory, but she gives up bonds, international stocks and automatic de-risking — and must remember to change course decades from now.
The principle: a target-date fund works best alone; to change the risk, change the year.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers

Tom and Rachel plan to retire around 2031–2032. Tom's 401(k) offers a target-date series costing 0.62%; Rachel's offers an index series at 0.08%. Their taxable account holds 25 technology stocks. Which is the sensible structure?

Two 2030 fundsEach fund would do its job, but their $220,000 of technology stocks would sit on top, pushing their true stock share well above the glide path and concentrating it in one sector. They would also pay 0.62% on Tom's balance.
One household portfolioRight. A target-date fund cannot see other accounts. At their level of wealth, managing the combined mix (or paying their advisor to) is worth the effort, especially with sequence risk just ahead (INV-078).
The principle: a target-date fund assumes it is your whole portfolio; if it is not, plan the whole.
Scenario 3 · Ruth

Ruth's $780,000 IRA is invested in a 2020 "through" fund holding about 36% stocks. A friend says she is "in the wrong fund because 2020 has passed." What should she consider?

Sell immediatelyA misunderstanding. "Through" funds keep managing the mix after the date, and the fund may later merge with its family's retirement-income fund.
Check and keepRight. With Social Security covering most of her bills, she might even prefer slightly more in stocks than the fund holds; that is a judgment about her whole plan, not a defect in the fund.
The principle: after the target year, read what the fund is doing now, not what the label says.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

On a $400,000 balance, how many more dollars a year does a 0.62% target-date fund cost than a 0.08% one?

0.0062 × $400,000 = $2,480; 0.0008 × $400,000 = $320; difference $2,160 a year, before any compounding.
Exercise 2

Using Figure 50.11, what percentage of the 2050 fund's stocks are foreign?

Stocks total 54.4% + 36.8% = 91.2%. Foreign share of stocks = 36.8 ÷ 91.2 = 40.4%.
Exercise 3

A fund with 40% stocks and 60% 10-year Treasuries lived through 2008 (stocks −36.55%, Treasuries +20.10%). What was its return, in percent (one decimal)?

0.40 × (−36.55%) = −14.62%; 0.60 × 20.10% = +12.06%; total −2.6%.
Exercise 4

Maya puts half her 401(k) in a fund with 91.6% stocks and half in an S&P 500 fund. What is her overall stock share, in percent (one decimal)?

0.5 × 91.6% + 0.5 × 100% = 45.8% + 50% = 95.8%. Her international share of stocks also falls from about 40% to about 19%.
