Planning by Life Stage

The right financial move at 25 is often the wrong one at 65. This module walks through the priorities of each decade, the ages at which the rules change, and how your net worth compares with the Federal Reserve's most recent survey of American families — and why that comparison matters less than you might think.

The question this module answersWhat should I be focusing on at my age, and how am I doing compared with others at the same stage?

Start here

The same principles, different priorities

Some rules never change: spend less than you earn, keep a cushion, diversify, keep costs low. You already know them, and they hold at every age. What changes is which problem is most urgent and which tools become available or disappear.

That shifting set of priorities is what planners call life-stage planning. Early on, the biggest asset is your future earnings and the biggest lever is the habit of saving. In the middle years, other people depend on you, so protection matters most. Near and in retirement, the job turns from building a portfolio to turning it into income that lasts. Figure 26.1 shows how the typical family's net worth changes with age along the way.

What this module covers

  1. How to read net-worth benchmarks, with a calculator using the Federal Reserve's data.
  2. The priorities of each decade, from your 20s to your 70s and beyond.
  3. The ages at which the rules change: catch-up contributions, penalty-free withdrawals, Social Security, Medicare, required distributions.
  4. What each of the six households should focus on now.

How am I doing?

Net-worth benchmarks from the Survey of Consumer Finances

The best public source on American family wealth is the Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances, run every three years. The most recent published survey covers 2022; results were released in October 2023. The 2025 survey was fielded in 2025, but its results had not been published as of September 2026.

Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF)Federal Reserve Board, every three years
What it is
A detailed triennial survey of US families' assets, debts, income and financial behavior, designed to represent all families, including wealthy ones (only the 400 wealthiest people in the country are excluded). The 2022 survey completed 4,602 interviews.
Used for
Measuring the distribution of wealth, retirement readiness, debt burdens and how they change; setting benchmarks like the ones below.
What it means for you
It tells you where a family of your age stood in 2022. It does not tell you whether your own plan is on track; only your goals can do that.
Median and mean
What it is
The median is the middle value: half of families have more, half less. The mean is the ordinary average: total wealth divided by the number of families.
Used for
Describing a "typical" family (median) versus the total spread across families (mean).
What it means for you
Wealth is extremely unequal, so the mean is pulled far above the median by the richest families. For a benchmark, use the median.
Going furtherFour cautions when comparing yourself
  1. Families, not individuals. The SCF measures the "primary economic unit," so a married couple is one family. A single person should expect to be below a figure that includes two-earner couples.
  2. Home equity is included. Among homeowners the 2022 median net worth was $396,200; among renters, $10,400. Much of the typical family's wealth is its house.
  3. Dollars of 2022. Prices rose about 9.3% from 2023 to 2025 (Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers, CPI-U, in the course dataset); the tool can scale the figures.
  4. Markets move the benchmark. Real median net worth rose 37% from 2019 to 2022, the largest three-year increase in the modern survey; from 2007 to 2010 it fell 39%.
Practitioner depthWhy the SCF is the reference survey

Household surveys usually miss the wealthiest families, who hold a large share of total wealth. The SCF adds a supplemental "list sample," drawn from statistical records derived from tax returns, to reach them, and interviews each family at length about every account, property and debt. That is why its mean is so much higher than other surveys' and why economists treat it as the benchmark for wealth distribution. The trade-off is its three-year cadence: between surveys, the figures age.

Starting out

Your 20s: build the habits and the base

In your 20s your portfolio is small and your future earnings are large. The single most valuable thing you can build is a saving habit, because every dollar invested now has four decades to compound. Figure 26.3 makes it concrete.

Priorities in your 20s

  1. A starter cushion, growing to three months of essential spending (INV-019).
  2. The full employer match. A 50% match on 6% of pay is an immediate 50% return on that money (INV-022).
  3. High-interest debt first, then a plan for student loans. In 2022, 22% of families had student debt; among them the median balance was about $24,500 and the mean about $46,600 (SCF).
  4. A retirement saving rate that rises with your pay. Raising your contribution by a percentage point with each raise is painless and powerful.
  5. Credit built carefully (INV-021) and health, renters and, if others depend on your income, disability coverage (INV-023).
💡

Limits for 2026. You can contribute up to $24,500 to a 401(k), 403(b), governmental 457 plan or the Thrift Savings Plan, and up to $7,500 to individual retirement accounts (IRAs, traditional and Roth combined), or your taxable compensation if less. Source: IRS news release IR-2025-111 (November 2025) and IRS IRA contribution limits page.

Going furtherWhy a Roth often fits your 20s

Early-career workers are often in their lowest tax bracket of their working lives. Paying tax now on Roth contributions, and never on the growth, can be a good trade if your rate later will be higher. It also builds flexibility: Roth IRA contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn without tax or penalty. INV-032 covers the rules and the income limits.

Building a family

Your 30s: protect the people who depend on you

For many people the 30s bring a partner, children and a mortgage. The balance sheet grows, but so do obligations. The priority shifts from building alone to building and protecting: if an income stopped tomorrow, would the family be secure?

How much life insurance? One way to estimate it is to ask what it would cost to replace an income until the children are grown. Replacing Elena Rivera's $60,000 a year for 15 years, with the money invested at a 2% real return, requires a lump sum of about $771,000 in today's dollars; replacing Marcus's $85,000 requires about $1.09 million. Subtract savings the family would use and add the mortgage balance if it should be paid off, and the result is a starting point for term coverage. This is an illustration of the arithmetic, not a recommendation of an amount.

Lump sum = Income × [1 − (1 + r)−n] ÷ r    (60,000 × [1 − 1.02−15] ÷ 0.02 ≈ $771,000)
Going furtherThe home decision

About 66% of families owned their home in 2022 (SCF), and home equity is the largest asset for most of them. A home is a place to live first and an investment second: it concentrates wealth in one property in one town, costs money to maintain, and cannot be sold a room at a time. Stage 9 compares renting and buying (INV-063) and mortgages (INV-064). The Riveras' 3.1% mortgage, for example, costs less than many safe investments now pay, so paying it off early is rarely their best use of spare cash.

Practitioner depthOwnership and titling

Marriage changes how property is owned. In the nine community property states (Arizona, where the Riveras live, is one), most property acquired during marriage belongs to both spouses equally, with consequences for taxes, divorce and death. How a home and accounts are titled, and who is named as beneficiary, often matters more than the will. INV-027 and INV-085 cover the details.

Mid-career

Your 40s: peak pressure and a checkpoint

The 40s are often the most crowded decade: peak costs for children, perhaps help for aging parents, and a retirement that is suddenly only 20-odd years away. It is also a good checkpoint. If saving has been light, there is still time; each extra dollar has about two decades to grow.

Priorities in your 40s

  1. Check retirement progress against a goal, not against coworkers. Use INV-024's saving calculator with a realistic return.
  2. Keep retirement ahead of college. Students can borrow; nobody lends you a retirement.
  3. Diversify concentrated positions. Employer stock that has grown for years is a common mid-career risk (INV-004).
  4. Review protection. Life and disability coverage as income peaks; long-term care is still some years off (INV-083).
  5. Start the conversation with aging parents about their plans and documents (INV-097).
Going furtherThe sandwich years

Supporting children and parents at the same time can squeeze retirement saving for years. The order in INV-024 still applies: protect the household first, keep at least the employer match flowing, and make any support for adult children or parents a decision with a written amount rather than an open-ended commitment. Help for parents often works best when it is planned with the whole family, including siblings, before a crisis.

Pre-retirement

Your 50s: the catch-up decade

The 50s often combine the highest income of a career with children leaving home, which frees cash flow. The tax code rewards it: from the year you turn 50, you may make extra catch-up contributions, and a higher catch-up applies at ages 60 through 63.

For the Harpers, 57 and 55, both are eligible for the 50-plus catch-up in 2026: each can defer up to $32,500, or $65,000 together, before any employer contributions. Six years of $65,000 at a 5% real return adds about $442,000 in today's dollars. Their priorities are just as much about shape as size:

  1. Reduce risk they do not need (INV-025) and diversify their 25 technology stocks with a tax plan (INV-035, INV-037).
  2. Balance tax buckets. With $820,000 pre-tax and only $60,000 in Roth, Roth contributions or conversions may help later (INV-038).
  3. Plan the first five years of retirement income, where sequence-of-returns risk is highest (INV-077).
  4. Price health coverage from retirement to 65, when Medicare begins (INV-079).
  5. Price the advice. At 1.25% of $1.1 million they pay $13,750 a year; now is when advice can earn it, or not (INV-007).

Monica Walker, 58, is in the same decade with a different shape. She contributes 12% of her $92,000 pay ($11,040) and her employer adds $3,680. At a 5% real return, her $370,000 of invested savings grows to about $574,000 in today's dollars by 67 with no further saving, and to about $736,000 if she keeps saving $14,720 a year. Adding the catch-up in full ($8,000 at 58–59 and 64–66, $11,250 at 60–63, $85,000 over nine years in today's dollars) raises it to about $841,000. Her gap after Social Security, including about $3,500 a year of federal tax, is about $38,300 a year, so at a 4% first-year withdrawal (INV-076) her target is about $957,500. Current saving reaches about 77% of it ($29,450 a year of withdrawals); the full catch-up schedule reaches about 88% ($33,600 a year). Her binding limit is cash flow, not the tax code: with essentials of $5,200 a month, each extra 1% of pay ($920 a year) costs her about $810 of take-home pay after the 12% federal bracket. Her other priorities are protection for Eli, which cannot wait for retirement, and a plan for the first withdrawals, where sequence risk is highest (INV-077).

Going furtherAge 55 and 59½

Withdrawals from IRAs before age 59½ generally owe a 10% additional tax on top of income tax, with exceptions (IRS Publication 590-B). Some 401(k) plans allow penalty-free withdrawals if you leave the employer in or after the year you turn 55. These rules matter to anyone retiring in their 50s; INV-074 covers every route to money before 59½.

Retirement

Your 60s, 70s and beyond: turning savings into income

In your 60s the job changes from accumulating to spending. The key decisions are when to stop work, when to claim Social Security, how to bridge to Medicare, and in what order to draw from each account. In your 70s and beyond, the focus shifts again: required distributions, simplicity, protecting against fraud and cognitive decline, and passing on what remains.

Priorities in your 60s

  1. Decide the Social Security claiming age between 62 and 70; for people born in 1960 or later, full retirement age is 67, and delaying beyond it raises the benefit until 70 (INV-078).
  2. Enroll in Medicare on time. The initial enrollment period generally lasts seven months, from three months before the month you turn 65 to three months after; missing it can bring a lifelong late penalty (Medicare.gov; INV-079).
  3. Set a withdrawal plan and order across taxable, pre-tax and Roth accounts (INV-042, INV-076).
  4. Keep several years of withdrawals in safe assets so a crash never forces a sale (INV-077).

Priorities in your 70s and beyond

  1. Required minimum distributions from traditional IRAs and most workplace plans begin at 73 under current rules (IRS).
  2. Qualified charitable distributions: from age 70½, IRA owners can transfer money directly to charity tax-free, and from 73 these count toward the required distribution (IRS).
  3. Simplify. Consolidate accounts, automate income, and name a trusted contact at each institution.
  4. Keep documents current: powers of attorney, health care directives, beneficiaries (Stage 12).
Going furtherWhy the wealth curve bends down after 75

Median net worth was $409,900 for families headed by someone 65–74 in 2022 and $335,600 for those 75 and older. Spending down savings is part of it, and that is the point of saving. But the two groups are also different generations with different histories, so the dip is not a precise measure of how fast any one retiree spends.

Real evidence

What the surveys show about life stages

Case study 1A record three years for the typical family, 2019–2022
What happened
Between the 2019 and 2022 surveys, real median net worth rose 37% to $192,900 and real mean net worth 23% to $1,063,700. The Federal Reserve called the median's growth the largest increase in the history of the modern SCF, more than double the next-largest on record. Under-35 families saw the largest gain, from $16,100 to $39,000 in 2022 dollars.
What it teaches
Benchmarks move with house and stock prices. A family that fell behind the 2019 median might look fine against 2022's, or the reverse, with no change in its own habits. Measure yourself against your plan.
Source
Aladangady, A., et al. (2023). Changes in U.S. family finances from 2019 to 2022: Evidence from the Survey of Consumer Finances. Federal Reserve Board.
Case study 2The Great Recession, 2007–2010
What happened
Between the 2007 and 2010 surveys, real median net worth fell 39% and real mean net worth 15%; between 2010 and 2013 the median slipped a further 2% despite the recovery in house and other asset prices.
What it teaches
A severe downturn hits families differently by life stage. Young families can wait and keep buying; families near retirement may have to sell or delay. That is why risk capacity falls as the spending date nears (INV-025).
Source
Aladangady et al. (2023), footnote on earlier survey periods.
Case study 3Only a third feel on track, 2025
What happened
In the Federal Reserve's 2025 Survey of Household Economics and Decisionmaking, 35% of non-retirees thought their retirement savings plan was on track, up from 2022 and 2023 but below 40% in 2021.
What it teaches
Most working adults are unsure whether they are saving enough. The remedy is not a benchmark but a number: a retirement goal priced in today's dollars and the monthly saving it needs (INV-024, INV-075).
Source
Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System (2026), Economic well-being of U.S. households in 2025.

Decisions

Stage-appropriate choices

Scenario 1 · Jordan

Jordan reads that the median net worth for families under 35 is $39,000. Hers is about −$25,000 because of her student loans. She feels behind and considers pausing retirement contributions to pay the loans faster. What should she do?

Stop the 401(k)She would give up $1,860 a year of match and years of compounding to improve a comparison that measures other people. Negative net worth at 24 with a steady job is common and temporary.
Match, cushion, extra to loansRight. The match is worth more than the loan interest, and the cushion keeps a surprise off a credit card. The benchmark is context, not a target.
The principle: in your 20s, habits and free money beat rankings.
Scenario 2 · Monica

Monica, 58, learns that her net worth of about $615,000 is well above the $364,500 median for families aged 55 to 64. A friend says she is "ahead" and could cut her 401(k) contribution from 12% of pay to the 4% that still captures the full match, putting the $7,360 a year difference toward her 3.4% mortgage. What balances the life stage best?

Cut to 4%The median describes other households, most without a single income or a child who will need support for life. At a 5% real return her $370,000 of invested savings reaches about $736,000 by 67 at 12%; saving less shrinks the pot that must fill a gap of about $3,190 a month (including tax) and back Eli's trust. The mortgage costs only 3.4% and ends in 12 years regardless.
Keep saving and use the catch-upRight. Her benchmark is her own plan: a retirement gap of about $38,300 a year in today's dollars, including tax (a target of about $957,500 at 4%), plus money for Eli after her death. The 50-plus catch-up, and the larger 60–63 catch-up from 2028, exist for exactly this decade.
Stop the 401(k)She would give up $3,680 a year of employer match, a guaranteed return no mortgage payoff can match, in the last nine years before retirement.
The principle: in your late 50s, measure against your own plan, not a median, and use the catch-up years.
Scenario 3 · Ruth

Ruth, 68, has accounts at three institutions, CDs at two banks, and a beneficiary form on her IRA that still names Walter. What should come first?

Leave itAn outdated beneficiary form can send an IRA through a default process that may not match her wishes, and scattered accounts are harder for anyone helping her to manage.
Update and simplifyRight. A beneficiary designation overrides a will. Fewer accounts make withdrawals, required distributions from 2031 and any future help from Anna far easier.
The principle: in your 70s and beyond, simplicity and current paperwork are financial assets.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

The Riveras own a $420,000 home with a $310,000 mortgage and have $62,000 in 401(k)s, $9,000 in an emergency fund and $41,000 in checking. How many times the 35–44 median ($135,600) is their net worth?

Net worth = 420,000 − 310,000 + 62,000 + 9,000 + 41,000 = $222,000. 222,000 ÷ 135,600 = 1.64.
Exercise 2

Tom (57) and Rachel (55) each have a 401(k). What is the most they can defer together in 2026, before employer contributions?

Each: $24,500 + $8,000 catch-up = $32,500. Together: $65,000.
Exercise 3

$6,000 is invested at the end of each year for 40 years at a 5% real return. What is it worth at the end, in today's dollars?

1.0540 = 7.0400. 6,000 × (7.0400 − 1) ÷ 0.05 = $724,799.
Exercise 4

What lump sum, invested at a 2% real return, replaces $60,000 a year (paid at the end of each year) for 15 years?

1.02−15 = 0.74301. 60,000 × (1 − 0.74301) ÷ 0.02 = $770,956.
