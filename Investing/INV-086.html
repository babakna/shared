Probate

Probate is the court-supervised process of proving a will, paying a person's debts and taxes, and transferring what is left. Its reputation is worse than its reality in many states, and better than its reality in a few. This module walks through the steps and deadlines, shows real statutory fee schedules, explains the small-estate shortcuts, and sorts which assets need probate at all.

The question this module answersHow much of my estate would go through probate, what would that involve for my family, and is it worth avoiding?

Start here

What probate actually is

You have probably heard that probate is something to avoid at all costs. For some estates, in some states, that is fair. For many ordinary estates it is closer to a supervised checklist: someone proves the will, gets authority from the court, collects the assets, pays the bills, files the tax returns and hands out what is left.

Probate exists for good reasons. Banks and buyers need to know who has the legal right to sell a dead person's house or close their account. Creditors need a fair chance to be paid. Heirs need protection from an executor who might take more than their share. The court supplies all three through one document — letters that give the personal representative authority — and through deadlines and accountings.

What makes probate expensive or slow is usually not the core process but its surroundings: fees set as a percentage of the estate in a few states, disputes among heirs, real estate in more than one state, hard-to-value assets like a business or royalties, and executors who do not know what to do next. This module separates those from the basic process, using probate in Pennsylvania, where Ruth lives, as the running example.

Step by step

From death certificate to final distribution

Letters testamentaryor letters of administration if there is no will
What it is
The official document from the court or register that appoints the personal representative and proves their authority to act for the estate.
Used for
Opening an estate bank account, closing the decedent's accounts, selling property, dealing with the IRS and insurers.
What it means for you
An executor named in a will has no power to act until letters are granted. Until then, even the named executor can do little beyond arranging the funeral and securing property.

How it works in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania the process begins with the Register of Wills in the county where the person lived (for Ruth, Allegheny County). The will is proved by the oaths of two witnesses, or accepted without testimony if it was made "self-proved" with notarized affidavits when signed (20 Pa.C.S. §§ 3132, 3132.1). The register grants letters testamentary to the executor named in the will (§ 3155(a)). Without a will, letters of administration go, in order, to those entitled to the residue under the will, the surviving spouse, then heirs under intestacy law, then creditors (§ 3155(b)).

A bond — an insurance-backed promise to repay the estate if the executor mismanages it — is generally required before letters are granted (§ 3171), but an individual named as executor in the will who lives in Pennsylvania, or whom the will excuses from bond, generally does not need one unless the court orders it (§ 3174). This is one reason a will saves money: a court-appointed administrator for an intestate estate may have to pay for a bond.

The executor's to-do list

  1. Order several certified death certificates; secure the home and valuables; find the original will.
  2. File for letters and obtain an employer identification number for the estate from the IRS; open an estate bank account.
  3. Advertise the grant of letters and notify beneficiaries as the court's rules require.
  4. File a verified inventory of the probate assets with the register (§ 3301).
  5. Pay valid debts and expenses; file the final personal income tax return, any estate income tax return (Form 1041), the Pennsylvania inheritance tax return (REV-1500) and, if required, federal Form 706.
  6. Prepare an accounting, get receipts and releases from beneficiaries or court approval, and distribute.
Going furtherSafe deposit boxes in Pennsylvania

Pennsylvania requires an inventory before the contents of a decedent's safe deposit box are removed, unless the box was held jointly by spouses and one survives. Until the inventory, no one may enter except to remove a will or burial instructions in the presence of a bank employee. The estate representative must give the Department of Revenue at least seven days' written notice before the inventory (REV-584). A will kept in a bank box is therefore reachable, but slowly; many attorneys keep the original instead.

Practitioner depthInformal probate in Uniform Probate Code states

Arizona and Colorado follow the Uniform Probate Code, which offers "informal" probate handled by a court registrar without a hearing when no one objects, and "formal" probate before a judge when there is a dispute. Many uncontested estates in those states never see a courtroom. Pennsylvania's Register of Wills system is similarly administrative for uncontested estates. The labels differ, but in each the goal for an uncontested estate is the same set of steps.

The money question

What probate costs, measured honestly

You will often see a single national figure for probate costs, such as "3% to 7% of the estate." We do not repeat one here, because it depends heavily on the state, the size of the estate, the fee arrangement and whether anyone fights. What we can show precisely is what the law says in two contrasting states.

California: a fee set by statute

California sets the ordinary compensation of both the personal representative and the estate's attorney by the same schedule: 4% of the first $100,000, 3% of the next $100,000, 2% of the next $800,000, 1% of the next $9 million and 0.5% of the next $15 million, with a court-determined amount above $25 million (Cal. Prob. Code §§ 10800, 10810). The base is the gross value of the probate property "without reference to encumbrances," so a house is counted at full value even if it carries a mortgage.

Pennsylvania: "reasonable and just"

Pennsylvania has no fee schedule. The court "shall allow such compensation to the personal representative as shall in the circumstances be reasonable and just, and may calculate such compensation on a graduated percentage" (20 Pa.C.S. § 3537). Attorneys may charge hourly, a flat fee or a percentage by agreement. Family executors often waive their commission, especially when they are also the only heir, because an executor's fee is taxable income while an inheritance is not (IRS Publication 559).

The other costs

  • Filing fees set by each county's register or court, often scaled to the estate's size.
  • Advertising the grant of letters in a newspaper and a legal journal.
  • Bond premiums, if a bond is required.
  • Appraisals of real estate, businesses, collectibles.
  • Ancillary probate: a second proceeding in each other state where the decedent owned real estate.
  • Time: the executor's own hours, and the months during which heirs cannot use the property.
Going furtherProbate fees versus taxes

Probate costs and death taxes are separate. Avoiding probate with a revocable trust does not reduce federal estate tax or Pennsylvania inheritance tax at all: Pennsylvania taxes property passing by will, intestacy, trust, joint ownership with non-spouses and most other transfers alike (INV-089). Families sometimes confuse the two and pay for a trust expecting tax savings that do not exist.

Timelines

Why some estates close in months and others take years

No reliable national average exists for how long probate takes, and published figures vary widely. What is reliable is the structure: statutory waiting periods set a floor, and the estate's complications set everything above it.

A partial distribution is normal

The one-year creditor rule in Pennsylvania does not freeze the whole estate for a year. An executor may distribute earlier "at his own risk" (20 Pa.C.S. § 3532): they are protected against claims they did not know about within that year, but personally exposed if they hand out money and a known claim later goes unpaid. The usual practice is to pay known bills and taxes, make partial distributions, and hold back a reserve until the year has passed and final returns are accepted.

The long cases

The famous multi-year estates are the ones with disputes or unusual assets. Prince's estate, which CNN reported at $156 million, had no will; his six half-siblings were named his legal heirs, and the estate took six years to settle (INV-084). An ordinary estate with a clear will, a house and a few accounts is a different animal.

Going furtherWhat heirs can do while they wait

Assets that pass outside probate are usually available within weeks: a life insurance claim with a death certificate, an IRA or 401(k) transfer to an inherited account (INV-091), a POD account. That is a strong reason to make sure a surviving spouse or dependent has enough outside probate to live on for a year. Pennsylvania also lets banks pay up to $20,000 from a deceased depositor's account to close family members once a funeral bill or funeral director's affidavit is presented, and employers pay up to $10,000 of final wages and benefits to family, without letters (20 Pa.C.S. § 3101).

The shortcuts

Small-estate procedures in our households' states

Every state has a simplified route for small estates, but "small" means very different things, and the details matter: whether real estate counts, how long heirs must wait, and whether a court is involved at all.

What it is
A sworn statement (or, in some states, a short court petition) that lets heirs collect a small estate's assets without full probate and without an appointed personal representative.

If most of your wealth passes by beneficiary designation or joint title, the probate estate that remains may be small enough to qualify, even if your total wealth is large.

Going furtherRuth and the $50,000 petition

Pennsylvania's small-estate petition covers personal property only, "exclusive of real estate," and the court's authority to distribute personal property this way is not affected by the decedent owning real estate (20 Pa.C.S. § 3102). For Ruth, it could handle a small bank account and her car, but her house in her name alone would still need a grant of letters to be sold or transferred. That is why the question for Ruth is really about the house.

Sort your own assets

What passes outside probate

Six arrangements move property without a court. Each was covered in INV-085 or is covered in INV-087; here is the whole list in one place, followed by a sorter to see where your own assets would go.

  1. Beneficiary designations: retirement accounts, life insurance, annuities, HSAs.
  2. Joint ownership with survivorship: joint tenancy, tenancy by the entirety, Arizona's community property with right of survivorship.
  3. TOD and POD registrations on bank and brokerage accounts; vehicle TOD titles where allowed.
  4. Transfer-on-death deeds for real estate, in states that authorize them (not Pennsylvania or North Carolina, as of September 2026).
  5. A funded revocable living trust (INV-087).
  6. Small-estate procedures for whatever is left, if it is under the limit.

With the default inputs, Ruth's IRA and checking account pass outside probate, and her house, CDs and belongings go through it: $460,000 of her $1,255,000 of listed assets, or 37%. Switching the CDs and savings to a POD designation brings the probate estate to $400,000; titling the house to a trust would leave only the car and belongings, $20,000, which fits Pennsylvania's small-estate petition.

Practitioner depthWhy some probate can be healthy

Probate gives creditors a deadline, after which claims are cut off; non-probate transfers can leave heirs exposed for longer in some states. It gives disputes a forum with rules. And it provides court supervision where the heirs do not trust the executor. For a family with conflict, a modest probate estate under a clear will can be a feature rather than a failure.

Real law and history

What probate looks like in practice

Case study 1Prince's estate: six years and a $3 million closing budget
What happened
After Prince died without a will in 2016, a bank was appointed to administer his estate during the litigation. When the estate was settled in August 2022, court documents reported by CNN assigned Comerica Bank & Trust $3 million to pay the costs of closing the estate, "including the preparation of tax returns, professional fees, expenses and any awards entered in pending litigation."
What it teaches
Probate costs scale with complexity and conflict, not only with size. A large estate with a clear will and cooperative heirs can close far faster and cheaper than this.
Source
Garvey, M. (2022, August 3). Prince's estate is finally settled after a 6-year battle. CNN.
Case study 2California's fee schedule on a mortgaged house
The law
California computes statutory fees on the inventory's appraised value "without reference to encumbrances or other obligations on estate property" (Cal. Prob. Code § 10800(b)).
The arithmetic
A $500,000 house with a $400,000 mortgage, the decedent's only probate asset, has $100,000 of equity. The combined ordinary fees are still computed on $500,000: $26,000, or 26% of the equity the heirs actually receive.
What it teaches
In a percentage-fee state, avoiding probate for a heavily mortgaged house can matter far more than the house's equity suggests.
IllustrationRuth's estate in Allegheny County
The setup
Ruth dies with a will naming Anna executor and sole heir. Her IRA names Anna; her house, CDs and belongings are in her name alone.
The process
Anna files the will with the Register of Wills and receives letters testamentary. If Anna lives outside Pennsylvania, she avoids a bond only if Ruth's will excuses her from one (assumed here) or she serves with a Pennsylvania co-executor (§ 3174), which is a reason to include that clause. She advertises the letters, inventories the house and accounts, pays Ruth's final bills, files the inheritance tax return and pays within three months to get the 5% discount, sells or keeps the house, and distributes to herself after the creditor year, with a receipt and release.
What it teaches
For a single heir who is also the executor, Pennsylvania probate is mostly paperwork and patience. The inheritance tax, about $51,000 after the early-payment discount (INV-089), is a far larger cost than the probate process, and a trust would not reduce it.

Decisions

Avoid it, shrink it, or accept it?

Scenario 1 · Ruth

An attorney quotes Ruth a revocable trust package to keep her house out of probate. Anna is her only heir and executor. What should she weigh?

Avoid at all costsPennsylvania probate for a single heir and one house is usually manageable, and the trust saves no inheritance tax. "At all costs" is the wrong frame.
Compare and weighRight. If Ruth values having a successor trustee who can manage or sell the house if she develops dementia, the trust has a real benefit beyond probate. If not, a will, a POD on her CDs and good powers of attorney may do the job for less.
Anna on the deedIt avoids probate but gives Anna half the house now, may cost part of the step-up in basis (INV-090), and makes half the house taxable at Ruth's death anyway for inheritance tax.
The principle: price probate in your own state before paying to avoid it.
Scenario 2 · Monica

Monica's 401(k), Roth IRA, brokerage account and savings could all carry beneficiary, TOD or POD designations naming a trust for Eli, and Ohio lets her record a TOD designation affidavit on her house. What would be left for Ohio probate if she did all of that?

Very littleRight. With the house on a TOD affidavit and every account designated to Eli's trust, the probate estate could fall under Ohio's $35,000 release-from-administration limit. Monica still needs a will: it names Eli's guardian while he is a minor and sends any stray asset to his trust rather than to Eli.
EverythingMarital status does not matter; titling does. Designations work for single people too.
The principle: designations shrink probate; the will still names the guardian.
Scenario 3 · The Harpers

The Harpers' wills name each other, then Ethan, as executor. Ethan is 20 and in college. Is that a problem?

Always familyFamily executors save fees and know the wishes, but a 20-year-old settling an estate with $1.1 million of investments and a house, alone and grieving, is a heavy load.
Add experienceA sensible middle path: a trusted relative, friend or professional as co-executor or first successor, revisited as Ethan matures.
The principle: choose an executor for the job as it will be, not as a mark of affection.

Almost nothing to probate. With a named 401(k) beneficiary, Jordan's belongings and a small account would fall under Colorado's small-estate affidavit.

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

Under California's schedule, what are the combined ordinary fees of the executor and the attorney on a $500,000 gross probate estate, in dollars?

Each fee: 0.04 × 100,000 + 0.03 × 100,000 + 0.02 × 300,000 = 4,000 + 3,000 + 6,000 = $13,000. Combined: $26,000.
Exercise 2

Same schedule, a $1,000,000 gross probate estate. Combined fees, in dollars?

Each: 4,000 + 3,000 + 0.02 × 800,000 (16,000) = $23,000. Combined $46,000, or 4.6% of the estate.
Exercise 3

Ruth adds a POD designation to her $60,000 of CDs and savings. Her IRA ($780,000) and checking ($15,000) already pass outside probate. Her house ($380,000) and car and belongings ($20,000) stay in her name alone. How large is her probate estate, in dollars?

Only assets in her name alone with no beneficiary: $380,000 + $20,000 = $400,000.
Exercise 4

In Pennsylvania, for how many months after the first complete advertisement of letters does an executor who distributes early remain at risk for claims that become known to them?

20 Pa.C.S. § 3532: claims known within one year after the first complete advertisement, so 12 months.
