How US Income Tax Works

Every investing decision in this stage, from which account to use to which shares to sell, depends on a few pieces of tax machinery. This module builds that machinery from the ground up with the 2026 numbers: how income becomes taxable income, how brackets really work, why a credit beats a deduction, and the separate payroll taxes that most workers pay first.

The question this module answersHow is my federal tax actually calculated, and what is the real tax rate on my next dollar?

Start here

You already know the number on your paycheck

Most people meet the income tax twice a year: in the withholding line on each pay stub, and in the spring when a return either produces a refund or a bill. For many households that is all they need. Software asks the questions and does the arithmetic.

That is a perfectly ordinary way to live, and it works until you face a real choice: a traditional or a Roth 401(k), whether to sell a stock this year or next, whether a raise will "push you into a higher bracket". Those decisions turn on one question software rarely shows you: what happens to the tax bill if one more dollar of income arrives, or one more dollar is deducted? That is your marginal tax rate. To answer it you need the machinery, and the machinery is simpler than its reputation.

Take Jordan Brooks, 24, in her first full-time job in Denver at $62,000. She puts 6% of pay, $3,720, into her traditional 401(k) to earn the full employer match, and she pays about $1,900 of interest on her student loans this year. Figure 29.1 follows her $62,000 all the way down to the federal income tax she owes for 2026.

The path

Four subtractions between your pay and your tax

Federal income tax is not charged on everything you earn. It is charged on taxable income, which is what is left after a series of subtractions. Each subtraction has its own name and rules, and a rule elsewhere in the tax code often refers to one particular step, so it pays to know them by name.

Gross incomestep 1
What it is
All income the tax law counts: wages, self-employment income, interest, dividends, capital gains, rents, withdrawals from traditional retirement accounts, part of Social Security benefits, and more. Some income is excluded by law, such as municipal bond interest and qualified Roth withdrawals.
Used for
The starting point of the return, and a test for whether you must file at all.
What it means for you
Income that is never counted here, such as money growing inside an IRA or Roth, is the reason tax-advantaged accounts exist.
Adjusted gross income (AGI)step 3
What it is
Gross income minus a short list of "above-the-line" adjustments: deductible contributions to traditional individual retirement accounts (IRAs) and health savings accounts (HSAs), student loan interest, half of self-employment tax, and a few others. Pre-tax 401(k) contributions never appear in gross income at all, because they are left out of the wages on your W-2; the effect is the same.
Used for
Almost every income limit in the tax code starts from AGI: Roth IRA eligibility, the child tax credit phase-out, education credits, the new senior deduction, and the threshold for the net investment income tax.
What it means for you
Lowering AGI can help twice: it saves tax at your marginal rate and can keep you under a phase-out that would otherwise cost you a credit or deduction.
Standard deductionstep 4
What it is
A flat amount subtracted from AGI by anyone who does not itemize. The alternative, itemized deductions, adds up specific expenses: state and local taxes (capped), mortgage interest, charitable gifts and large medical bills. You take whichever is larger.
Used for
Keeping a basic amount of income free of tax and sparing most households from tracking receipts.
What it means for you
With the higher standard deduction made permanent in 2025, most households take it. Itemizing pays only when your listed expenses exceed the figure below.

Going furtherAGI versus MAGI

Modified AGI is AGI with certain items added back, and the definition changes from rule to rule. For the net investment income tax, MAGI adds back excluded foreign earned income, so for most people it simply equals AGI. For Roth IRA eligibility it adds back items such as deductible IRA contributions, and it excludes the income from a Roth conversion. When a rule mentions MAGI, find the definition for that rule before assuming it equals the AGI on your return.

Practitioner depthThe state and local tax cap after 2025

Public Law 119-21 raised the cap on itemized deductions for state and local income, sales and property taxes from $10,000 to $40,000 for 2025 and $40,400 for 2026, rising 1% a year through 2029 and returning to $10,000 in 2030. The cap is reduced by 30% of modified AGI above $505,000 in 2026 ($500,000 in 2025), but not below $10,000. Married individuals filing separately get half of each figure. In the reduction range, each extra dollar of income costs an extra 30 cents of deduction, which raises the effective marginal rate. Source: 26 U.S.C. §164(b)(6)–(7) as amended; IRS Publication 17 (2025).

Rates

Brackets tax slices, not whole incomes

The federal income tax is progressive: taxable income is cut into slices, and each slice is taxed at its own rate. The first slice is taxed at 10% for everyone, including millionaires. Moving into a higher bracket only raises the rate on the dollars inside the new bracket. Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025, made the seven rates first set in 2018 permanent; the IRS adjusts the bracket edges for inflation each year.

Marginal tax ratethe rate on your next dollar
What it is
The rate that applies to the last, or next, dollar of taxable income: the rate of the bracket you are in. Jordan's is 12%; the Riveras' is 22%.
Used for
Every "what if" question: the tax saved by a deduction or a traditional 401(k) contribution, the tax on a bonus, the value of shifting income between years.
What it means for you
Your marginal rate is the price of the next decision. It is the number to use when comparing traditional and Roth accounts (INV-031).
Effective tax rateyour average rate
What it is
Total tax divided by income. It can be measured against gross income or taxable income; this course uses gross income unless stated.
Used for
Understanding your overall burden and comparing across years or households.
What it means for you
It is always below your top marginal rate. It tells you how much you paid, not what your next decision costs.
Tax = Σ (ratek × income inside bracket k)   |   effective rate = tax ÷ income
Going furtherThe shortcut used on the IRS rate schedules

Rev. Proc. 2025-32 writes each bracket as "a base amount plus a rate on the excess". For a single filer with $80,000 of taxable income in 2026: $5,800 plus 22% of the excess over $50,400 = $5,800 + $6,512 = $12,312. The $5,800 is simply the tax on the first $50,400 (10% of $12,400 plus 12% of $38,000). The same answer, faster.

Practitioner depthWhen the real marginal rate is not the bracket rate

Phase-outs create hidden marginal rates. Each $1,000 of income above the child tax credit threshold removes $50 of credit, adding 5 points. Above $75,000 of MAGI, a single retiree's $6,000 senior deduction shrinks by 6 cents per dollar, so a 22% bracket works like about 23.3%. Up to 85% of Social Security benefits become taxable as other income rises, which for some retirees makes each extra IRA dollar cost 1.85 dollars of taxable income. And the 3.8% net investment income tax stacks on investment income above fixed thresholds. The honest marginal rate is found by recomputing the whole return with and without the extra dollar, which is exactly what the calculator in the next tab does.

Practitioner depthWhat the calculator does and does not do

It computes AGI, the larger of the standard or itemized deduction, the senior deduction with its 6% phase-out, ordinary tax from the 2026 schedules, preferential rates for qualified dividends and long-term gains (INV-030), the $2,200 child tax credit with its $50-per-$1,000 phase-out above $200,000 ($400,000 joint) and the refundable portion (up to $1,700 per child, limited to 15% of earned income above $2,500), and the 3.8% net investment income tax. It ignores the alternative minimum tax, the earned income tax credit, education credits, the new tips, overtime, car-loan and non-itemizer charitable deductions, and all state taxes. For a real return, use IRS Free File, commercial software or a preparer; for withholding, the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator.

Two kinds of tax break

A deduction lowers income; a credit lowers the tax itself

Both are called "tax breaks", but they enter the calculation at different steps and are worth very different amounts.

Tax credit
What it is
An amount subtracted directly from the tax you owe. A nonrefundable credit can reduce your tax to zero but no further; a refundable credit can be paid to you even if you owe nothing.
Used for
Supporting families (child tax credit), work (earned income tax credit), education (American opportunity and lifetime learning credits) and retirement saving by lower earners (the saver's credit).
What it means for you
A $1,000 credit saves $1,000 whatever your bracket. A $1,000 deduction saves $1,000 times your marginal rate: $120 for Jordan, $220 for the Riveras.

Going furtherWhy the child tax credit is "partly" refundable

The refundable piece, the additional child tax credit, is limited to $1,700 per child for 2026 and to 15% of earned income above $2,500. A family with little or no income tax still receives up to that amount; a family that owes tax simply uses the whole credit against its tax. The Riveras owe more than $4,400 before credits, so they use their full $4,400 and the refundable rule never matters to them.

The other federal tax on work

Social Security and Medicare taxes come first

Before income tax, every worker pays payroll taxes, often labeled FICA on pay stubs, after the Federal Insurance Contributions Act. They fund Social Security and Medicare and are calculated on wages alone, with no deductions and no brackets.

Social Security wage base$184,500 for 2026
What it is
The maximum amount of earnings taxed for Social Security in a year, and also the maximum counted toward future benefits. It rises each year with the national average wage index.
Used for
Capping the 6.2% tax: a worker earning $184,500 or more pays $11,439 in 2026, and so does the employer.
What it means for you
Above the base, a salaried worker's payroll tax rate drops from 7.65% to 1.45% (or 2.35% above the additional Medicare threshold). If you change jobs mid-year and two employers together withhold too much Social Security tax, you claim the excess back on your return.
💡

For most workers, payroll tax is the bigger federal tax. Jordan pays $4,743 of payroll tax (7.65% of $62,000) and $4,585.60 of income tax. A traditional 401(k) contribution lowers her income tax but not her payroll tax: deferrals are still subject to Social Security and Medicare (IRS Publication 15). HSA contributions made through an employer's cafeteria plan are the main exception; they skip both (INV-033).

Real history and law

Three moments that shaped your tax bill

Case study 1The 16th Amendment, 1913
What happened
Congress passed the amendment on July 2, 1909; it was ratified on February 3, 1913 and certified on February 25, 1913, settling Congress's power to tax income after the Supreme Court had struck down an 1894 income tax. The National Archives notes that in 1913, because of generous exemptions and deductions, less than 1% of the population paid income tax, at a rate of only 1% of net income.
What it teaches
The income tax began as a tax on the very rich and became a tax on most workers. The rate structure is set by ordinary legislation, which is why it changes: seven rates today, made permanent in 2025, but "permanent" only until Congress acts again.
Source
National Archives, "16th Amendment to the U.S. Constitution: Federal Income Tax (1913)".
Case study 2Public Law 119-21, July 4, 2025
What happened
Many individual provisions of the 2017 tax law were scheduled to expire after 2025, which would have restored higher rates and a smaller standard deduction in 2026. P.L. 119-21, which the IRS now calls the Working Families Tax Cuts, made the seven brackets permanent, raised the standard deduction to $15,750 single and $31,500 joint for 2025 (indexed to $16,100 and $32,200 for 2026), raised the child tax credit to $2,200, and added temporary deductions for seniors, tips, overtime and car-loan interest for 2025–2028.
What it teaches
Plans built on "rates will go up in 2026" had to be revisited. Tax law is an input with its own uncertainty; decisions that depend on a specific future rate, like a large Roth conversion, deserve a margin for error.
Source
IRS, IR-2025-103 (October 9, 2025) and Rev. Proc. 2025-32.
Case study 3The Social Security wage base, 1937 to 2026
What happened
The first taxable maximum was $3,000 (1937–50). SSA notes that the amounts for 1937–74 and 1979–81 were set by statute and all others by the automatic adjustment provisions of the Social Security Act, which tie it to the national average wage index. It stayed at $106,800 from 2009 through 2011 and is $184,500 for 2026.
What it teaches
Consumer prices rose about 23-fold from 1937 to 2025, so $3,000 then is roughly $69,500 today; the 2026 base is about 2.7 times that because wages grew faster than prices. Indexing to wages keeps a stable share of total earnings under the tax.
Source
Social Security Administration, Office of the Chief Actuary, "Contribution and benefit base"; CPI from the course dataset (Damodaran, NYU Stern).

Decisions

Three tax decisions, three households

Scenario 1 · Jordan

Jordan is offered a promotion to $70,000. A coworker warns her that "the raise might put you in a higher bracket and you'll take home less." What should she do?

Refuse the raiseThis is the most common tax myth. At $70,000 her taxable income is $47,800 and she is still entirely in the 12% bracket. Even if a future raise pushes her over $50,400 of taxable income, only the dollars above that line pay 22%.
Take itRight. Of the $8,000 raise, $480 goes to her 401(k), $902 to federal income tax, $612 to payroll tax, and about $6,006 is take-home before Colorado tax. A higher bracket only ever taxes the new dollars.
Take it and stop the 401(k)That would forfeit the 50% match on 6% of pay, $2,100 a year at $70,000, to save a few hundred dollars of tax. The match is worth more than any bracket.
The principle: brackets tax slices; more income always means more after-tax income under the federal income tax.
Scenario 2 · The Riveras

The Riveras have about $9,600 of mortgage interest (3.1% on roughly $310,000), $2,600 of property tax, about $2,600 of Arizona income tax (2.5% of Arizona taxable income, less the $100 credit for each child) and $1,500 of charitable gifts. Should they itemize for 2026?

ItemizeItemizing would replace a $32,200 deduction with about $16,300, raising taxable income by $15,900 and their federal tax by $3,498, all of it at 22%. Itemizing is optional; you choose the larger.
Standard deductionRight. Their low-rate mortgage and Arizona's flat income tax keep their itemizable expenses well below the standard deduction. From 2026 their $1,500 of cash gifts is still deductible, under the new deduction for non-itemizers of up to $2,000 on a joint return, saving about $330. If they ever want to give more to charity, "bunching" several years of gifts into one year, often through a donor-advised fund, is the usual way to get over the line (INV-039).
The principle: compare your itemized total with the standard deduction each year; most households now take the standard deduction.
Scenario 3 · The Riveras' extra 401(k) dollars

The Riveras' taxable income is $104,100, which is $3,300 into the 22% bracket (it starts at $100,800 for joint filers). They can raise their traditional 401(k) contributions by $6,600 a year. How much tax does that save?

$1,452Only the first $3,300 comes out of the 22% bracket. The next $3,300 comes out of the 12% bracket.
$1,122Right: $726 + $396. This is why the traditional-versus-Roth choice (INV-031) is often "traditional up to the bracket edge, Roth above it" for families near a boundary.
NothingThe tax is deferred, not avoided, but deferral has value: the money grows untaxed, and it may be withdrawn later at a lower rate. Whether it beats a Roth depends on the two rates.
The principle: measure a deduction at the rates it actually removes income from, bracket by bracket.

Marginal and effective rates for each household in 2026

12% marginal, 7.4% effective, and payroll tax larger than income tax. With $10,120 of room left in the 12% bracket, Jordan is a natural candidate for Roth contributions (INV-031).

Head of household is worth real money. Filing as head of household rather than single gives Monica a $24,150 standard deduction and wider brackets: $92,000 of wages less $11,040 of pre-tax 401(k) contributions leaves taxable income of $56,810 and tax of $4,263 after the $2,200 credit for Eli, who is 13. Filing single would cost $2,518 more and put her top dollars in the 22% bracket instead of 12%. Eli turns 17 in 2030, when the child credit becomes a $500 credit for other dependents (about $5,963 of tax at 2026 figures). A child who is permanently and totally disabled can be a qualifying child at any age (IRS Publication 501), so if Eli meets that definition and keeps living with her, her head-of-household status need not end when he turns 19.
22% marginal, but close to extra taxes. AGI of $204,800 leaves them $45,200 under the $250,000 threshold of the 3.8% net investment income tax (NIIT), and their combined wages of $260,000 already trigger $90 of additional Medicare tax. Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A single filer has $80,000 of taxable income in 2026. What is the federal income tax, in dollars?

10% × $12,400 = $1,240; 12% × ($50,400 − $12,400) = $4,560; 22% × ($80,000 − $50,400) = $6,512. Total $12,312, an average of 15.4% on taxable income with a 22% marginal rate.
Exercise 2

A married couple filing jointly has $100,000 of taxable income in 2026. What is their federal income tax before credits, in dollars?

10% × $24,800 = $2,480; 12% × ($100,000 − $24,800) = $9,024. Total $11,504. They are still entirely in the 12% bracket, which for joint filers runs to $100,800.
Exercise 3

A taxpayer in the 24% bracket makes a $1,000 deductible IRA contribution. How much federal income tax does it save, in dollars? (Compare with a $1,000 credit.)

A deduction saves its amount times the marginal rate: $1,000 × 0.24 = $240. A $1,000 credit would save $1,000.
Exercise 4

Tom Harper earns $170,000 and Rachel $90,000. A colleague of Tom's earns $250,000. How much Social Security tax (employee share) does the colleague pay in 2026, in dollars?

Only wages up to the 2026 wage base count: 6.2% × $184,500 = $11,439. Tom, below the base, pays 6.2% × $170,000 = $10,540.
