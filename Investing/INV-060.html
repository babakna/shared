Biases That Cost Money

Most investing mistakes are not failures of intelligence. They are predictable patterns in how every human brain handles risk, loss and uncertainty. Psychologists and economists have measured these patterns, and what they cost, for almost fifty years. This module shows you the main ones, lets you test yourself, and gives you a defense for each.

The question this module answersWhich of my own instincts are most likely to cost me money, and what can I do about them before the moment arrives?

Start here

You are not a calculator, and neither is anyone else

If someone offers you a choice between $3,000 for certain and an 80% chance at $4,000, you probably take the sure $3,000. That is sensible: a bird in the hand. Now flip it. You must choose between losing $3,000 for certain and an 80% chance of losing $4,000. Most people now gamble.

In the gamble, the expected loss is 0.80 × $4,000 = $3,200, worse than the sure $3,000. Yet when Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky put these exact choices to respondents in their 1979 paper on loss aversion and prospect theory, 80% took the sure gain and 92% took the gamble to avoid the sure loss. The numbers were the same size; only the direction changed. Figure 60.1 shows the result.

This is the ordinary case, and it is worth conceding: in daily life these instincts often serve us well. Avoiding losses kept our ancestors alive; following the crowd is usually a good way to find the best restaurant. The trouble is that financial markets punish exactly these instincts. Selling after a fall locks in the loss. Buying what everyone is buying means buying after the price has already risen. Trading on confidence means paying costs that are certain for an edge that usually is not there.

Economists call these patterns biases: systematic, predictable departures from the choice a careful calculation would make. "Systematic" is the important word. These are not random errors that cancel out; they push most people in the same direction at the same time, which is why their cost can be measured.

What this module covers

  1. Loss aversion and prospect theory: why a loss hurts about twice as much as an equal gain pleases, and a model you can adjust.
  2. Overconfidence: what 66,465 households' trading records showed about active trading.
  3. Recency, anchoring and herding: the biases behind bubbles and performance chasing.
  4. Mental accounting and the disposition effect: why investors sell their winners and keep their losers.
  5. A self-test of eight scenarios, and a defense for each bias.

The next module, INV-061, measures the total cost of these behaviors, the "behavior gap," and walks through a century of bear markets, when they do the most damage.

The most important bias

Why a loss hurts more than a gain helps

Before 1979, economists mostly assumed that people judge a risky choice by its effect on their total wealth. Kahneman and Tversky showed that people do something different: they judge each outcome as a gain or a loss relative to where they are now, and they feel the losses much more. Their model is called prospect theory. In 2002 Kahneman shared the Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel for integrating insights from psychology into economics, especially about judgment and decision-making under uncertainty.

Prospect theoryKahneman and Tversky, 1979
What it is
A description of how people actually choose under risk. Outcomes are measured as gains or losses from a reference point; the pain of a loss is larger than the pleasure of an equal gain; and people are less and less sensitive to each additional dollar in either direction.
Used for
Explaining choices that expected-value arithmetic cannot, such as refusing a favorable coin flip, buying lottery tickets and insurance at the same time, and holding losing stocks too long.
What it means for you
How you feel about your portfolio depends on where you start counting. A balance that is down 10% from last month's high but up 40% from what you invested can feel like a loss. Choosing your reference point deliberately is part of staying calm.
Loss aversionλ (lambda)
What it is
The finding that losses weigh more than equal gains. Tversky and Kahneman (1992) estimated a median loss-aversion coefficient of 2.25: a loss feels about 2.25 times as large as a gain of the same size.
Used for
Explaining why most people find a 50/50 bet to win or lose the same amount "distinctly unattractive" (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979), why a coefficient of 2.25 implies refusing even a bet to win $1,100 or lose $1,000, and why market falls trigger selling.
What it means for you
A 20% fall will feel worse than a 20% rise feels good. Plan your mix around the fall you can live through, not the average return you hope for.

The value function can be written down. Use the tool below to see how a gain and a loss feel under the 1992 estimates, and how large a gain must be before a typical person accepts a 50/50 chance of losing.

v(x) = xα  for gains  ·  v(x) = −λ(−x)α  for losses
α = 0.88, λ = 2.25

At the defaults, a $1,000 gain is worth about +437 "felt" units and a $1,000 loss about −982. For the coin flip to feel fair, the possible gain must be about $2,513: $1,000 × 2.251/0.88. In other words, the typical person in these experiments wanted to be able to win about two and a half times what they could lose.

Going furtherWhy this matters in a falling market

Loss aversion does not just make falls unpleasant; it changes behavior. In the loss version of the problem in Figure 60.1, people took a worse gamble to avoid a sure loss. In a portfolio, the equivalent is "getting even": holding or even adding to a losing position in the hope it comes back, or, at the other extreme, selling everything to make the pain stop. Both are attempts to escape the feeling of a loss rather than decisions about the future. The defense is a written plan (INV-028) that says in advance what you will do when prices fall, usually "keep contributing and rebalance."

A related finding is the certainty effect: people overweight outcomes that are certain relative to those that are merely likely. It helps explain the appeal of "guaranteed" products, whose guarantees can be expensive (INV-017, INV-082).

Practitioner depthThe 1992 estimates and probability weighting

Tversky and Kahneman's 1992 paper, "Advances in prospect theory: Cumulative representation of uncertainty," fitted the model to each subject's choices. The median exponent of the value function was 0.88 for both gains and losses, and the median loss-aversion coefficient was 2.25. The same paper added probability weighting: small probabilities are overweighted and moderate-to-large ones underweighted, which helps explain why long-shot bets (lottery tickets, far out-of-the-money options) attract more money than their odds justify. These are medians from laboratory choices over hypothetical and modest stakes; individual loss aversion varies widely, and later research has debated how large it is for small stakes. The qualitative pattern, losses weigh more than gains, is among the most replicated results in behavioral economics.

Trading is hazardous to your wealth

Overconfidence and the cost of trading

Most people rate themselves above average at driving, judging character and, it turns out, picking stocks. That confidence would be harmless if it did not lead to action. In investing it leads to trading, and trading has costs that are certain while the benefit is not.

Overconfidence
What it is
Overestimating how much you know, how precise your forecasts are, or how much of a past success was skill rather than luck.
Used for
Explaining why individual investors trade far more than any rational need (saving, spending, rebalancing, taxes) would require.
What it means for you
A strong year for your own picks is weak evidence of skill. Every extra trade costs a spread, sometimes a commission, often taxes, and it is a new chance to be wrong.

The classic evidence comes from Brad Barber and Terrance Odean, who studied the accounts of 66,465 households at a large US discount broker from 1991 to 1996. They sorted households into five groups by how much they traded. Before costs, the groups earned almost the same: between 18.5% and 18.7% a year. After costs, the picture changed completely.

The average household turned over about 75% of its stock portfolio each year. The most active fifth averaged more than 250% a year, replacing its entire portfolio every few months. In the paper's words: "Our central message is that trading is hazardous to your wealth." A follow-up study by the same authors (2001) sorted accounts by gender, because psychologists had found men more overconfident in areas such as finance. Men traded 45% more than women, and trading reduced men's net returns by 2.65 percentage points a year, against 1.72 points for women.

Annual trading drag ≈ turnover × round-trip cost  ·  e.g. 75% × 4% = 3% a year

In the 1990s the average round-trip trade of more than $1,000 cost about 3% in commissions and 1% in spread, so 75% turnover cost about 3% a year. Commissions on US stocks at major brokers are now usually zero, which shrinks this cost, but it does not remove it. Spreads remain, short-term gains in taxable accounts are taxed at ordinary income rates (INV-030), and each trade is a fresh opportunity for the mistimed decisions that INV-061 measures. At the calculator's default of 75% turnover and 1% per round trip, the drag is 0.75% a year, which gives up about 14% of a 20-year result.

Going furtherSkill or luck?

Separating skill from luck takes far more data than people think. With five stocks and one year, a strong result is well within what chance produces: in any year, many amateur portfolios beat the market by luck alone, just as some coin-flippers throw five heads in a row. Professional managers with large research staffs mostly fail to beat low-cost index funds over long periods after costs (INV-043, INV-045). A useful habit is to write down, before each trade, why you are making it and what would prove you wrong; reviewing those notes a year later is humbling and corrects hindsight bias.

Practitioner depthWhat the study actually measured

Barber and Odean used monthly position statements and trades for households with common stock holdings. Monthly turnover averaged 0.19% for the lowest-turnover quintile and 21.49% for the highest. Gross returns were close to the market (the average household earned 18.7% gross against 17.9% for the value-weighted index, helped by a tilt toward small and value stocks that happened to do well). Net returns fell with turnover because of costs: weighted by trade size, a round trip cost about 0.9% in spread and 1.4% in commissions. The period was a strong bull market; the lesson is not the level of returns but the gap that trading opened between otherwise similar investors.

Bubbles and chases

Recency, anchoring and herding

Three biases often act together. Recency makes the recent past feel like the future. Anchoring ties judgments to a number that happens to be in your head. Herding makes other people's actions feel like evidence. Together they explain much of why investors buy high and sell low.

Recency bias
What it is
Giving too much weight to what has happened lately when judging what will happen next.
Used for
Explaining performance chasing: money flowing into last year's best fund or asset just as its best days may be behind it.
What it means for you
Three great years do not make a fourth more likely. Start every expectation from long-run history and a wide range around it.
Anchoring
What it is
Relying too heavily on a starting number, even an irrelevant one, when making an estimate.
Used for
Explaining why the price you paid, a stock's all-time high, or a round number shapes when you buy and sell.
What it means for you
The market does not know what you paid. "I'll sell when it gets back to even" is an anchor, not a plan.

How strong is anchoring? In Tversky and Kahneman's 1974 experiment, a wheel of fortune was spun in front of participants to produce a number between 0 and 100, and they were then asked to estimate the percentage of African countries in the United Nations. The number on the wheel was obviously random. Yet the group that saw 10 gave a median estimate of 25%, and the group that saw 65 gave 45%. Paying people for accuracy did not remove the effect.

Herding
What it is
Following what other people are doing rather than your own analysis, often called fear of missing out.
Used for
Explaining bubbles, crashes and surges of money into whatever is popular.
What it means for you
By the time "everyone" is buying, the good news is already in the price. If friends and coworkers are all excited about the same investment, that is information about sentiment, not value.

The dot-com bubble shows herding and recency at market scale. The Nasdaq Composite, dominated by technology companies, closed at a record 5,048.62 on March 10, 2000, after years of spectacular gains that encouraged ever more investors to pile in. By October 9, 2002 it had closed at 1,114.11, a fall of 78%. A more recent example appears in the case studies: investors in spot bitcoin funds poured in after price jumps and pulled out after falls.

Going furtherThe availability heuristic

Tversky and Kahneman also described the availability heuristic: judging how likely something is by how easily examples come to mind. After a crash dominates the news, another crash feels imminent; after years of calm, risk feels remote. Both feelings are about memory, not probability. INV-061 gives the base rates: in the 98 calendar years from 1928 to 2025, the S&P 500 with dividends fell in 26 and fell 20% or more in 6 (computed from Damodaran, NYU Stern).

Practitioner depthAnchors in professional settings

Anchors are everywhere in finance: a stock's 52-week high, an analyst's price target, an initial asking price for a house or a business, last year's salary in a negotiation. The 1974 paper also showed "insufficient adjustment" from partial calculations: high school students given five seconds to estimate 8×7×6×5×4×3×2×1 gave a median of 2,250, while those given 1×2×3×4×5×6×7×8 gave 512. The true answer is 40,320. Both groups anchored on the first few steps. When valuing anything, build the estimate from fundamentals first (INV-006) and look at market prices and targets afterward.

How we keep score

Mental accounting and the disposition effect

People keep money in mental "accounts": the rent money, the vacation fund, the bonus, the "house money" from a lucky trade. Labels can help with budgeting. But money is interchangeable, and treating it differently by source leads to choices that cost money.

Mental accountingThaler; Tversky and Kahneman
What it is
Treating money differently depending on where it came from or what it is labeled for, rather than as one pool.
Used for
Explaining why people carry credit card debt at 20%-plus while holding savings earning far less, or spend a tax refund more freely than a paycheck.
What it means for you
A dollar is a dollar. Look at your whole balance sheet: paying off a 22% card is a guaranteed 22% return (INV-020).

Tversky and Kahneman's 1981 paper on framing, which called these "psychological accounts" and drew on examples by the economist Richard Thaler, gives a clean demonstration. One group was told: you have decided to see a play costing $10 and, at the theater, discover you have lost a $10 bill; would you still buy a ticket? 88% said yes. A second group was told they had already bought the $10 ticket and lost it; would they buy another? Only 46% said yes. The loss is $10 either way. But a lost ticket is charged to the "theater" account, making the play feel like it costs $20.

Disposition effectShefrin and Statman, 1985
What it is
The tendency to sell investments that have risen ("winners") too soon and hold investments that have fallen ("losers") too long.
Used for
Explaining why investors realize gains more readily than losses, often the opposite of what is tax-efficient.
What it means for you
Each stock is filed in its own mental account with the purchase price as the reference point. Selling a winner "closes the account at a gain"; selling a loser admits a loss. The market ignores both feelings.

Terrance Odean tested this with trading records from 10,000 accounts at a discount broker from 1987 to 1993. On any day an investor sold something, he compared the share of paper gains that were realized with the share of paper losses that were realized. Investors realized 14.8% of their gains but only 9.8% of their losses, about 50% more readily. Holding the losers was not wise: the winners they sold went on to beat the losers they kept by 3.4 percentage points over the following year.

Proportion of gains realized (PGR) = realized gains ÷ (realized gains + paper gains) = 13,883 ÷ (13,883 + 79,658) = 0.148
Going furtherThe tax cost of the disposition effect

In a taxable account, selling a winner creates a taxable capital gain, while selling a loser creates a capital loss that offsets gains and, up to an annual limit, ordinary income. Holding losers and selling winners therefore tends to pay tax earlier and use losses later, the reverse of good practice. Odean's data show investors knew this at some level: only in December, when year-end tax planning comes to mind, did they realize losses faster than gains. Tax-loss harvesting (INV-037) turns this into a routine rather than a year-end afterthought.

Practitioner depthThe sunk cost and the "buy it today" test

The price you paid is a sunk cost: it is gone whether you sell or not, so it should not affect the decision. The practical test is to ask, for each holding, "If I had this amount in cash today, would I buy this investment at today's price?" If not, the only remaining questions are taxes and transaction costs. Inherited or long-held positions add the endowment effect: we value what we own more than we would pay to buy it. The same test applies.

Rules made in calm weather

A defense for each bias

You cannot switch off loss aversion or the pull of a crowd. What you can do is make decisions ahead of time, when you are calm, and arrange your accounts so that the easiest thing to do is the right thing. Economists call this precommitment. It works because most costly mistakes happen in a few emotional moments.

  1. Automate the good behavior. Payroll contributions, automatic escalation and target-date or balanced funds that rebalance themselves remove dozens of decisions a year. Status quo bias then works for you.
  2. Write an investment policy statement. One page: your target mix, when you rebalance (for example, once a year or when a holding drifts five percentage points), and what you will do in a 30% fall. INV-028 walks through one.
  3. Check less often. The investment does not change; how often it appears to be losing does. The tool below shows why.
  4. Separate speculation. If you enjoy picking stocks or trying new assets, give yourself a small, fixed "play" allocation and never top it up from the core portfolio.
  5. Use a waiting period and a second opinion. For any unplanned trade over a set amount, wait 48 hours and explain it to someone else first.
  6. Keep a decision journal. Write why you are doing something and what would prove you wrong. It is the best cure for overconfidence and hindsight bias.

With a 7% expected return and 16% volatility, this simple model says a daily look shows a loss about 49% of the time, a monthly look about 45%, a yearly look about 33%, and a look every five years about 16%. If each loss feels 2.25 times as strong as a gain, the average daily or monthly check feels bad, while the average look every five years feels good. The effect of too-frequent evaluation on a loss-averse investor is sometimes called myopic loss aversion.

Going furtherWhen an advisor earns their fee

Much of the value a good advisor adds is behavioral: talking a client out of selling in a crash or out of concentrating in a hot sector. INV-007 showed what advice costs; INV-103 covers how to choose. If you do use an advisor, agree in writing, in advance, what happens in a large decline.

From real history

Three measured cases

Case study 166,465 households, 1991–1996
What happened
At a large US discount broker, households that traded the most earned 11.4% a year after costs; those that traded least earned 18.5%; the market returned 17.9%. Before costs, the groups' returns were nearly identical. The average household turned over 75% of its portfolio a year.
What it teaches
Trading did not improve stock selection; it only added costs. The researchers concluded that overconfidence, not rational needs, best explained the trading.
Source
Barber, B. M., & Odean, T. (2000). Trading is hazardous to your wealth. The Journal of Finance, 55(2), 773–806.
Case study 2The dot-com bubble, 2000–2002
What happened
After years of rapid gains in technology and internet stocks, the Nasdaq Composite closed at a record 5,048.62 on March 10, 2000. It closed at 1,114.11 on October 9, 2002, 78% lower. The broader S&P 500 fell 49% from its March 24, 2000 close to October 9, 2002.
What it teaches
Herding and recency reinforce each other: rising prices attract buyers, whose buying raises prices further, until expectations detach from what companies could earn. Investors who held a diversified portfolio suffered, but those concentrated in the popular stocks suffered far more.
Source
Nasdaq Composite daily closes: Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, FRED series NASDAQCOM. S&P 500: Yardeni Research, Stock market historical tables: Bull & bear markets (2024), from Standard & Poor's data.
Case study 3Spot bitcoin funds, 2024–2026
What happened
The first US spot bitcoin exchange-traded funds began trading in January 2024. Morningstar estimates that from their launch through June 30, 2026 the funds' aggregate total return was 8.5% a year, but the average dollar invested in them lost about 5.8% a year, a gap of more than 14 percentage points. The biggest inflows came after bitcoin had already risen, in early 2024 and again in the first half of 2025, followed by redemptions during later price falls.
What it teaches
A good product can produce bad investor results when money chases it. Investors "bought into a story that had already played out," in Morningstar's words: herding and recency, in a single asset.
Source
Ptak, J. (2026, August 6). The winning formula for fund investors, and why others left money on the table. Morningstar (Mind the Gap 2026).

Decisions

Three households, three biases

Scenario 1 · Maya

Maya's written rule is to rebalance her 60/40 portfolio whenever stocks drift more than 5 points from target. Stocks then fall 20% while bonds are flat: her $370,000 becomes $325,600, and stocks are now 54.5% of it. She says, "I'll leave it alone until I'm back to $370,000, then I'll think about it." What should she do?

Wait for $370,000That balance is an anchor. It matters only because she once saw it on a statement; the market does not know it, and waiting lets the portfolio drift further from the risk level she chose.
Follow the ruleRight. Stocks at 54.5% are below the 55% edge of her band. Moving about $17,760 from bonds to stocks restores 60/40 ($195,360 of stocks, $130,240 of bonds) and buys at the lower prices the rule exists to exploit (INV-056).
Move to bondsLoss aversion in action. She would lock in the fall and would need a rule for getting back in, which most people who leave never write. Nine years from retirement, with money meant to support Eli for decades, she needs the recovery.
The principle: a plan made for good reasons should not be rewritten by a number that only matters to you.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers

The Harpers want to raise $40,000 from their taxable account of 25 technology stocks to diversify. Twenty of the stocks are well above their cost; five are below. Rachel wants to sell the biggest winners "to lock in profits." Tom wants to sell the losers. Who is closer?

Sell the winnersThis is the disposition effect. It realizes taxable gains now, keeps the losses unused, and keeps positions only because they are below an anchor. Odean found winners sold went on to beat losers kept.
Losers first, then trimRight. Realized losses offset realized gains, so the Harpers can trim concentrated winners with less tax. The question for every position is "would we buy this today?", not "are we up or down on it?" (INV-035, INV-037).
The principle: sell for the portfolio you want, not to close a mental account at a gain.
Scenario 3 · Denise

Denise's coworkers are all buying a new cryptocurrency token that has tripled in two months. She has $3,000 in savings and $28,000 of student loans at 6.8%. A coworker says, "You'll regret it if you don't." What should she do?

Buy with her savingsThis is herding with her only safety net. If the token falls, Denise has no emergency fund and still owes 6.8% on her loans. Morningstar's bitcoin-fund data show how badly money that chases a rise tends to fare.
Plan first, small stake if anyRight. The 50% 401(k) match and paying down 6.8% debt are guaranteed returns (INV-020, INV-022). A tiny, capped stake satisfies curiosity without risk to her plan. INV-015 covers crypto assets and INV-062 covers crypto scams.
The principle: other people's excitement is information about sentiment, not about value.

Test the claims

Six things people say about investor psychology

Apply it

Which bias is each household most exposed to?

Herding and recency. Denise's coworkers and social feeds are full of hot tips. Her defense is automation: the 401(k) contribution and match happen before she sees the money, and any speculation is capped.
Mental accounting. The Riveras keep $41,000 idle in checking, separate in their minds from the $9,000 emergency fund, while saving nothing yet for Sofia and Leo's education. One balance sheet would show a fully funded emergency fund, an opportunity to start 529 plans (INV-034), and money earning almost nothing.
Anchoring and loss aversion. A 20% fall in stocks would take about $74,000 off her $370,000 if it were all in stocks, and about $44,400 at 60/40, with no second income to steady her. The old balance becomes a number in her head. A written rebalancing band, followed whatever the balance, removes the anchor, and a fully stocked $34,200 reserve means she never has to sell in a bad month.
Overconfidence and the disposition effect. Twenty-five hand-picked technology stocks reflect confidence in one sector. A rule of selling losers first and trimming the biggest positions each year addresses both biases.
Loss aversion. After Walter's death, Ruth may feel every market fall more sharply. A cash reserve for the next few years of spending, separate from the IRA's investments, lets her ignore short-term swings (INV-076, INV-098).

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A gamble offers an 80% chance of losing $4,000 and a 20% chance of losing nothing. What is its expected value, in dollars? (Enter a negative number.)

0.80 × (−4,000) = −$3,200, which is worse than a sure loss of $3,000. Yet 92% of Kahneman and Tversky's respondents chose the gamble.
Exercise 2

Ignore diminishing sensitivity (set α = 1). With a loss-aversion coefficient of 2.25, how large must the possible gain be for a 50/50 chance of losing $1,000 to feel neutral, in dollars?

Neutral means G − 2.25 × 1,000 = 0, so G = $2,250. With α = 0.88 the answer rises to about $2,513, as in the tool.
Exercise 3

A household replaces 75% of its portfolio each year, and each round-trip trade costs 4% (3% commission plus 1% spread, as in the 1990s study). What is the annual cost, in percent of the portfolio?

0.75 × 4% = 3% a year. Over 20 years at 7% before costs, that gives up about 46% of the ending balance: 1 − (1 − 0.03)20 ≈ 0.456.
Exercise 4

Odean's accounts had 11,930 realized losses and 110,348 paper (unrealized) losses. What proportion of losses was realized, in percent?

PLR = 11,930 ÷ 122,278 = 0.0976, about 9.8%, compared with 14.8% of gains. Gains were realized about 1.5 times as readily as losses.
