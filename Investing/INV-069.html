REITs, Syndications and Crowdfunding

You can own real estate without owning a building: through shares of a real estate investment trust on the stock exchange, through a REIT that does not trade, through a private partnership run by a sponsor, or through an online offering. Each is a different bundle of liquidity, fees, taxes and trust. This module separates them with the rules the SEC and the tax code actually set.

The question this module answersIf I want real estate without being a landlord, which wrapper should I use, and what does each really cost me?

Start here

Owning a slice instead of the whole

Most people who own real estate own one property: their home. If you have a target-date or total stock market fund, you probably also own a little of hundreds of apartment buildings, warehouses and shopping centers without knowing it, because those companies are in the index.

That is the ordinary case, and for many households it is enough. The questions start when someone offers you more real estate: a real estate investment trust (REIT) fund in your 401(k), a non-traded REIT from a broker promising a steady 5% payout, a friend's apartment syndication, or an app that lets you buy $500 of a rental. They all say "real estate," but they differ on the things that matter most: whether you can sell, what you pay, who decides, and how the income is taxed. Figure 69.1 lines them up.

Concept 1

A company that agrees to pay out its income

A normal corporation pays tax on its profits, and its shareholders pay tax again on dividends. The REIT rules let small investors own large-scale, income-producing property the way an index fund owns stocks, without that double tax. In exchange, a REIT must stick to real estate and pay out most of its income.

Real estate investment trust (REIT)Internal Revenue Code §§856–857
What it is
A company that owns, and typically operates, income-producing real estate or real estate loans — office buildings, shopping malls, apartments, hotels, resorts, self-storage, warehouses, mortgages and loans — and that meets a set of tax tests. If it does, it deducts the dividends it pays, so most of its income is taxed only once, in shareholders' hands.
Used for
Giving investors diversified, professionally managed real estate with a stream of dividends. The SEC notes that, unlike other real estate companies, a REIT buys and develops properties mainly to operate them, not to resell them.
What it means for you
REIT dividends tend to be higher than those of other stocks because the law requires it, and most are taxed as ordinary income rather than at the lower qualified-dividend rate. That makes where you hold them — IRA or taxable — matter (INV-036).
Funds from operations (FFO)
What it is
A measure REITs report of what their properties earn from operations, built by adding depreciation on real estate back to net income and leaving out gains and losses from selling property.
Used for
Judging whether the dividend is paid from the business itself. Net income alone understates this, because it subtracts depreciation on buildings that may be holding their value.
What it means for you
A distribution that is consistently larger than FFO is being paid from something else — borrowing or new investors' money. The SEC warns that non-traded REITs "frequently" do this.
Going furtherEquity REITs and mortgage REITs

Equity REITs own buildings and earn rent. Mortgage REITs own mortgages or mortgage-backed securities and earn interest, usually with heavy borrowing, so they behave more like leveraged bond funds and are more sensitive to interest rates. Before buying a high-yielding REIT, check which kind it is.

Practitioner depthWhy REITs issue new shares so often

Because a REIT must distribute at least 90% of its taxable income, it cannot grow much from retained earnings. New acquisitions are funded by issuing shares or borrowing. That makes REITs sensitive to the cost of capital: when their share prices are high relative to the value of their properties, growth is cheap; when prices fall below property values, raising equity dilutes existing owners.

Concept 2

The same buildings, very different exits

A publicly traded REIT is listed on a stock exchange: you can buy or sell through any broker on any trading day, the price is visible, and it files audited reports with the SEC. A non-traded REIT (also called a public non-listed REIT) is registered with the SEC and files the same reports, but its shares do not trade. You buy from a broker or adviser taking part in the offering, and you sell back to the REIT itself, under its share repurchase plan, if and when it allows.

Non-traded REITSEC, investor.gov
What it is
An SEC-registered REIT whose shares are not listed on an exchange. The SEC lists four special risks: illiquidity; value transparency (non-traded REITs typically do not estimate a per-share value until 18 months after the offering closes); distributions paid from offering proceeds and borrowing; and conflicts of interest with an external manager paid on acquisitions and assets.
Used for
Offering a real estate portfolio whose reported value moves slowly, often with a high stated distribution rate. Some, including the two in this module's case studies, publish a monthly net asset value (NAV) and let investors redeem a limited amount each month.
What it means for you
The smooth price is not the absence of risk; it is the absence of a market. The SEC says sales commissions and upfront offering fees "usually total approximately 9 to 10 percent" of the investment. On $100,000, that is up to $10,000 that never reaches a building.

Going furtherWhy a smooth price can mislead

Public REIT prices react instantly to interest rates and the economy. Appraisal-based values react slowly, because appraisals look backward at comparable sales. When the two diverge — public REITs falling while a non-traded REIT's value holds — investors who can see the gap rush to redeem at the higher value, and the repurchase limits are what stop them. The two case studies in this module show exactly that.

Concept 3

Why a REIT's dividend is taxed like a paycheck, mostly

Because a REIT deducts its dividends, the income arrives in your hands untaxed at the corporate level, and the code taxes most of it as ordinary income, not at the 0%/15%/20% rates for qualified dividends. Your Form 1099-DIV splits each distribution into pieces with different treatment.

Qualified REIT dividends and the §199A deductionafter P.L. 119-21
What it is
A deduction of 20% of qualified REIT dividends, part of the qualified business income deduction created in 2017. It was scheduled to end after 2025. Section 70105 of P.L. 119-21 rewrote the provision that ended it, so the deduction continues for 2026 and later with no end date, still at 20%.
Used for
Lowering the top federal rate on ordinary REIT dividends: in the 24% bracket, 24% × 80% = 19.2%; in the 37% bracket, 29.6%. It is available whether you itemize or take the standard deduction, but the total §199A deduction cannot exceed 20% of taxable income minus net capital gain.
What it means for you
You must hold the REIT shares for more than 45 days within the 91-day window that starts 45 days before the ex-dividend date. The 3.8% net investment income tax still applies above its thresholds. Inside an IRA or 401(k) none of this matters: nothing is taxed yearly, and traditional-account withdrawals are ordinary income.
Going furtherREIT funds pass the deduction through

A mutual fund or ETF that holds REITs can pay "section 199A dividends" to its own shareholders, who then treat them as qualified REIT dividends if they meet the same holding-period rule for their fund shares (Form 1099-DIV instructions). So a REIT index fund in a taxable account keeps the 20% deduction.

Concept 4

Pooling money with a sponsor

A real estate syndication is a private deal: a sponsor finds a property, raises equity from investors through a limited partnership or limited liability company, borrows the rest, runs the property for several years, then sells or refinances. Investors are passive; the sponsor makes the decisions and is paid for it.

Regulation D and the accredited investorSEC
What it is
Most syndications are sold under Regulation D, an exemption from SEC registration. Under Rule 506(b) a sponsor may raise unlimited money from accredited investors and sell to no more than 35 non-accredited but financially sophisticated investors in any 90 days; public advertising is incompatible with it. Under Rule 506(c) the sponsor may advertise, but every buyer must be accredited and the sponsor must take reasonable steps to verify it.
Used for
Raising private capital without a prospectus. An individual is an accredited investor with net worth over $1 million excluding the primary residence, or income over $200,000 ($300,000 with a spouse or partner) in each of the last two years with the same expected, or with a Series 7, 65 or 82 license in good standing.
What it means for you
"Accredited" is a wealth test, not a protection. It means the SEC assumes you can evaluate the deal and survive losing the money. There is no SEC review of the offering's merits.
Preferred return and promote
What it is
The preferred return ("pref") is a rate — 8% in this module's examples — that investors receive before the sponsor shares in profits. The promote, or carried interest, is the sponsor's share of profits above the pref — 20% or 30% in the examples here.
Used for
Aligning the sponsor with investors: the sponsor earns big only if the deal does well. The order in which cash is paid is called the waterfall.
What it means for you
A preferred return is a priority, not a guarantee. If the property earns less, you get less. Fees charged regardless of results — acquisition, asset management, refinancing, disposition — come off the top.

In the default, the property earns 12% a year on equity for five years. After a 2% acquisition fee, a 1.5% annual asset-management fee and a 30% promote above an 8% preferred return, $100,000 becomes about $158,306: 9.62% a year, 2.4 points below the property's own return. At a 6% property return the investor earns 4.17% and the sponsor still collects $9,500 of fees.

Going furtherTaxes in a syndication

A partnership or LLC passes income, depreciation and losses through to you on a Schedule K-1. As a limited partner you are passive and generally cannot actively participate, so the $25,000 allowance does not apply; losses offset only passive income until you sell (INV-068).

Concept 5

Regulation Crowdfunding and Regulation A+

What the SEC tells crowdfunding investors
What it is
The SEC's investor bulletin lists the risks: offerings are speculative and early-stage businesses often fail; you are restricted from reselling for a year and may need to hold indefinitely; valuation is hard and you may overpay; disclosure is limited.
Used for
Knowing your rights. You can cancel a commitment for any reason until 48 hours before the offering deadline. Before investing, the platform must have you review educational materials and affirm that you can lose all of your investment.
What it means for you
Crowdfunding makes it easy to spread small amounts across many deals; it does not make each deal safer. Treat it like venture investing with a real estate theme.

Concept 6

A due-diligence checklist

For a public REIT index fund, due diligence is short: check the expense ratio and put it in the right account. Everything else deserves the questions in Figure 69.6 before you commit.

⚠️

Liquidity is worth something. A public REIT fund can be sold in a day. When a non-traded REIT or syndication pays a higher stated yield, part of that is compensation for money you cannot get back. Ask whether the extra yield is large enough to pay you for giving up the exit, after all fees.

For most households, the simplest real estate exposure is already in a total-market index fund. A dedicated REIT index fund adds more. Either can be held in an IRA or 401(k), where the ordinary-income character of REIT dividends does not matter each year (INV-036). Private deals and crowdfunding belong, if anywhere, in the small slice of a portfolio you could lose entirely.

Real filings

Two redemption crises in non-traded REITs

Case study 1Blackstone Real Estate Income Trust hits its limits, November 2022
What happened
In October 2022, BREIT fulfilled all repurchase requests: 2.7% of net asset value, about $1.8 billion. In November, requests exceeded the plan's 2% monthly and 5% quarterly limits. BREIT paid out 2% of NAV, about $1.3 billion, which was roughly 43% of the shares submitted; about 0.3% of NAV remained available for December.
What it teaches
A repurchase plan is not a promise of liquidity. It works when few people want out and is capped when many do — exactly the moments when you might need the money. The limits were disclosed in advance; many investors had not focused on them.
Source
Blackstone Real Estate Income Trust, Inc. (2022). Prospectus supplement (Form 424B3) filed December 15, 2022, SEC EDGAR.
Case study 2Starwood REIT narrows its exit, May 2024
What happened
Starwood Real Estate Income Trust said redemption requests had been above its plan's monthly and quarterly limits for a year and a half, peaking in January 2023. On May 23, 2024, its board limited repurchases to 0.33% of NAV a month and, from July 1, 1% a quarter. Its adviser agreed to waive 20% of its management fee, from 1.25% to 1.0% of NAV, until the 2% and 5% limits were restored.
What it teaches
The fee was 1.25% of NAV a year before any waiver, charged whether or not investors could leave. When comparing a non-traded REIT's yield with a public REIT fund's, subtract that cost and price the illiquidity.
Source
Starwood Real Estate Income Trust, Inc. (2024). Form 8-K and Exhibit 99.1, filed May 23, 2024, SEC EDGAR.

Decisions

Three pitches, three households

Scenario 1 · Ruth

A broker suggests Ruth move $150,000 of her $780,000 IRA into a non-traded REIT paying a 5.5% distribution, "with less volatility than the stock market." What should she weigh?

Accept the pitchThe low volatility comes from appraisal pricing, not lower risk. Ruth has required distributions starting at 73; an asset she cannot sell on demand is awkward in an IRA she must draw from.
Ask the questionsRight. The BREIT and Starwood filings show redemptions can be capped for long periods. A REIT index fund in her IRA gives the same kind of property exposure, sells in a day, and costs a fraction as much.
Go all inThat would concentrate her retirement savings in one illiquid product. No single non-traded offering belongs at that size.
The principle: a smooth price is not safety; know how you get out before you get in.
Scenario 2 · Maya

A crowdfunding app offers Maya a share of a Columbus fourplex renovation, projecting a 14% annual return. Her $30,000 of cash is her whole emergency fund, already $4,200 below its target, and she is nine years from retirement with a son who will need support for life. What makes sense?

Use the savingsMoney she may need next month, for a job loss, a repair or a gap in Eli's care, should not sit in a speculative, illiquid investment the SEC says may have to be held "for an indefinite period."
Small, long-term, optionalRight. Her income is under $124,000, so Maya's legal limit is the greater of $2,500 or 5% of the greater of income and net worth, about $400,000: $20,000. The legal limit is a ceiling, not a recommendation; $20,000 would be half of her taxable account.
The principle: match the investment's lockup to the money's deadline.
Scenario 3 · The Harpers

A former colleague invites the Harpers, who qualify as accredited investors, into a $100,000 apartment syndication with an 8% preferred return and a 30% promote. What should they check first?

Diligence firstRight. The waterfall calculator shows that at a 6% property return they would net about 4.2% a year, with no promote paid and $9,500 of fees still collected. Floating-rate debt and a looming loan maturity are the most common ways such deals fail.
Trust the projectionThe preferred return is a place in line, not a guarantee. And as limited partners at their income, any tax losses will be passive and suspended.
The principle: in a private deal, the sponsor is the investment; judge them as carefully as the building.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

You receive $10,000 of qualified REIT dividends in a taxable account and are in the 24% bracket. Ignoring NIIT and the taxable-income cap, what is the federal tax, in dollars?

The §199A deduction removes 20%: $10,000 × 0.8 = $8,000 taxable × 24% = $1,920, an effective 19.2%.
Exercise 2

Maya, a non-accredited investor, has $92,000 of income and $400,000 of net worth excluding her home. What is the most she can put into Regulation Crowdfunding offerings in 12 months, in dollars?

Income is below $124,000, so the limit is the greater of $2,500 or 5% of the greater of income and net worth: 5% × $400,000 = $20,000.
Exercise 3

You invested $100,000 in a syndication with an 8% compounded preferred return and a 20% promote. After 5 years, your share of the sale proceeds before the split is $200,000. How much do you receive, to the nearest dollar?

Preferred amount: $100,000 × 1.085 = $146,933. Excess: $200,000 − $146,933 = $53,067, of which you keep 80%, $42,454. Total $189,387.
Exercise 4

A product takes 10% of your purchase in upfront fees. If what remains grows 6% a year, how many years until you are back to your original amount?

0.9 × 1.06n = 1, so n = ln(1.111) ÷ ln(1.06) = 0.1054 ÷ 0.0583 = 1.81 years.
