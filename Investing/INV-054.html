International Investing and Currency

About a third of the world's stock market value sits outside the United States. For fifteen years that third has mostly lagged, and many American investors have concluded it is not worth owning. This module looks at the longer record, shows how exchange rates can add or subtract ten points in a year, and helps you decide how much to hold abroad and whether to hedge.

The question this module answersShould my portfolio own stocks outside the US, how much, and what does the currency do to my returns?

Start here

You already own a global life

Your phone may be assembled in Asia from chips made in Taiwan, your car may come from Japan or Germany, and your coffee from Colombia. The companies behind those products trade on stock markets around the world, and together they make up about a third of global stock market value.

Many Americans hold only US stocks, a tilt toward home known as home bias, and for the past fifteen years that has worked very well. The honest case for looking abroad is not that international stocks will beat US stocks; nobody knows that. It is that no country leads forever, that the US has had long stretches of lagging, and that owning the whole world removes one big bet you may not have meant to make. Figure 54.1 shows how a widely used global stock index, the MSCI All Country World Index (ACWI), divides up today.

Weights and labels

How the world's stock market is divided

Market-cap weighting
What it is
Giving each company (or country) a weight in an index equal to its share of the total market value of all the companies included.
Used for
Building indexes that represent "the market" as a whole, which every investor together must own.
What it means for you
A total-world index fund holds the US at about 64% because US companies are about 64% of the value. It is a neutral starting point, not a forecast.
Developed markets and emerging markets
What it is
Index providers classify countries by economic development and market accessibility. MSCI's developed markets include the US, Japan, the UK, Canada, most of Western Europe, Australia and others (23 in all); its 24 emerging markets include China, India, Taiwan, Korea, Brazil and Saudi Arabia.
Used for
Grouping countries with similar risks, and building international funds ("developed ex-US," "emerging markets," "total international").
What it means for you
Funds labeled "total international" generally hold both; check a fund's documents for its emerging-market share.
MSCI ACWIAll Country World Index
What it is
A widely used global stock index covering large and mid-cap companies in developed and emerging markets: 2,458 companies on August 31, 2026.
Used for
Measuring global stock performance and benchmarking global funds.
What it means for you
Its country weights are the reference point for asking whether your own portfolio is tilted toward home.

The global market is also concentrated at the top. The ten largest ACWI companies made up 24.26% of the index in August 2026; nine were American and one, Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing, was Taiwanese. Information technology was 31.2% of the index. Because the largest US companies weigh so heavily, adding international stocks also changes a portfolio's company and industry mix, not just its countries.

Going furtherWhy a US company's global sales are not the same as international stocks

Many large US companies earn much of their revenue abroad, and people sometimes conclude that owning them is "international enough." But their stocks trade in dollars, are valued by US markets, and tend to move with other US stocks. The historical data below show that US and developed-market stocks had a correlation of about 0.74 over 1991–2025: related, but far from identical.

Over the full 35 years, the US won decisively: 11.4% a year against 6.7% for developed markets outside the US. That is the record an investor who says "just own the US" is pointing to, and it is real. But it rests heavily on two periods, the 1990s and the 2010s. In the 2000s, a US-only investor earned essentially nothing for ten years while emerging markets grew more than 10% a year.

With the default settings (40% of stocks international, a quarter of that in emerging markets, 1991–2025), the mix grew 10.1% a year against 11.4% for the US alone, and its typical yearly swing was almost identical (17.5% versus 17.7%). Over this particular window, international diversification cost return without reducing volatility much. Change the first year to 2000 and the US lead shrinks from 1.4 to 0.7 points a year (8.3% for the US versus 7.6% for the mix). The lesson is not that one answer is right, but that the answer has depended heavily on the starting date — which no one gets to choose in advance.

Going furtherWhy the US did so well: earnings, valuations and the dollar

A country's stock return can be split into earnings growth, dividends, change in valuation (the price investors pay per dollar of earnings) and, for a foreign investor, currency. Part of the US lead since 2010 came from rising valuations: Robert Shiller's cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio (CAPE) for US stocks rose from 22.4 in December 2010 to 39.6 in December 2025, and in August 2026 the developed-markets index, heavily weighted by the US, traded at a P/E of 23.2 against about 15 for emerging markets. Valuation gains cannot repeat indefinitely. Part also came from a strong dollar — a euro bought $1.33 at the end of 2010 and $1.04 at the end of 2024 — which reduced foreign returns for US investors; that part reversed sharply in 2025.

The second return

How exchange rates change what you earn

Exchange rate
What it is
The price of one currency in another: for example, 1.1736 US dollars per euro, or 156.80 yen per dollar, the Federal Reserve's H.10 rates for the last business day of 2025.
Used for
Converting prices, incomes and investment values between countries.
What it means for you
When you own a foreign stock, you own the stock and the currency it is priced in.
Currency risk
What it is
The chance that exchange-rate moves change the dollar value of a foreign investment, whatever the investment itself does.
Used for
Understanding why foreign returns in dollars differ from the same returns in local currency.
What it means for you
A strong dollar hurts your foreign holdings; a weak dollar helps them.
Return in dollars = (1 + local return) × (1 + change in the foreign currency's dollar value) − 1

Suppose a European stock index rises 10% in euros, and the euro falls 10% against the dollar. In dollars you earn 1.10 × 0.90 − 1 = −1.0%, not zero, because the currency loss applies to your gain too. The calculator below lets you try your own numbers.

Practitioner depthWhy currency matters less for stocks than for bonds

A foreign bond fund might swing 5% in a year in local currency; a currency move of 10% would dominate it, turning a safe asset into a risky one. That is why many funds hedge foreign bonds: Vanguard's target-date funds, for example, hold foreign bonds "hedged by the advisor to minimize foreign currency exposure," according to their prospectus. Stocks already swing 15–20% a year, so the currency adds a smaller share of the total risk, and it sometimes offsets stock moves. That is one reason many international stock funds are left unhedged.

Removing the currency bet

What hedging does, and what it costs

Currency hedging
What it is
Using forward contracts to lock in today's exchange rate for a future date, so that the investment's dollar value depends only on its local-currency return.
Used for
Removing currency swings from foreign bonds or stocks. Some funds offer "hedged" and "unhedged" versions of the same portfolio.
What it means for you
A hedge swaps the currency's unpredictable move for a predictable gain or cost equal, roughly, to the difference in short-term interest rates between the two countries.
Covered interest parity
What it is
The principle that forward exchange rates adjust so that you cannot earn a riskless profit by borrowing in one currency and lending in another with the exchange rate locked in.
Used for
Explaining the cost of hedging: if US short-term rates are higher than foreign rates, a US investor hedging foreign assets earns roughly the difference; if lower, pays it.
What it means for you
Hedging is not free insurance; it has a known carry that changes with interest rates in each country.
Hedged return ≈ (1 + local return) × (1 + US short-term rate − foreign short-term rate) − 1

Should you hedge? For international bonds, hedging is common because currency swings would otherwise swamp the bonds' modest returns. For international stocks, reasonable investors differ. Hedging reduces short-term volatility, but it adds cost and complexity, and an unhedged foreign stock holding gives you some protection if the dollar weakens — which is exactly the environment in which a US-dollar-based retiree's purchasing power for imported goods would suffer. Vanguard's target-date funds, for example, hedge their foreign bonds back to the dollar.

Going furtherHedge ratios

A fund can hedge part of its currency exposure. A 50% hedge ratio removes half the currency effect. The calculator's "share hedged" slider shows how the return changes as you move from 0% to 100%. Choosing a middle ratio is a way of avoiding the regret of being entirely right or entirely wrong about the dollar.

How much abroad?

Home bias, emerging markets and foreign taxes

Home bias
What it is
Holding more of your own country's stocks than its share of the world market.
Used for
Measuring how concentrated a portfolio is in one country relative to the world market.
What it means for you
A US investor with 100% US stocks is making a 64%-to-100% bet on one country. That can be a choice, but it should not be an accident.

There is no single "right" amount. The market-weight answer is about 36% of stocks abroad. Many US investors choose less, reasoning that they spend in dollars, that foreign stocks can carry extra costs and taxes, and that US companies earn money globally. Others choose market weight, reasoning that they cannot know which country will lead. The two target-date funds above land at about 28% and 40%. What matters most is picking a share you will keep through a decade when the other side is winning.

Emerging marketshigher risk, different engines
What it is
Stocks of countries such as China, India, Taiwan, Korea and Brazil. They have produced some of the best and worst years in global markets.
Used for
Adding exposure to faster-growing economies and to companies absent from developed indexes.
What it means for you
The MSCI Emerging Markets Index returned +37.3% in 2017, −14.6% in 2018 and +33.6% in 2025; its largest decline since 2000 was 65% (October 2007 to October 2008). Small allocations go a long way.
Foreign tax credit
What it is
A US tax credit for qualifying foreign income taxes paid on income that is also subject to US tax, such as tax withheld by foreign governments on dividends. You may instead take the foreign tax as an itemized deduction.
Used for
Avoiding double taxation of the same income. The IRS says taking the credit is usually more advantageous than the deduction; individuals claim it on Form 1116.
What it means for you
Funds that hold foreign stocks may pass the foreign taxes they paid through to shareholders. Because the credit applies to income you pay US tax on, foreign taxes withheld inside an IRA or 401(k) generally cannot be recovered this way — one reason some investors hold international funds in taxable accounts (INV-036).

Real history

Three periods that shaped opinions

Case study 1The US "lost decade," 2000–2009
What happened
From the start of 2000 to the end of 2009, the US total market returned −0.4% a year in dollars, while developed markets outside the US returned 2.6% a year and emerging markets 10.4%. From 2002 through 2007 alone, developed ex-US stocks gained 158% and US stocks 51%, helped by a euro that rose 64% against the dollar between the end of 2001 and the end of 2007.
What it teaches
A decade is long enough to test anyone's patience. Investors in 2009 who concluded "international always wins" were as wrong about the 2010s as investors in 2025 may be to conclude the opposite.
Source
Computed from Kenneth R. French Data Library; Federal Reserve Board H.10 exchange rates.
Case study 2US dominance, 2010–2024
What happened
US stocks grew 13.6% a year in the 2010s against 6.3% for developed ex-US and 4.8% for emerging markets, and led again in 2023 (26.7% versus 16.1%) and 2024 (25.0% versus 3.6%). The yen went from 103 to 157 per dollar between the end of 2020 and the end of 2024, cutting a US investor's return on Japanese stocks by about a third in currency alone.
What it teaches
Much of the gap came from rising US valuations and a strong dollar, two sources that cannot rise forever. By August 2026, the US made up about 64% of the MSCI ACWI.
Source
Computed from French Data Library; Federal Reserve H.10; MSCI ACWI factsheet.
Case study 32025: the reversal nobody scheduled
What happened
In 2025, developed markets outside the US returned 31.9% in dollars and emerging markets 29.6%, against 17.6% for the US total market. The euro rose from $1.035 to $1.174 (about 13%) over the year, adding directly to European returns for US investors. The MSCI Emerging Markets Index returned 33.6%.
What it teaches
Leadership can change abruptly, and currency is often part of the change. Investors who had abandoned international stocks after years of lagging missed one of their best years in a decade.
Source
Computed from French Data Library; Federal Reserve H.10; MSCI ACWI factsheet.

Decisions

International choices in six different household contexts

Scenario 1 · Jordan

Jordan notices that about 40% of the stocks in her target-date fund are international, which have lagged for years. Should she switch to a US-only fund?

US onlyA bet that the last 15 years repeat for her next 40. The 2000s show it can go the other way for a decade; so did 2025.
Keep the fundRight for most investors. Its roughly 40% international share is close to world weights and removes the single-country bet. Switching after a bad stretch is performance chasing.
All internationalThe mirror-image mistake: a big bet against the country whose currency she spends.
The principle: choose an international share you can hold through a decade of lagging, then leave it alone.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers

The Harpers' $220,000 taxable account holds 25 US technology stocks. As they diversify, their advisor suggests adding an international fund in the taxable account and keeping bonds in their 401(k)s. Why might that placement make sense?

Only in IRAsThere is no rule requiring that, and it may waste the foreign tax credit.
Taxable for internationalA reasonable asset-location choice (INV-036). It also reduces their concentration in one country and one sector at the same time.
The principle: where you hold international funds can matter for taxes, not just what you hold.
Scenario 3 · Ruth

Ruth's balanced fund offers two versions of its international bond slice: hedged and unhedged. She wants her bonds to be the calm part of her portfolio. Which fits?

UnhedgedA 10% currency move in a year could exceed several years of bond income, turning her safe asset into a risky one.
HedgedRight. That is why many funds, including Vanguard's target-date funds, hedge their foreign bonds. Her stock slice can carry currency risk instead.
The principle: hedge where currency would dominate the asset's own risk; accept it where it does not.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A foreign index rises 10% in local currency while that currency falls 10% against the dollar. What is the return in dollars, in percent?

(1 + 0.10) × (1 − 0.10) − 1 = 0.99 − 1 = −1.0%.
Exercise 2

The dollar bought 103.19 yen at the end of 2020 and 157.37 yen at the end of 2024. By what percentage did the dollar value of one yen change (one decimal)?

A yen was worth 1/103.19 dollars, then 1/157.37. Ratio: 103.19 ÷ 157.37 = 0.656, so a change of −34.4%.
Exercise 3

A fully hedged foreign stock holding earns 8% in local currency, and US short-term rates are 1 point above the foreign rate. Approximately what is the hedged return in dollars, in percent (one decimal)?

(1.08) × (1.01) − 1 = 1.0908 − 1 = 9.1%, whatever the currency does.
Exercise 4

The US was 63.59% of the MSCI ACWI on August 31, 2026. What percentage of a world-market-weight stock portfolio would be outside the US (one decimal)?

100% − 63.59% = 36.4%.
