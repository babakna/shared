Five Households, Five Plans

You have met Maya, the Riveras, Jordan, the Harpers and Ruth in every module. Here each gets a complete plan: what to do first, what to do next, the numbers behind every step, and the module that explains it. Then you build your own.

The question this module answersHow do all the pieces — saving, investing, taxes, insurance, retirement and estate planning — fit into one plan, in the right order?

Start here

A plan is an order of operations

Most people do not fail financially because they chose the wrong fund. They fail because they did the right things in the wrong order — investing before they had a cushion, paying 22% interest while earning 7%, or reaching retirement with every dollar in one tax bucket.

Every plan in this module follows the same sequence. The details change with age and family; the order rarely does. Each step names the module that teaches it, so you can go back for the full reasoning.

⚠️

How to read the plans. The households are fictional. Their numbers come from their profiles and from assumptions made in earlier modules (for example, essential spending from INV-019). Tax and benefit figures are for 2026 as verified in the modules cited. Projections use constant assumed returns and are illustrations, not predictions.

Going furtherWhy Roth first for Maya

Maya's taxable income is modest, so her marginal federal rate today is likely one of the lowest she will ever face. Paying tax now on Roth contributions, and letting decades of growth come out tax-free, usually wins when the current rate is low and future income is expected to rise (INV-031, INV-032). Her 401(k) match arrives in the pre-tax side regardless, so she ends up with both kinds of money — useful flexibility in retirement (INV-042).

Decisions

Three trade-offs that cut across households

Scenario 1 · Maya's first raise

Maya gets a $3,000 raise after her cushion is built. Where should the extra money go?

All to the loanA guaranteed 6.8% return and freedom sooner. Reasonable — but she gives up years of compounding on retirement money she can never get back.
Split itBalances a guaranteed 6.8% against long-term growth and builds the habit of raising savings with every raise. Many planners would choose this.
Spend itSome lifestyle upgrade is fine. Spending every raise is how savings rates stay flat for decades (INV-018).
The principle: direct part of every raise to savings before it becomes part of your lifestyle.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers retire at 62

In their first retirement year the Harpers have very low taxable income. What should they do with it?

Change nothingLeaves $820,000 of pre-tax money to grow into large required distributions at 73, possibly in higher brackets and with Medicare surcharges.
Fill the bracketsThe low-income years between retirement and Social Security or RMDs are the classic window for Roth conversions and gain harvesting (INV-037, INV-038). It also lowers future RMDs and can reduce Ethan's tax if he inherits.
The principle: in retirement, manage lifetime taxes, not just this year's.
Scenario 3 · Ruth and a market fall

The market falls 25% in Ruth's second year of the plan. What should she do?

Sell stocksSelling after a fall locks in losses and is exactly the sequence risk the reserve exists to prevent (INV-077).
Use the reserveRight. Two to three years of withdrawals in cash and short bonds lets her wait out most declines. A modest spending freeze, as guardrail strategies suggest, adds more safety (INV-076).
The principle: build the reserve before you need it, then use it when you do.

Apply it

Build your own plan, step by step

If you are starting out: cushion, match, habits. Your savings rate beats your investment choice for the next decade.
If you have young children: insurance and wills first; then retirement, then college.
If you depend on one income: a larger cushion and no concentration in your employer.
If retirement is under ten years away: a tax map for the gap years, a Social Security strategy and a lower-risk withdrawal reserve.
If you are retired: a guaranteed floor for essentials, a sustainable withdrawal, and a clear handoff for your heirs.

Practice

Check the plans' arithmetic

Exercise 1

Maya's essential spending is $2,737 a month. INV-019 sets her target at five months plus a $1,000 buffer. What is the target, in dollars?

5 × 2,737 = 13,685, plus 1,000 = $14,685.
Exercise 2

The Riveras hold $50,000 of cash and need a $43,800 cushion. How much can move to long-term goals, in dollars?

$9,000 + $41,000 = $50,000; $50,000 − $43,800 = $6,200.
Exercise 3

Tom's benefit at full retirement age (67) is $3,800. Delayed credits add 8% a year for three years. What is his benefit at 70, in dollars a month?

3 × 8% = 24%; $3,800 × 1.24 = $4,712.
Exercise 4

Ruth spends $60,000 a year and receives $34,800 of Social Security. What percentage of her $780,000 IRA must she withdraw?

$25,200 ÷ $780,000 = 3.23%.
