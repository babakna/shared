Six Households, Six Plans

Denise, the Riveras, Maya, the Harpers, Ruth, and Daniel and Priya Shah do not need the same plan. Here each gets a context-specific sequence: what comes first, what changes with age and family, the numbers behind each step, and the module that explains it. Then you build your own.

The question this module answersHow do all the pieces — saving, investing, taxes, insurance, retirement and estate planning — fit into one plan, in the right order?

Start here

A plan is an order of operations

Most people do not fail financially because they chose the wrong fund. They fail because they did the right things in the wrong order — investing before they had a cushion, paying 22% interest while earning 7%, or reaching retirement with every dollar in one tax bucket.

Every plan in this module follows the same sequence. The details change with age and family; the order rarely does. Each step names the module that teaches it, so you can go back for the full reasoning.

⚠️

How to read the plans. The households are fictional. Their numbers come from their profiles and from assumptions made in earlier modules (for example, essential spending from INV-019). Tax and benefit figures are for 2026 as verified in the modules cited. Projections use constant assumed returns and are illustrations, not predictions.

Denise: protect flexibility while starting early

Denise expects her professional income to rise after specialization, while Roth IRA contributions are taxed before they enter the account and qualified withdrawals are tax-free. That can make current Roth contributions attractive, but the conclusion still depends on her actual current and future tax rates. She should not sacrifice essential cash or borrow merely to reach the maximum. Once employment begins, workplace-plan matches and account eligibility change the order (INV-031, INV-032).

Going furtherWhy direct inheritance can be harmful

Assets in Eli's own name may count against means-tested benefit limits. A properly drafted third-party special needs trust can hold family money for supplemental support without making the assets Eli's directly and ordinarily without a Medicaid payback for third-party funds. The trust must be coordinated with beneficiary forms; a will alone does not control retirement accounts or life insurance. An ABLE account can serve a different, more flexible spending role. Exact drafting and benefit effects require an experienced special-needs attorney in Maya's state (INV-085, INV-087, INV-102).

Practitioner depthWhat $400,000 does and does not answer

The balance is a starting fact, not a plan. It does not reveal liquidity, tax character, embedded gains, investment risk, retirement spending, public benefits, insurance or lifetime care costs. A credible recommendation first maps those facts, then stress-tests Maya's retirement and Eli's support under long lives, early death, disability, inflation and poor early market returns. Until then, precise allocation and retirement-date claims would be false precision.

Decisions

Three trade-offs that cut across households

Scenario 1 · Denise receives extra cash

Denise has already maxed her Roth IRA and receives $3,000 that is not needed for tuition or current bills. She has no other savings. What should she do?

Excess Roth contributionThe annual limit is a legal ceiling, not a target she may exceed. An excess can trigger a 6% excise tax for each year it remains uncorrected.
Start the emergency fundRight. Her Roth IRA is retirement money, not a substitute for accessible cash. A starter reserve helps prevent a surprise from becoming high-cost debt or forcing an early Roth withdrawal.
SpeculateA long horizon does not make short-term cash expendable. She has no other savings and cannot count on a volatile asset being whole when needed.
The principle: retirement investing and emergency liquidity are different jobs; fund both before adding speculation.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers retire at 62

In their first retirement year the Harpers have very low taxable income. What should they do with it?

Change nothingLeaves $820,000 of pre-tax money to grow into large required distributions at 75, possibly in higher brackets and with Medicare surcharges.
Fill the bracketsThe low-income years between retirement and Social Security or RMDs are the classic window for Roth conversions and gain harvesting (INV-037, INV-038). It also lowers future RMDs and can reduce Ethan's tax if he inherits.
The principle: in retirement, manage lifetime taxes, not just this year's.
Scenario 3 · Ruth and a market fall

The market falls 25% in Ruth's second year of the plan. What should she do?

Sell stocksSelling after a fall locks in losses and is exactly the sequence risk the reserve exists to prevent (INV-077).
Use the reserveRight. Two to three years of withdrawals in cash and short bonds lets her wait out most declines. A modest spending freeze, as guardrail strategies suggest, adds more safety (INV-076).
The principle: build the reserve before you need it, then use it when you do.

Apply it

Build your own plan, step by step

If you are starting out: cushion, match, habits. Your savings rate beats your investment choice for the next decade.
If you have young children: insurance and wills first; then retirement, then college.
If you are a single parent, or care for a child with a disability: a six-month cushion, term life payable to a trust, a guardian nomination and a supplemental needs trust come before investment choices; after 50, add every catch-up dollar you can afford.
If retirement is under ten years away: a tax map for the gap years, a Social Security strategy and a lower-risk withdrawal reserve.
If you are retired: a guaranteed floor for essentials, a sustainable withdrawal, and a clear handoff for your heirs.
If spouses have a large age gap and a child may need support for life: use separate horizons for each obligation, coordinate Social Security family and survivor benefits, map taxable, tax-deferred and Roth assets, and make the third-party trust, beneficiary forms, caregivers and investment policy one coherent plan.

Practice

Check the plans' arithmetic

Exercise 1

If one $7,500 Roth contribution compounds for 38 years at an illustrative 7% annual return, about what is it worth, in dollars?

$7,500 × 1.0738 = about $98,095. The return is an assumption, not a promise.
Exercise 2

The Riveras hold $50,000 of cash and need a $43,800 cushion. How much can move to long-term goals, in dollars?

$9,000 + $41,000 = $50,000; $50,000 − $43,800 = $6,200.
Exercise 3

Tom's benefit at full retirement age (67) is $3,800. Delayed credits add 8% a year for three years. What is his benefit at 70, in dollars a month?

3 × 8% = 24%; $3,800 × 1.24 = $4,712.
Exercise 4

Ruth spends $60,000 a year and receives $34,800 of Social Security. What percentage of her $780,000 IRA must she withdraw?

$25,200 ÷ $780,000 = 3.23%.
Exercise 5

The Shahs have $5 million invested and 60% is in taxable brokerage accounts. How many dollars are taxable?

$5,000,000 × 0.60 = $3,000,000.
