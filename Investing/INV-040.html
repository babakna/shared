Equity Compensation

Many employers pay part of compensation in their own stock: restricted stock units, discounted purchase plans, or options. Each is taxed on its own schedule, some can trigger the alternative minimum tax, and all of them tie more of your wealth to the company that already pays your salary.

The question this module answersHow is my company stock taxed, and how much of it should I keep?

Start here

Stock pay is still pay

If your employer gave you a $10,000 cash bonus, you would owe tax on it and then decide what to do with the money. Stock compensation is the same idea with two twists: the tax arrives on a schedule set by the type of award, and the "decision" often defaults to keeping the shares.

For most employees with a small stock purchase plan, the right approach is simple: take the discount, sell the shares soon after buying them, and invest the money in a diversified portfolio. The complexity matters when stock pay is a large part of income or wealth — as it is for the illustrative employee, whose $56,000 of employer stock is 40% of $140,000 of retirement savings. None of the original five households has stock pay; the Shahs' employers and holdings inside their accounts are unspecified, so they must check rather than assume.

The most common award

Restricted stock units

Restricted stock unitRSU
What it is
A promise to deliver shares (or their cash value) once you meet a vesting condition, usually staying employed for a set time. You own nothing at grant.
Used for
Retaining employees; a large part of pay at many public companies.
What it means for you
When shares are delivered, their full market value is wages, exactly as if you had been paid cash and bought the shares. Keeping them is an investment decision.

Publication 525's general rule applies: "if you receive property for your services, you must include its FMV in your income in the year you receive the property." (FMV is fair market value.) Your cost basis in the delivered shares is that value; later changes are capital gains or losses, long-term after more than one year.

💡

The cash-bonus test. On each vesting date, ask: "If I had received this as cash, would I buy my employer's stock with it?" If not, selling the delivered shares right away costs little or no extra tax, because the basis equals the value you were just taxed on.

Going furtherRestricted stock is different

Restricted stock (sometimes called a restricted stock award) is actual shares transferred to you at grant, subject to forfeiture until they vest. Publication 525 treats it as restricted property: its value, minus anything you paid, is income when it "becomes substantially vested" — unless you make a section 83(b) election, covered in tab 5.

Buying at a discount

Employee stock purchase plans

A section 423 employee stock purchase plan lets employees buy company stock through payroll deductions, often at a discount. The Internal Revenue Code sets the limits.

Rule (26 U.S.C. §423)What it means
Price at least 85% of market valueThe discount can be up to 15%, measured against the lower of the price at the start of the offering (grant) or at purchase, if the plan has a "lookback"
$25,000 a yearNo employee may accrue the right to buy more than $25,000 of stock (valued at the grant date) per calendar year
Offering lengthAt most 27 months, or 5 years if the price is based only on the purchase-date value
Broad eligibilityGenerally open to all employees on the same terms

Qualifying versus disqualifying dispositions

No tax is due when the plan buys the shares. The tax depends on when you sell, according to Publication 525:

Qualifying disposition
What it is
A sale more than 2 years after the offering date and more than 1 year after the purchase. Ordinary income is the lesser of the discount measured at the offering date or your actual gain; the rest is long-term capital gain.
Used for
Turning part of the discount into lower-taxed long-term gain.
What it means for you
You must hold the stock one to two years, bearing the risk of a fall, to earn a tax saving that is often small.
Disqualifying disposition
What it is
Any earlier sale. Ordinary income is the purchase-date market value minus the price you paid, "even if the stock later falls" — Publication 525: "This ordinary income isn't limited to your gain from the sale." Your basis rises by that income; the rest is a capital gain or loss.
Used for
Selling promptly to lock in the discount and diversify.
What it means for you
Selling right after purchase makes almost the whole discount ordinary income, with little or no capital gain.

An example plan

Suppose an employee in Columbus, Ohio, has a plan that offers 15% off with a lookback. The stock was $50 at the start of the offering and $58 on the purchase date, so the employee paid 85% of $50: $42.50 a share for 200 shares. Selling at $58 right away creates $3,100 of ordinary income. The employee is in the 12% federal bracket (a head of household with $88,000 of income) and pays Ohio's 2.75% on income above $26,050, so the combined rate on that income is about 14.75%, while long-term gains at the margin would be taxed at up to 15% federal plus 2.75% Ohio (17.75%). At those rates, a qualifying sale at the same $58 price leaves less after tax than selling now ($11,095 versus $11,143). For this employee, holding for a qualifying disposition can only pay if the stock rises.

Going furtherWhen holding does pay, and how little

At higher rates the qualifying sale saves more, but not much. With a 37% ordinary rate and a 23.8% long-term rate, the same shares leave $10,664 after a qualifying sale versus $10,453 selling at once — a $211 advantage that disappears if the stock falls more than about 2.4% during the one-to-two-year wait. Individual stocks commonly move far more than that in a year.

Incentive stock options

Tax-favored options with an AMT catch

Incentive stock optionISO
What it is
A statutory option to buy company stock at a fixed "strike" price. No regular tax is due at grant or exercise. If you hold the shares until the later of 2 years from grant and 1 year from exercise, the whole gain over the strike price is long-term capital gain (Publication 525).
Used for
Rewarding employees, often at private or young companies. Only $100,000 of stock (valued at grant) can first become exercisable as ISOs per year; the excess is treated as non-qualified options (26 U.S.C. §422(d)).
What it means for you
The spread at exercise — the "bargain element" — is an adjustment for the alternative minimum tax in the year you exercise, if you keep the shares past year-end.

The alternative minimum tax is a parallel tax calculation with fewer deductions and its own exemption. You pay whichever is higher. For 2026 (Rev. Proc. 2025-32), the exemption is $90,100 for single and head-of-household filers and $140,200 for joint filers, reduced by 50 cents per dollar above $500,000 and $1,000,000 of alternative minimum taxable income (AMTI: taxable income with the standard deduction and certain other items added back, including the ISO spread); the rate is 26% on the first $244,500 above the exemption and 28% beyond.

AMT owed = max(0, 26% × first $244,500 of (AMTI − exemption) + 28% × the rest − regular tax)

At the defaults — a single filer with $150,000 of wages exercising 2,000 options with a $10 strike when the stock is $40 — the $60,000 spread creates about $6,440 of AMT, payable in cash even though no shares were sold. The same employee could exercise about $35,200 of spread in 2026 before any AMT applied. Publication 525 notes that your AMT basis in the shares rises by the adjustment, and the AMT paid on this timing item generally becomes a credit against regular tax in later years.

Going furtherDisqualifying ISO sales

Selling ISO shares before the holding periods end turns the spread at exercise (limited to your actual gain) into ordinary income, reported as wages; any further gain is capital gain. Publication 525's example: an ISO granted March 12, 2023 at $10, exercised January 7, 2024 at $12, sold January 27, 2025 at $15. Because two years had not passed since grant, $200 of the $500 gain on 100 shares is wages and $300 is capital gain. A disqualifying sale in the same year as exercise also removes the AMT adjustment.

Practitioner depthPlanning ISO exercises

Common approaches: exercise each year only up to the AMT-free amount; exercise early in the year so a disqualifying sale is still possible before December 31 if the price falls; or exercise and sell immediately ("cashless"), which is simply ordinary income. The worst outcome is exercising a large block, holding past year-end, and watching the price fall: the AMT is based on the value at exercise.

Non-qualified options and early elections

NSOs, and choosing to be taxed early

Non-qualified stock optionNSO or NQSO
What it is
Any option that does not meet the ISO or ESPP rules. When you exercise an option that had no readily determinable value at grant (the usual case), the spread is compensation income, reported in box 12 of Form W-2 with code V (Publication 525).
Used for
Employee and director pay; any option granted to non-employees.
What it means for you
Exercising 1,000 NSOs with a $20 strike when the stock is $50 creates $30,000 of wages. Your basis becomes $50 a share; any later gain or loss is capital.
Section 83(b) election
What it is
A choice to include restricted property in income when it is transferred to you rather than when it vests. It must be filed with the IRS "no later than 30 days after the date the property was transferred," and cannot be revoked without IRS consent (Publication 525). It cannot be made for stock options.
Used for
Restricted stock grants whose value is low now and expected to rise, common at early-stage companies.
What it means for you
You pay tax on today's small value; later growth is capital gain. If you forfeit the shares, you cannot deduct the tax you paid, only any amount you paid for the shares minus what you got back.
Going furtherWhen an 83(b) election is a mistake

If the company fails or you leave before vesting, you paid tax on value you never keep. The election makes most sense when the income at transfer is small (the shares are cheap or you paid close to full value) and you are confident you will stay. It is a one-way door with a 30-day deadline.

The real risk

Your job and your savings in one company

Taxes are the smaller issue with equity pay. The larger one is concentration. INV-004 showed that most individual stocks lag Treasury bills over their lifetimes, and that Enron employees lost jobs and savings together. Equity pay builds exactly that exposure a little at a time.

Why people hold too much

Shlomo Benartzi studied large 401(k) plans and found that "about a third of the assets" were in company stock. Employees of firms with the worst stock performance over the previous ten years put 10.37% of discretionary contributions into company stock; employees of the best performers put 39.70% — yet those allocations "do not predict future performance." Past gains make concentration feel safe.

Ways to reduce it without a tax shock

  1. Sell ESPP shares soon after each purchase and RSU shares at vesting, when little or no extra tax is due.
  2. Sell high-basis lots first, using specific identification (INV-035), and harvest losing lots, avoiding purchases in the 61-day wash-sale window (INV-037).
  3. Spread large sales over several tax years to stay in lower brackets, and use low-income years for 0% gains.
  4. Give the lowest-basis shares held more than a year to charity or a donor-advised fund (INV-039).
  5. Write a rule and follow it, for example "sell every purchase within a month; never above 10% of investments." A rule removes the temptation to wait for a better price.
Going furtherHow employees value company stock

Benartzi, Thaler, Utkus and Sunstein (2007) estimated that "for workers who expect to work for a company for many years, a dollar of company stock can be valued at less than 50 cents after accounting for risk," and found evidence that employees underestimate that risk. The discount you receive in an ESPP rarely makes up for holding the stock long term.

Evidence and law

Three lessons from research and Congress

Case study 1Excessive extrapolation
What happened
Benartzi found that about a third of assets in large retirement plans, and about a quarter of discretionary contributions, were in company stock, with allocations rising after strong past performance and no ability to predict future returns.
What it teaches
The feeling "my company's stock has done well, so it is safe" is a documented bias, not an investment thesis.
Source
Benartzi, S. (2001). Excessive extrapolation and the allocation of 401(k) accounts to company stock. The Journal of Finance, 56(5), 1747–1764.
Case study 2What a dollar of company stock is worth to its employee
What happened
Some 11 million 401(k) participants had more than 20 percent of their retirement savings in their employer's stock. The authors estimated that for long-tenured workers, a dollar of company stock can be worth less than 50 cents after accounting for risk, because it is undiversified and tied to the worker's own job.
What it teaches
Equity compensation is worth less to you than its market value suggests unless you diversify it. Selling promptly converts it into full-value money.
Source
Benartzi, S., Thaler, R. H., Utkus, S. P., & Sunstein, C. R. (2007). The law and economics of company stock in 401(k) plans. The Journal of Law and Economics, 50(1), 45–79.
Case study 32006: Congress and stranded AMT credits
What happened
AMT paid on timing items, such as an ISO adjustment, generally becomes a credit usable only when regular tax exceeds the tentative minimum tax, which can take many years. The Tax Relief and Health Care Act of 2006 let individuals with "long-term unused" minimum tax credits recover part of them as a refundable credit each year: the greater of 20% of the credit or the lesser of $5,000 and the credit, phased out at higher incomes, for years before 2013.
What it teaches
AMT from exercising and holding ISOs is real cash out of pocket, and recovering it can be slow enough that Congress once stepped in. Size ISO exercises so the AMT is manageable if the stock falls.
Source
Public Law 109-432, division A, section 402 (2006), adding IRC §53(e); IRS Publication 525 on the AMT adjustment and AMT basis.

Decisions

Keep, sell or exercise?

Scenario 1 · The example employee

The employee's plan buys shares twice a year. They have kept every purchase for years, and the stock has risen to 40% of their savings. Their child starts college in three years. What policy should they adopt?

Hold for qualifying salesIn this employee's bracket a qualifying sale saves nothing at an unchanged price, and every lot held adds to a 40% concentration in their employer.
Buy, sell, diversifyRight. The 15% discount with a lookback is valuable when shares are sold promptly: it is income that carries little extra risk. Older lots can be trimmed over two or three tax years; proceeds can fund the college account or a diversified fund.
Stop participatingGives up a discount that, sold promptly, is one of the better deals in employee benefits. The problem is holding, not buying.
The principle: take the discount, not the concentration.
Scenario 2 · An illustrative engineer with ISOs

An engineer (a fictional example), single, earns $150,000 and holds 10,000 vested ISOs with a $10 strike; the shares trade at $40. She wants to exercise all of them this year and hold for long-term treatment. What should she consider?

Exercise all and holdA $300,000 spread would create a large AMT bill due in cash next April. If the stock fell by then, she would owe tax on value she no longer has.
Stage the exercisesRight. Using the calculator's AMT-free amount each year spreads the tax, and exercising early in the year keeps open the option of a same-year disqualifying sale if the price drops.
The principle: size ISO exercises to the AMT you can afford to lose.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

An ESPP offers 15% off the lower of the offering-date price ($50) and the purchase-date price ($58). What price per share does the employee pay, in dollars?

The lower price is $50; 0.85 × $50 = $42.50.
Exercise 2

The employee sells the 200 shares immediately at $58 (a disqualifying disposition). How much ordinary income is reported, in dollars?

Purchase-date value minus price paid: ($58 − $42.50) × 200 = $3,100; no capital gain because the sale price equals the purchase-date value.
Exercise 3

You exercise 1,000 non-qualified stock options with a $20 strike price when the stock is $50. How much wage income results, in dollars?

Spread = ($50 − $20) × 1,000 = $30,000, reported on Form W-2 (box 12, code V).
Exercise 4

$40,000 of RSUs vest; the employer withholds 22% federal. The employee's marginal federal rate is 32%. How much more federal tax will they owe on that income, in dollars?

$40,000 × 0.32 = $12,800 owed; $8,800 withheld; shortfall $4,000.
