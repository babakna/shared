Windfalls and Inheritance

An inheritance, a legal settlement, a business sale, a large bonus or a winning ticket can change a household's finances in a single day. The research on what people actually do with sudden money is sobering. This module sets out a calm order of operations: pause, understand the tax, clear the expensive debt, then invest on purpose.

The question this module answersA large sum just arrived. What should I do first, what should I never do, and how do I make it last?

Start here

Most people will receive a large sum at some point

You already know how to handle a normal paycheck: bills first, then savings, then everything else. A windfall breaks that routine because it is large, rare and often arrives with grief, excitement or pressure from other people. The ordinary rules still work. The danger is making big, permanent decisions in the first few weeks.

Windfalls are not rare. Jay Zagorsky of Ohio State studied the National Longitudinal Survey of Youth 1979, which has followed the same Americans for decades, and found that about one-fifth of families receive an inheritance and about one-seventh expect one. He also found that roughly half of all inherited money was saved; the other half was spent or lost investing. That second half is what this module is about.

The good news is that almost every windfall mistake is avoidable with a sequence. Figure 99.1 lays it out; the rest of the module fills in each step with real rules and numbers for 2026.

What this module covers

  1. The financial pause: what to do, and not do, in the first 90 days.
  2. Taxes: why an inheritance, a gift and a lottery prize are taxed completely differently.
  3. Lottery cash option or annuity, decided with present value.
  4. Paying off debt versus investing the money.
  5. All at once or in stages: what 98 years of data say.
  6. Sudden-wealth pitfalls, and what careful research found about windfall recipients.

Step 1

The financial pause

Planners often call the first stage a financial pause: a deliberate period, usually one to three months and longer after a death, when the money sits somewhere safe and nothing irreversible happens. It is not doing nothing. It is doing the small, reversible things first so the big decisions are made with full information.

Windfallalso called sudden money
What it is
A large sum received outside normal income: an inheritance, a life insurance payout, a gift, a legal settlement, the sale of a business or home, a large bonus or equity payout, or gambling and lottery winnings.
Used for
Planning language that groups very different events that share one feature: a large amount arrives at once, often with emotion attached.
What it means for you
The source decides the tax, but the order of decisions is the same for all of them. "Large" is relative: $20,000 is a windfall to someone with $3,000 in savings.
Financial pause
What it is
A planned waiting period after a windfall during which the money is held in safe, liquid, insured accounts and no major, hard-to-reverse decisions are made.
Used for
Separating the emotional shock from the financial decisions, gathering facts (taxes, debts, the estate's timeline) and resisting pressure from salespeople and relatives.
What it means for you
A few months of low interest costs very little. A rushed purchase, loan to a relative or high-commission product can cost a large share of the windfall and cannot be undone.

Where to park the money depends on its size. Up to $250,000 fits within FDIC insurance at one bank in one ownership category; larger sums can be spread across banks or ownership categories, or held in short-term Treasury bills or a Treasury money market fund at a brokerage (INV-008 compares these). The goal during the pause is safety and access, not return.

Going furtherInheritances take time anyway

An inheritance that passes through probate (INV-086) is distributed only after the court process and the executor's work are done, and the executor may hold back money for final taxes and bills. Assets that pass by beneficiary designation (life insurance, retirement accounts, payable-on-death accounts) skip probate and usually arrive sooner. Either way, the executor's timeline is a natural pause; use it to plan rather than to pre-spend money that has not arrived.

Practitioner depthChoosing help without buying a product

A windfall attracts pitches. The cleanest structure is advice paid for directly — hourly, a flat project fee or a retainer — from a fiduciary, plus a certified public accountant (CPA) for the tax questions and an estate attorney if trusts or an estate are involved. Check any adviser on BrokerCheck, run by the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA), and the Securities and Exchange Commission's (SEC's) adviser search before signing anything (INV-103). Be most skeptical of complex products that pay large commissions: variable and indexed annuities, permanent life insurance sold as an investment, and private placements.

Step 2

An inheritance, a gift and a jackpot are taxed completely differently

People often assume a windfall is "taxable" or "tax-free" as a whole. In fact the federal rules depend entirely on where the money came from. Figure 99.3 sets out the three main cases.

Inheritances

IRS Publication 525 is direct: in most cases, property you receive as a gift, bequest or inheritance is not included in your income. What it earns afterward (interest, dividends, rent) is taxable to you. The large exception is an inherited IRA or pension: withdrawals from a traditional account are taxable income to the heir, and most non-spouse heirs must empty the account within 10 years (INV-091).

The federal estate tax is paid by the estate, not the heir, and for 2026 applies only to estates above the $15,000,000 basic exclusion amount set by P.L. 119-21 (INV-088). A handful of states tax the heir instead, through an inheritance tax. Pennsylvania, where Ruth lives, is one: 0% to a surviving spouse, 4.5% to children and other lineal descendants, 12% to siblings and 15% to other heirs, with a 5% discount if paid within three months of death (Pennsylvania Department of Revenue).

Step-up versus carryover basis
What it is
Inherited property generally takes a new cost basis equal to its fair market value at the owner's death: a step-up in basis. A gift from a living person keeps the giver's old basis: a carryover basis (IRS Publication 551).
Used for
Measuring the capital gain when you later sell.
What it means for you
Shares bought for $20,000 and worth $50,000: inherited and sold for $55,000, the taxable gain is $5,000. Received as a gift and sold for $55,000, it is $35,000.

Gifts

A gift is not income to the person receiving it. The IRS gift tax FAQs state that the donor is generally responsible for any gift tax. For 2026 the first $19,000 given to any one person in the year is excluded entirely (the annual gift exclusion); larger gifts require the donor to file Form 709 and use part of the same $15,000,000 lifetime exclusion, so actual tax is rare.

Lottery and gambling winnings

These are the opposite: fully taxable as ordinary income in the year received (IRS Tax Topic 419). For 2026, payers withhold 24% federal tax on lottery prizes of more than $5,000 (Instructions for Forms W-2G and 5754), but a large prize is taxed mostly at the top rate of 37%, which applies to single filers' taxable income above $640,600 and joint filers' above $768,700 in 2026. The withholding is therefore usually far too low. P.L. 119-21 also changed gambling losses: starting in 2026 only 90% of losses are deductible, and only up to winnings, for those who itemize.

Going furtherSettlements, life insurance and bonuses

Life insurance proceeds paid because of the insured person's death generally are not taxable income to the beneficiary, though interest paid on proceeds held or paid in installments is (IRS Publication 525). A bonus or severance is wages, taxed like any paycheck. Legal settlements depend on what they replace: compensatory damages for personal physical injury or sickness are excluded, while interest on an award, lost wages and, in most cases, punitive damages are ordinary income (IRS Publication 525). Ask a CPA before spending a settlement.

Lottery decisions

Cash now or payments for 30 years: decide with present value

Large jackpots are usually advertised as the total of a stream of yearly payments. The winner can instead take a smaller cash option now. Comparing a smaller amount today with a larger total spread over decades is exactly the problem present value solves (INV-002).

Present value of a growing annuity
What it is
What a series of future payments is worth today, found by discounting each payment by how long you must wait for it and what the money could otherwise earn.
Used for
Comparing a lump sum with payments: lottery options, pension lump sums (INV-080), structured settlements and severance paid over time.
What it means for you
The advertised total is not what the payments are worth. At any realistic discount rate, 30 years of payments are worth far less than their sum.
PV = Σk=0n−1 P(1 + g)k ÷ (1 + r)k    with first payment P = J · g ÷ [(1 + g)n − 1]

Here J is the advertised total, n the number of payments, g the yearly increase and r the discount rate. Take an illustrative $100 million jackpot paid in 30 yearly payments, each 5% larger than the last, with the first paid now. The first payment is $1,505,144 and the last is about $6.2 million. At a 4.5% discount rate the stream is worth about $48.4 million today; at 6%, about $39.5 million. If the cash option is $45 million, the two are equal at a discount rate of about 5.0%.

Going furtherThe case for the annuity is behavioral, not mathematical

If you could reliably earn more than the break-even rate, the cash option wins on paper. But the evidence in the Case studies tab shows many windfall recipients spend quickly. An annuity is a spending limit that renews every year: a mistake in year 2 does not consume year 20's money. Winners who doubt their own discipline, or who expect pressure from others, may reasonably value that protection above a few points of return.

Practitioner depthTaxes, estate and the discount rate

Both options are ordinary income when received. The cash option pushes almost everything into the 37% bracket in one year; annuity payments of $1.5–6.2 million are also largely taxed at 37%, so spreading saves less than people expect for jackpots this size. For smaller prizes spreading can keep income in lower brackets. The right discount rate is the after-tax return you would realistically earn on a diversified portfolio of similar risk, not a stock market best case.

Step 3

Paying off debt is an investment with a guaranteed return

Paying off a loan that charges 6.8% earns exactly 6.8% a year, with no risk and no tax. Investing the same money in stocks might earn more, or less. That is the whole trade-off, and it gives a simple priority order.

With the defaults — Denise's $28,000 of student loans at 6.8% over 10 years, a $50,000 windfall and a 6% expected after-tax return — paying off first ends ahead by about $2,200 after 10 years, and without any market risk. Raise the expected return to 8% and investing ends ahead by about $2,400, but only if that return actually arrives. When the two are close, the guaranteed option usually deserves the tie.

Going furtherWhen not to pay off debt

Keep cheap, fixed-rate debt: the Riveras' 3.1% mortgage costs less than safe Treasury bills have recently paid (INV-008), so prepaying it from a windfall gives up liquidity for a low return. Also keep enough cash: money sent to a lender cannot be borrowed back easily in a job loss. And federal student loans carry protections, such as income-driven repayment and forgiveness programs, that a payoff ends; check what you would give up first (INV-020).

Step 4

Lump sum or staged investing: the recap

Once the plan is written, a large sum can go into the market all at once (lump-sum investing) or in pieces over months or years (staged investing, a form of dollar-cost averaging). INV-051 covers the evidence in depth; this is the short version for a windfall.

Vanguard's 2023 study, "Cost averaging: Invest now or temporarily hold your cash?", compared investing a lump sum immediately with splitting it into three monthly pieces, using global stock returns from 1976 to 2022. The lump sum ended the year ahead about 68% of the time. Staging was still better than staying in cash, which it beat 69% of the time.

The calculator below runs the same test on US data back to 1928 with longer staging periods. Staging a sum into stocks over three years, with the waiting cash in Treasury bills, the lump sum was ahead after three years in 71 of 96 start years (74%). The lump sum's worst results came from starting just before a crash: 1930 (39% behind), 1931 (38%), 1973 (26%) and 2008 (23%).

💡

Staging is regret insurance, and it has a price. If investing everything at once would keep you awake, or make you sell in the first downturn, a fixed schedule — for example equal amounts on set dates over 6 to 12 months, written down in advance — is a reasonable compromise. What does not work is waiting for a "better time" with no schedule.

What goes wrong

Six ways windfalls disappear

Research on lottery winners is valuable because winning is random: large and small winners, or people who win in different years, are otherwise similar, so differences afterward are caused by the money. A large study of US lottery winners by Mikhail Golosov and co-authors, published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2024, found that each extra dollar of unearned income reduced pre-tax earnings by about 50 cents and increased consumption by about 60 cents.

  1. Lifestyle creep. Recurring costs — a bigger house, a second car, private school — turn a one-time sum into a permanent obligation. A useful test: could this cost be paid from the income the windfall will sustainably produce (roughly 3–4% a year, INV-076), rather than from the principal?
  2. Loans and gifts to family and friends. Decide a total amount for generosity, in writing, before anyone asks. Treat any "loan" as a gift you may never see again.
  3. Quitting work too soon. The Golosov results show earnings fall. Leaving a career is hard to reverse; run the numbers in INV-072 first.
  4. Concentration. An inheritance of one company's stock, or a business-sale payout in employer stock, carries the single-stock risk of INV-004. The step-up in basis often makes diversifying soon after death nearly tax-free.
  5. Products and pitches. High-commission annuities, private deals and "exclusive" opportunities find new money quickly (INV-062).
  6. Taxes left unpaid. Spending a lottery prize or an inherited IRA withdrawal before setting aside the tax (Figure 99.4) creates debt to the IRS.
Going furtherA generosity budget

Many people find it easier to say no to requests when they can point to a decision already made: "We set aside $10,000 for family help this year and it is committed." Gifts up to $19,000 per recipient in 2026 need no gift tax return. Larger help, such as a down payment, can be structured as a documented loan or a gift; either way, decide within a budget you can afford to lose.

Real research

Three studies of what people do with sudden money

Case study 1Florida lottery winners and bankruptcy
What happened
Scott Hankins, Mark Hoekstra and Paige Marta Skiba linked Florida Lottery winners to bankruptcy records. Winners of $50,000 to $150,000 were 50 percent less likely to file for bankruptcy in the two years after winning than small winners, but were equally more likely to file three to five years afterward. The median large winner who later filed could have paid off all of their unsecured debt, but did not.
What it teaches
A windfall that is not used to repair the household's finances can merely postpone trouble. Paying off expensive debt and building a cushion are the steps that make the effect last.
Source
Hankins, S., Hoekstra, M., & Skiba, P. M. (2011). The ticket to easy street? The financial consequences of winning the lottery. Review of Economics and Statistics, 93(3), 961–969.
Case study 2Do people save or spend their inheritances?
What happened
Jay Zagorsky followed inheritance recipients in the National Longitudinal Survey of Youth 1979. Roughly half of all money inherited was saved; the other half was spent or lost investing. About one-fifth of families received an inheritance and about one-seventh expected one.
What it teaches
Inheritances are common, and half of the value typically does not survive as wealth. Deciding in advance what the money is for — retirement, a child's education, a paid-off debt — is the best defense.
Source
Zagorsky, J. L. (2013). Do people save or spend their inheritances? Understanding what happens to inherited wealth. Journal of Family and Economic Issues, 34(1), 64–76.
Case study 3US lottery winners, work and spending
What happened
Using tax records of US lottery winners, Golosov, Graber, Mogstad and Novgorodsky estimated that an extra dollar of unearned income reduced pre-tax labor earnings by about 50 cents, reduced labor taxes by about 10 cents and raised consumption by about 60 cents, with larger earnings reductions at higher incomes.
What it teaches
Windfalls change behavior, not just balances. Plan the work decision and the spending level explicitly, rather than letting them drift.
Source
Golosov, M., Graber, M., Mogstad, M., & Novgorodsky, D. (2024). Quarterly Journal of Economics, 139(2), 1321–1395.

Decisions

Three households, three windfalls

Scenario 1 · Denise

Denise's grandmother leaves her $50,000 (an illustration for this module). She has $28,000 of student loans at 6.8% and $3,000 in savings. Friends suggest she buy a new car and invest the rest in a few tech stocks. What should she do?

Car and tech stocksTurns part of the money into a depreciating asset and the rest into a concentrated bet (INV-004). Her 6.8% debt keeps compounding against her.
Invest it allDefensible if she is confident of beating 6.8% after tax, but she would still have only $3,000 of cash. The calculator shows the two paths are close at 6% to 8% expected returns; the payoff is certain.
Pay off, cushion, RothRemoves a guaranteed 6.8% cost, gives her a real emergency fund and starts tax-free growth. The $322 a month she no longer sends to the lender can go straight into her 401(k) (INV-031).
The principle: use a windfall first to remove guaranteed costs and risks, then invest.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers

Tom's mother dies and leaves him $400,000 in a taxable brokerage account of index funds she bought decades ago. The Harpers already have a written plan. They are nervous: markets are at a record high. What should they do with the inherited account?

Wait in cashNo one can reliably time the fall (INV-052). Holding cash with no schedule is the option that performs worst on average in the Vanguard study, beating a lump sum only about 30% of the time.
Invest nowThe step-up in basis means selling soon after death creates little capital gain, so they can align the account with their plan cheaply. Historically the most likely to finish ahead.
Stage over 12 monthsA reasonable compromise if it prevents a panic sale later. Expect to give up some return on average; write the dates down so the plan does not stall.
The principle: the decision is about behavior, not forecasting. Choose the approach you will stick with, then stick with it.
Scenario 3 · Ruth

Ruth receives a letter saying she has won $2.5 million in an international lottery. To release the prize she must wire $4,800 for "taxes and fees." She does not remember entering. What should she do?

Pay the feeThis is the classic advance-fee fraud. Payment is followed by new "fees." The windfall does not exist.
Refuse and reportRight. Real US lottery winnings are paid by the lottery, with tax withheld from the prize itself. Report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and warn Anna (INV-062).
The principle: if a windfall requires you to send money first, it is not a windfall.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

Using the calculator's illustrative $100 million annuity (30 payments growing 5% a year, first payment now), what is it worth today at a 6% discount rate, in millions of dollars?

First payment P = 100M × 0.05 ÷ (1.0530 − 1) = $1,505,144. PV = Σ P × (1.05 ÷ 1.06)k for k = 0 to 29 = $39.49 million. Set the calculator's discount rate to 6% to confirm. Against a $45 million cash option, the cash wins at 6%.
Exercise 2

Anna inherits $300,000 of taxable Pennsylvania property from Ruth. The executor pays the inheritance tax within three months of death. How much is the tax, in dollars?

Lineal heir rate 4.5%: $300,000 × 0.045 = $13,500. The 5% early-payment discount: $13,500 × 0.95 = $12,825.
Exercise 3

A parent gives shares with a basis of $20,000, worth $50,000 on the day of the gift. The child later sells them for $55,000. What is the child's taxable gain, in dollars?

A gift keeps the donor's basis when the value is at or above that basis: gain = $55,000 − $20,000 = $35,000. Had the child inherited the shares at $50,000, the gain would be only $5,000.
Exercise 4

A single filer with no other income wins a $1,000,000 prize in 2026. The payer withholds 24%. Federal tax, after the $16,100 standard deduction, comes to about $320,000. About how much more is due with the return, in dollars?

Withheld: $1,000,000 × 0.24 = $240,000. Due: $320,000 − $240,000 = $80,000, plus any state tax. Paying it through an estimated payment in the quarter of the win can avoid an underpayment penalty (INV-101 explains the safe harbors).
