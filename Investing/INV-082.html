Annuities for Retirement Income

Most "annuities" sold are complex investment products. This module is about the simple kind: you give an insurer a sum of money and it pays you income for as long as you live. Understood properly, that is not an investment at all but insurance against a long life, and it can buy more safe income per dollar than any bond.

The question this module answersShould I turn part of my savings into guaranteed lifetime income, and if so, how much, what kind, and from whom?

Start here

You already own an annuity

If you receive Social Security, you already have the best annuity most Americans will ever own: income for life, adjusted every year for inflation, with benefits for a surviving spouse. Social Security benefits rose 2.8% for 2026 (Social Security Administration, SSA). A private income annuity is a smaller, privately bought version of the same idea.

The ordinary advice about annuities is to be wary, and it is mostly right: many products called annuities combine high fees, long surrender charges and sales commissions (INV-007, INV-017). But one simple kind — you pay a premium, the insurer pays you a fixed income for life, no investment choices, no account — does something nothing else can. It lets you spend more safely from the same savings, because it pools longevity risk, the risk of a long life, across thousands of buyers.

What this module covers

  1. The income annuity family: single premium immediate annuities (SPIAs), deferred income annuities (DIAs) and qualifying longevity annuity contracts (QLACs), with the 2026 QLAC limit.
  2. Mortality credits: why an annuity can pay more than a bond, explained with numbers.
  3. The income floor strategy: guaranteed income for essential bills, investments for the rest.
  4. What drives payouts today, and how to shop.
  5. Insurer financial strength and state guaranty association limits.

The family

Immediate, deferred, and the QLAC

An annuity in insurance terms is a contract with an insurance company. This module deals only with income annuities, where the contract's main purpose is to pay you a set income. Accumulation products such as variable annuities and fixed indexed annuities are covered in INV-017.

Single premium immediate annuitySPIA
What it is
One payment to an insurer, which starts paying you income within about a year, usually monthly, for life (or for two lives, as a joint annuity).
Used for
Turning savings into a paycheck now. Options include a period certain (for example, payments for at least 10 years) or a cash or installment refund to heirs; each option lowers the income.
What it means for you
The purchase is usually irreversible. You trade access to the money for income you cannot outlive. Buy only with money you will not need as a lump sum.
Deferred income annuityDIA
What it is
You pay now; income starts at a chosen later date, often 5 to 20 years out.
Used for
Insuring the far end of retirement ("longevity insurance") cheaply, because the insurer earns interest in the meantime and some buyers will not live to collect.
What it means for you
A small premium at 65 can buy a large income from 80 or 85, letting you spend the rest of your savings with less fear of running out.
Qualifying longevity annuity contractQLAC
What it is
A deferred income annuity bought inside an individual retirement account (IRA) or workplace plan that meets Internal Revenue Service (IRS) rules: income must start no later than age 85, it can have no cash surrender value, and total premiums are capped. For 2026 the cap is $210,000 (IRS Notice 2025-67). SECURE 2.0 removed the old limit of 25% of your account balance.
Used for
Longevity insurance with pre-tax money. The QLAC's value is excluded from the balance used to compute required minimum distributions until its income starts, which lowers required minimum distributions (RMDs) in the meantime (INV-081).
What it means for you
A QLAC can include a return-of-premium death benefit for heirs if you die early (Treasury final regulations, 2024); that feature lowers the income.

The engine

How an annuity can pay more than a bond

A bond, or your own savings, can only pay you back your money plus interest. An annuity pays three things: interest, a return of your own premium spread over your expected life, and a third component no individual can create alone: mortality credits.

Mortality creditsthe pooling payoff
What it is
The extra return survivors in an annuity pool receive because the money of members who die is not paid out to their heirs but stays in the pool.
Used for
Explaining why annuity payouts rise with age: the older the pool, the more members die each year, and the larger the credit to those who remain.
What it means for you
You "win" by living long and "lose" by dying early. That is exactly what insurance should do: pay most when you need it most.

A simple pool shows the mechanism. One thousand 70-year-old women each put $100,000 into a pool earning 4.5%. In the SSA's 2023 period life table, a 70-year-old woman has a 1.48% chance of dying within a year, so about 15 of the 1,000 die. The pool now holds $104.5 million to share among 985 survivors: about $106,091 each, a 6.1% return instead of 4.5%. The extra 1.6 points is the mortality credit.

Survivor's one-year return ≈ (1 + r) ÷ (1 − q) − 1, so the mortality credit ≈ (1 + r) × q ÷ (1 − q)

With q = 1.48% at 70 the credit is about 1.6% a year. At 85 (q = 7.18%) it is about 8.1%, and at 90 (q = 12.92%) about 15.5%. No bond can match that at those ages, which is why annuities are most powerful as insurance for the far end of life.

Practitioner depthHow the model prices an annuity

The fair premium for $1 a year of income paid monthly for life is the expected present value Σ tpx vt / 12 over monthly payment dates. The payout rate is (1 − loading) divided by that factor. The model uses the SSA 2023 period life table, scaled by the "buyer's mortality" slider because people who buy annuities tend to be healthier than average; insurers use their own annuitant tables. Real products also carry distribution costs, capital charges and profit, and prices change with interest rates, often weekly. Treat the output as an explanation of the price, not a price.

The strategy

Cover the essentials with guarantees; invest the rest

Income flooralso called floor-and-upside
What it is
A plan that matches essential spending — housing, food, utilities, insurance, basic health costs — with guaranteed lifetime income, and funds discretionary spending from an investment portfolio.
Used for
Making a bear market or a long life an inconvenience rather than a crisis. Floor sources: Social Security, pensions, income annuities and ladders of bonds or Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS).
What it means for you
Start by listing essential spending. Subtract Social Security and any pension. Only the gap, if any, is a candidate for an annuity or a ladder.

Switch the calculator in the previous tab to "Build an income floor" for Ruth's numbers. Suppose her essential spending is about $48,000 a year (an assumption for this example). Her $34,800 of Social Security covers 73% of it. Closing the $13,200 gap with a life annuity at 68 costs about $171,532 in this module's model, about a fifth of her $840,000 of IRA and cash savings, leaving about $668,468 invested.

Two alternatives deserve a comparison before buying an annuity. A TIPS ladder (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities maturing year by year, INV-010) provides inflation-protected income with no insurer risk, but only for a fixed number of years. Delaying Social Security, which earns delayed retirement credits, is often the cheapest inflation-adjusted annuity available: for people born in 1943 or later, benefits rise 8% for each year of delay past full retirement age, up to 70 (SSA). Babak's Retirement Income Compass compares a bond ladder, a TIPS ladder and a fixed annuity for exactly this job.

⚠️

Inflation is the annuity's weak spot. A level payment loses purchasing power: at 3% inflation, a fixed $13,200 buys what about $7,300 does today after 20 years. Some insurers offer payments that rise by a fixed percentage each year, at the cost of a lower starting income. Plan to supplement the floor over time from the portfolio.

What drives the price

Payout rates: what they depend on, and how to shop

This module does not quote current payout rates, because real quotes vary by insurer, change frequently and depend on details. What can be explained is why quotes are what they are. Five inputs set nearly every income annuity price:

  1. Age. Older buyers have shorter expected payment periods and larger mortality credits (Figure 82.3).
  2. Sex. Women live longer, so the same premium buys a woman less income: in the model, a 70-year-old man gets about $8.92 per $100 against a woman's $8.14.
  3. Interest rates. Insurers invest premiums mainly in high-quality bonds. As a reference point, the IRS 417(e) corporate bond segment rates for May 2026 were 4.42%, 5.47% and 6.31% for the short, middle and long segments. Higher yields mean higher payouts.
  4. Features. Joint life, a period certain, a refund, or yearly increases each reduce the starting income.
  5. Insurer costs and who buys. Annuity buyers live longer than average, and insurers charge for expenses and capital.

How to shop

  • Get quotes from several insurers for the same contract: same start date, same payment form, same refund option. Quotes for identical contracts can differ noticeably.
  • Compare the payout rate (yearly income ÷ premium) and the insurer's financial strength, not the salesperson's description.
  • Ask whether the income is fixed or rises, whether it can be commuted, and what happens at death.
  • Consider buying in stages over several years, which spreads the risk of buying when rates are low and lets you see how retirement feels before committing more.
Going furtherTaxes on annuity income

An annuity bought inside an IRA (a "qualified" annuity) pays fully taxable income, and its payments count toward your RMDs from that IRA. An annuity bought with after-tax money (a "non-qualified" annuity) returns part of your premium in each payment tax-free, using an exclusion ratio based on your expected return, until the premium is recovered. The details are in IRS Publication 939 and Publication 575.

Who stands behind it

Financial strength ratings and state guaranty associations

An annuity is only as good as the insurer that owes it, often for 20 or 30 years. Two protections stand behind that promise: the insurer's own capital, and, if the insurer fails, a state guaranty association.

Financial strength rating
What it is
An independent opinion of an insurer's ability to pay its policy obligations, published by rating agencies such as AM Best, S&P Global Ratings, Moody's and Fitch. Each uses its own letter scale.
Used for
Screening insurers before you buy a contract that must be honored for decades.
What it means for you
Check the rating on the agency's own site and read what the letter means on that agency's scale. Many buyers limit themselves to insurers in the top rating categories.
State guaranty association
What it is
A state-created organization, operating in all 50 states, Puerto Rico and the District of Columbia, that continues coverage or pays claims up to legal limits when a member life or health insurer fails (National Organization of Life & Health Insurance Guaranty Associations, NOLHGA).
Used for
Protecting annuity owners. For annuities, the limit applies to the present value of the benefits. Amounts above the limit become claims against the failed insurer's estate and may be paid in part.
What it means for you
Limits are per person, per failed insurer, and vary by state. Keep each insurer's annuities within your state's limit; spread larger purchases across insurers.

The guaranty association does not cover the part of a contract whose risk you bear yourself, such as a variable annuity's investment subaccounts sold by prospectus. That is one more reason the plain fixed income annuity is the right tool for a floor.

Law and illustration

Three stories about income annuities

Case study 1SECURE 2.0 expands QLACs (2022)
What happened
Rules adopted in 2014 created QLACs but capped premiums at the lesser of an indexed $125,000 or 25% of the account balance. Section 202 of SECURE 2.0 removed the 25% limit and raised the dollar limit to $200,000, indexed; the final regulations applied the higher limits even to contracts bought before December 29, 2022. For 2026 the limit is $210,000.
What it teaches
Under the old rule, someone with a $400,000 IRA could put at most $100,000 into a QLAC. Now the same person can use up to $210,000, making a meaningful longevity floor possible for middle-income savers.
Source
Treasury and IRS (2024), final regulations, Federal Register, July 19, 2024; IRS Notice 2025-67.
Case study 2Staying within a state guaranty limit (illustration based on Pennsylvania law)
The setup
A Pennsylvania resident wants to buy $400,000 of income annuities. Pennsylvania's guaranty association protects individual annuities up to $250,000 in present value per insured per insolvency.
The choice
Buying everything from one insurer leaves $150,000 of present value above the limit if that insurer fails. Buying $200,000 from each of two highly rated insurers keeps each within the limit.
What it teaches
Splitting purchases costs little and removes a tail risk. The limit is per insurer, so diversification across insurers is the practical defense.
Source
Pennsylvania Life and Health Insurance Guaranty Association, Notice of protection; NOLHGA, How you're protected. Illustrative.
Case study 3Delay as an annuity: Social Security's 8% a year (law)
What happened
For people born in 1943 or later, each year of delaying Social Security past full retirement age raises the benefit by 8% (two-thirds of 1% per month), up to age 70. The higher benefit is adjusted for inflation and, for a married couple, can become the survivor's benefit.
What it teaches
Spending savings to delay Social Security is, in effect, buying an inflation-adjusted joint annuity from the government. For many people it is a better first step than buying a private annuity; the private annuity then fills any remaining gap.
Source
Social Security Administration, Delayed retirement credits.

Decisions

Choosing guaranteed income

Scenario 1 · Ruth, 68

Ruth's essential spending is about $48,000 a year; Social Security pays $34,800. A salesperson again recommends a variable annuity with an income rider and about 2.5% in yearly charges. Her other choices: buy a simple life annuity for about $170,000 inside her IRA, or keep everything invested and withdraw as needed.

Variable annuityHigh ongoing charges reduce both growth and future income, surrender charges limit access, and the investment part is not covered by Pennsylvania's guaranty association. It solves her problem expensively (INV-007).
Simple life annuityA sound choice. About a fifth of her savings would turn her floor into a fully guaranteed one, below Pennsylvania's $250,000 limit with one insurer. Payments from an IRA annuity also count toward her future RMDs. Buying in stages reduces the risk of locking in a low rate.
No annuityWorkable: $13,200 a year is about 1.6% of $840,000, a modest withdrawal. The cost is that a long life and a bad market together could still squeeze her in her 90s, and the decision-making burden grows as she ages.
The principle: if you want a guarantee, buy the simplest product that provides it, sized to the gap in essential spending.
Scenario 2 · Tom Harper, retiring at 62–63

Tom wants more guaranteed income for himself and Rachel. He could claim Social Security at 63 and buy a joint life annuity with part of his 401(k), or live on the 401(k) for a few years and delay Social Security toward 70.

Claim and buyGives income now, but a private annuity is usually level (not inflation-adjusted) and priced with insurer costs. Claiming early also permanently lowers the survivor benefit Rachel could receive (INV-078).
Delay and bridgeUsually stronger: each year of delay past full retirement age adds 8%, inflation-adjusted, and the larger benefit can protect Rachel as a survivor. The 401(k) withdrawals in the bridge years also shrink future RMDs (INV-081).
The principle: buy the government's inflation-adjusted annuity first; buy private annuities to fill what is left.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

One thousand people each put $100,000 into a pool earning 4.5%. During the year 15 die, and their shares stay in the pool. What does each survivor have at year-end, in dollars?

The pool grows to $100 million × 1.045 = $104.5 million, shared by 985 survivors: $106,091 each, a 6.1% return, of which 1.6 points is the mortality credit.
Exercise 2

An annuity pays 7.7% of the premium each year for life. How large a premium closes a $13,200 yearly income gap, in dollars?

Premium = gap ÷ payout rate = $13,200 ÷ 0.077 = $171,429. (The calculator's slightly higher unrounded rate gives $171,532.)
Exercise 3

A Pennsylvania resident buys annuities with $400,000 of present value from a single insurer. How much is above the state guaranty association's annuity limit, in dollars?

The Pennsylvania limit for individual annuities is $250,000 of present value per insured per insolvency: $400,000 − $250,000 = $150,000 unprotected. Two insurers at $200,000 each would keep both within the limit.
Exercise 4

Ruth has a $780,000 IRA. What is the most she can put into QLACs in 2026, in dollars?

SECURE 2.0 removed the 25%-of-balance limit, so only the dollar limit applies: $210,000 for 2026 (IRS Notice 2025-67). Under the old rule, 25% of $780,000 would have been $195,000, also subject to the old dollar cap.
