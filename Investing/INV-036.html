Asset Location

Asset allocation decides what you own. Asset location decides where you keep it: in a taxable brokerage account, a tax-deferred 401(k) or individual retirement account (IRA), or a Roth account. The same portfolio, placed differently, can leave you with noticeably more after tax — without taking any extra risk.

The question this module answersI have more than one kind of account. Which investments should go in which one?

Start here

You already sort things by where they are safest

You keep your passport in a drawer and your umbrella by the door. Nothing about the passport or the umbrella changes; you simply put each where it works best. Asset location applies the same common sense to investments and the three kinds of account the tax code offers.

For most people early in their working lives, this module does not apply yet. If all your investing happens inside one 401(k), there is nothing to place: every fund enjoys the same tax treatment. That is the ordinary case, and it is fine. The idea becomes useful once you hold meaningful money in two or three kinds of account, as the Riveras, Monica, the Harpers and Ruth all do.

The core insight is simple. Different investments are taxed differently, and different accounts shield income differently. Interest from bonds is taxed every year at your full income-tax rate when held in a taxable account. Stock index funds mostly produce price growth, which is not taxed until you sell, and dividends that are often taxed at lower rates. So the shelter of a retirement account is worth more to some holdings than to others. Figure 36.1 shows the size of that difference.

The building blocks

Taxable, tax-deferred and tax-free

Every investment account in this course falls into one of three tax treatments. INV-031 to INV-035 covered each account in detail; here we only need the part that matters for placement: when the tax is paid and on what.

Taxable accounta regular brokerage or bank account
What it is
An account with no special tax status. You invest money that has already been taxed. Interest and dividends are taxed in the year they are paid; gains are taxed when you sell.
Used for
Savings beyond retirement-account limits, money you may need before retirement, emergency funds.
What it means for you
Every dollar of yearly income it produces creates a tax bill. Holdings that produce little yearly income suit it best.
Tax-deferred accounttraditional 401(k), 403(b), traditional IRA
What it is
A tax-deferred account is funded with money that was not taxed going in (a deduction or pre-tax payroll contribution). Nothing inside is taxed while it grows; every dollar withdrawn is taxed as ordinary income.
Used for
Retirement saving while your tax rate is high, with the expectation of withdrawing at a similar or lower rate.
What it means for you
Part of the balance is not really yours: it is the tax you will owe on the way out. The next paragraph shows how to count it.
Tax-free accountRoth IRA, Roth 401(k)
What it is
An account funded with money already taxed. Growth is never taxed, and qualified withdrawals are tax-free.
Used for
Long-term growth you want to keep entirely, including money you may leave to heirs.
What it means for you
Every dollar in it is a full dollar of spending power. That makes it the most valuable place for your highest-growth holdings.
After-tax value of a tax-deferred account = balance × (1 − tw), where tw is your tax rate when you withdraw

For the Harpers, whose $820,000 is in pre-tax 401(k)s, and assuming a 22% rate in retirement, that is about $639,600 of spending power. Their $1.1 million of savings is really about $919,600 after tax: $220,000 taxable + $639,600 + $60,000 Roth. That number, not the statement total, is the honest starting point for placement decisions.

Going furtherWhy the tax-deferred share "grows tax-free"

Put $100 in a traditional IRA and let it double to $200. At a 22% withdrawal rate you keep $156; had you started with your $78 share in a Roth, it would also have doubled to $156. The government's 22% share grows alongside yours and is collected at the end. So a tax-deferred account shelters the return on your share completely — provided the withdrawal rate stays the same. If your rate later is lower, the account is even better than a Roth; if it is higher, worse. INV-038 on Roth conversions builds on this.

Practitioner depthAfter-tax asset allocation

Planners who follow Reichenstein's approach restate every account in after-tax dollars before measuring the stock-bond mix. Two placements with the same pre-tax 60/40 mix can have different after-tax mixes. Using the Harpers' numbers and a 22% future rate: holding all $440,000 of bonds in the 401(k) gives an after-tax mix of about 63% stocks; holding stocks in the 401(k) and bonds in the taxable and Roth accounts gives about 56% stocks. The after-tax view says the first placement is quietly riskier. Some planners then rebalance to the same after-tax mix; many simply keep it in mind. Either way, compare placements at equal after-tax risk where you can.

Which holdings suffer most from taxes

From tax-inefficient to tax-efficient

An investment is tax-efficient if it produces little taxable income each year and most of its return as long-term growth taxed later at lower rates. It is tax-inefficient if it throws off income taxed every year at your top ordinary rate. The tax drag an investment suffers in a taxable account depends on both its return and how that return is taxed.

Qualified dividendthe lower-taxed kind
What it is
A dividend from a US company (or a qualifying foreign one) that you have held for more than 60 days during the 121-day period that begins 60 days before the ex-dividend date. It is taxed at the long-term capital-gain rates of 0%, 15% or 20% instead of ordinary rates.
Used for
Separating dividends into the kind taxed like gains (Form 1099-DIV box 1b) and the kind taxed like wages.
What it means for you
Most dividends from a broad US stock index fund are qualified, which is one reason such funds are comfortable in a taxable account.
HoldingHow its return is taxed in a taxable accountUsual home
Taxable bond funds, certificates of deposit (CDs), TreasuriesInterest as ordinary income every year (Treasury interest is exempt from state tax)Tax-deferred
Actively managed, high-turnover fundsFrequent gain distributions; short-term ones are taxed as ordinary dividendsTax-deferred or Roth
Broad stock index funds and exchange-traded funds (ETFs)Mostly qualified dividends; gains deferred until saleTaxable or Roth
Highest-growth holdings you expect to keep longestLarge deferred gains, taxed at sale unless held to death or donatedRoth first
Municipal bondsInterest exempt from federal income taxTaxable only
Emergency cashInterest taxed yearly, but you need to reach it without penaltyTaxable (a savings account)
Going furtherComparing a municipal bond with a taxable bond

Municipal bond interest is generally exempt from federal income tax, so munis pay lower yields. To compare, convert the muni yield into its tax-equivalent yield:

Tax-equivalent yield = muni yield ÷ (1 − your marginal tax rate)

A 3.2% muni is worth 3.2% ÷ 0.76 = 4.21% to someone in the 24% bracket. If a taxable bond of similar quality and maturity yields 4.5%, it keeps 4.5% × 0.76 = 3.42% after tax even in a taxable account — more than the muni's 3.2% — so for this investor the taxable bond wins, and inside a tax-deferred account it keeps all 4.5%. At higher brackets or lower taxable yields the answer flips. Always compare after-tax yields directly, and remember that munis never belong in an IRA, where their tax exemption is wasted.

Where each asset goes

The conventional rules, and why they work

Most planners use a short set of rules. They follow from one principle: give the shelter to the holding that would lose the most to taxes, measured in after-tax dollars over your time horizon.

Why bonds usually go in the tax-deferred account

Look again at Figure 36.1. With these assumptions, a bond loses about 1.08 percentage points of return a year in a taxable account; a stock index fund loses about 0.72. The bigger yearly wedge belongs in the shelter. That is the finding of Dammon, Spatt and Zhang (2004) in The Journal of Finance: they found "a strong preference for holding taxable bonds in the tax-deferred account and equity in the taxable account, reflecting the higher tax burden on taxable bonds relative to equity."

Why the Roth gets the growth

A Roth dollar is worth more than a tax-deferred dollar in after-tax terms, and it is never taxed again. Whatever grows fastest multiplies that advantage. There is a second reason: holdings in a Roth need no withdrawals during your lifetime, so a Roth is also the natural home for money you may leave to heirs (INV-091 covers inherited accounts).

Why the rules are only a starting point

The rules assume bond interest is taxed heavily and stock returns lightly. When bond yields are very low, or when the stock funds you own are tax-inefficient, the answer can flip. Poterba, Shoven and Sialm, studying 1962–1998 returns, found that savers who owned actively managed equity funds would have accumulated more by holding the equity funds in the tax-deferred account — because those funds distributed so much taxable income, and because taxable investors could hold tax-exempt bonds instead. The case study tab looks at both findings.

Going furtherSpecial cases: non-qualified dividends and planned gifts

Several holdings have features that change their placement. Funds whose dividends are mostly non-qualified (compare box 1a, total ordinary dividends, with box 1b, qualified dividends, on their Form 1099-DIV) behave like bonds for this purpose and usually belong in tax-deferred accounts. Holdings you might want to donate or leave to heirs gain from the taxable account's special exits (donation, INV-039; step-up in basis at death, INV-090). Check each fund's Form 1099-DIV history before deciding.

Measure it

Place the same portfolio three ways

The calculator holds your account sizes and your overall stock-bond mix fixed, and places the holdings three ways. A follows the conventional rules: bonds into tax-deferred accounts first, stocks into Roth and then taxable. B does the reverse. C holds the same mix in every account, which is what a single target-date fund in each account does. It then grows everything, taxes everything, and counts tax-deferred money after the future tax.

With the Harpers' balances, a 60/40 mix, 20 years, 7% stocks and 4.5% bonds, placement A ends at about $2.95 million after tax, about $32,700 more than the same mix everywhere and about $81,700 more than the reverse placement. Per year, A beats C by roughly 0.06 percentage points and beats B by about 0.14. That is real money for no added risk, but it is not a fortune; how much you save each year and what you pay in fees matter far more.

Practitioner depthHow the calculator computes each account

For n years, pre-tax return g, dividend yield d, bond yield y, ordinary rate to, gains rate tc and future withdrawal rate tw:

Roth: X(1 + g)n  ·  Tax-deferred: X(1 + g)n(1 − tw)  ·  Taxable bonds: X[1 + y(1 − to)]n

Taxable stocks are simulated year by year: price grows at g − d; dividends are taxed at tc and the remainder reinvested, adding to cost basis; at the end the unrealized gain is taxed at tc if you choose "sold." Placement A fills tax-deferred accounts with bonds, then puts stocks first in Roth and then in taxable, with any leftovers following the same order. The model ignores state tax, fees, rebalancing trades and the possibility that tax rates change.

The size of the prize

Modest, reliable, and larger when yields are high

The value of good placement depends on the gap between how the assets are taxed and on their returns. Figure 36.5 reruns the calculator's default case for bond yields from 1% to 7%.

Put in percentage terms, the benefit in these illustrations ranges from about 0.05 of a percentage point a year (placement A against the same mix everywhere) to nearly 0.2 (against the reverse placement at a 7% bond yield). Research on household data also shows that many investors leave this value on the table: in the Federal Reserve's 1998 Survey of Consumer Finances, Bergstresser and Poterba found many households holding equities in their tax-deferred accounts while holding taxable bonds in their taxable accounts, and concluded that "most of these households could reduce their taxes by relocating heavily-taxed fixed income assets to their tax-deferred account."

💡

The honest summary. Asset location is one of the few ways to raise after-tax return without raising risk or cost. It is worth doing once, carefully, and revisiting when accounts change. It is not worth paying high fees for, and it is never worth selling large taxable gains just to relocate.

Going furtherWhy the value is smaller than it first looks

Three things shrink the benefit. First, many households' taxable accounts are small next to their retirement accounts, so there is little to relocate. Second, the conventional placement makes the after-tax portfolio slightly riskier (tab 2), so part of its higher expected wealth is simply more stock exposure. Third, moving holdings out of a taxable account usually means selling, and the tax on accumulated gains can wipe out decades of location benefit. New money, dividends and required changes are the cheap way to improve placement.

What the research found

Three studies of where assets should live

Case study 1The theoretical answer: bonds in the shelter
What happened
Robert Dammon, Chester Spatt and Harold Zhang modeled an investor choosing both allocation and location over a lifetime. They found a strong preference for taxable bonds in the tax-deferred account and equity in the taxable account, robust to adding tax-exempt bonds and liquidity shocks. They also found that the share of total wealth in equity falls as the share of wealth in tax-deferred accounts rises.
What it teaches
When stocks are held tax-efficiently, the conventional rule is the right default. The allocation and the location decisions interact; they are not separate.
Source
Dammon, R. M., Spatt, C. S., & Zhang, H. H. (2004). Optimal asset location and allocation with taxable and tax-deferred investing. The Journal of Finance, 59(3), 999–1037.
Case study 2The historical exception: tax-inefficient stock funds
What happened
James Poterba, John Shoven and Clemens Sialm used actual returns on taxable bonds, tax-exempt bonds and a sample of actively managed equity mutual funds over 1962–1998. High-income savers would have accumulated more by holding their equity mutual fund in the tax-deferred account. Two reasons: the funds imposed substantial tax burdens through distributions, and taxable investors could hold tax-exempt bonds, whose lower yields implied a lower effective tax rate than the statutory one.
What it teaches
The rule "bonds in the shelter" assumes tax-efficient stock holdings. Low-cost index funds and ETFs make that assumption true for most investors today; a portfolio of high-turnover active funds may not.
Source
Poterba, J. M., Shoven, J. B., & Sialm, C. (2000). Asset location for retirement savers (NBER Working Paper 7991); published in Gale, Shoven and Warshawsky (Eds.), Public policies and private pensions, Brookings Institution, 2004.
Case study 3What households actually do
What happened
David Bergstresser and James Poterba used the 1998 Survey of Consumer Finances. Forty-five percent of households had some assets in a tax-deferred account, and more than ten million had at least $25,000 in both a taxable and a tax-deferred account. Many held equities in tax-deferred accounts but not taxable ones, while holding taxable bonds in taxable accounts. For nearly three-quarters of the apparently tax-inefficient households, a shift of less than $10,000 would have produced a tax-efficient allocation.
What it teaches
The fix is often small. A single exchange inside a 401(k) and a new-money decision in the taxable account can do most of the work.
Source
Bergstresser, D., & Poterba, J. (2004). Asset allocation and asset location: Household evidence from the Survey of Consumer Finances. Journal of Public Economics, 88(9–10), 1893–1915 (NBER Working Paper 9268).

Decisions

Placing real households' money

Scenario 1 · The Harpers

Five years from retirement, the Harpers decide to move from almost all stocks to about 60% stocks and 40% bonds. That means holding about $440,000 of bonds. Where should the bonds go?

Bonds in taxableThis realizes large capital gains now (INV-035) and then puts the most heavily taxed asset in the only account that taxes it every year. It is the worst of both.
Bonds inside the 401(k)sRight. Exchanges inside a 401(k) are not taxable events, and bonds are where the shelter helps most. The concentrated tech holdings in the taxable account still need diversifying, but that can be done with loss harvesting, gifts of appreciated shares and gradual sales (INV-037, INV-039).
Bonds in the RothNo account is "safe" or "risky"; the holdings are. The Roth is the most valuable place for growth, and at $60,000 it can hold only a small part of the bonds anyway.
The principle: change your allocation where it costs no tax, and let the shelter go to the most heavily taxed holding.
Scenario 2 · The Riveras

The Riveras have $62,000 in two 401(k)s and $41,000 idle in checking beyond their $9,000 emergency fund. They want some bonds and plan to invest the $41,000. What goes where?

Bond fund in taxableEvery year the interest would be taxed at their 22% federal rate plus Arizona's 2.5%. The same bonds inside the 401(k) would compound untaxed.
Roth first, bonds inside the 401(k)sRight. With $145,000 of income they are under the 2026 Roth IRA income phase-out, which begins at $242,000 for joint filers, and can contribute up to $7,500 each for 2026 (IRS Notice 2025-67). Stock funds in the Roth get the growth; a broad index fund suits the taxable account; bonds live in the 401(k)s.
The principle: with new money, you can get location right from the start at no cost.

What asset location means for each household

Nothing to place yet. Jordan's $3,000 is emergency cash and belongs in a savings account. As her 401(k) and perhaps a Roth IRA grow, she can start the Roth with stock funds; location will matter years from now.
Start right with new money. Roth IRAs for stock funds, bonds inside the 401(k)s, a broad index fund in taxable, and the $9,000 emergency fund in savings. Arizona taxes interest, so bonds in taxable cost them twice.
Her accounts already point the right way. As Monica moves toward a more conservative mix before retiring at about 67, the bonds belong inside the 401(k), where their interest is not taxed each year (Ohio taxes interest too); the $40,000 taxable account suits the broad stock index funds it already holds; and the $40,000 Roth IRA, which has no required withdrawals in her lifetime, can hold her longest-horizon stock funds. The $30,000 of cash is her emergency fund, not an investment to place.
Bonds into the 401(k)s. As they de-risk, the Harpers can hold their whole bond share inside the pre-tax accounts, keep stock index funds in taxable as they unwind the tech stocks, and put their best long-term growth in the Roth.
Mostly one account. With 93% in her IRA, Ruth's placement choice is small. Her CDs are her cash reserve; her IRA should hold the rest of her mix. Her bigger tax questions are withdrawals and conversions (INV-038, INV-042).

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A traditional IRA holds $500,000. You expect to withdraw at a 22% federal rate. What is it worth after tax, in dollars?

After-tax value = balance × (1 − tw) = $500,000 × 0.78 = $390,000. The other $110,000 is, in effect, the government's share.
Exercise 2

A bond fund yields 4.5%. Held in a taxable account by someone whose interest is taxed at 24%, what after-tax return does it keep each year, in percent?

4.5% × 0.76 = 3.42%. Inside a Roth, or on the owner's share of a tax-deferred account, it keeps the full 4.5%.
Exercise 3

A municipal bond yields 3.0%. What is its tax-equivalent yield for someone in the 32% federal bracket, in percent?

3.0% ÷ (1 − 0.32) = 3.0% ÷ 0.68 = 4.41%. A taxable bond must yield more than that to beat the muni for this investor.
Exercise 4

The Harpers have $220,000 taxable, $820,000 in pre-tax 401(k)s and $60,000 in Roth IRAs. At a 22% future withdrawal rate, what are their savings worth after tax, in dollars?

$220,000 + ($820,000 × 0.78 = $639,600) + $60,000 = $919,600, about 84% of the $1.1 million statement total. (Gains in the taxable account would also be taxed if sold; ignored here.)
