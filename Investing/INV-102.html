Special-Needs Planning

When a child has a disability that will last a lifetime, a well-meant gift or inheritance can cost them the benefits they will rely on for income and health care. The tools that prevent this — special needs trusts and ABLE accounts — are well established, and ABLE eligibility widened substantially on January 1, 2026. This module follows one Ohio mother through the rules and the plan.

The question this module answersHow do we provide for a child with a disability, during our lives and after, without disqualifying them from SSI and Medicaid?

Start here

Why "just leave it to my son" can backfire

For most heirs, receiving money is simply good news. For someone who depends on means-tested benefits, it can be the opposite. Programs such as Supplemental Security Income and Medicaid are available only to people with very few assets. An inheritance received outright can push the person over the limit until it is spent down.

A special needs trust and an ABLE account are the two tools that solve this. The ordinary case still applies: families want to help, and extra money can pay for things benefits never will — therapy, a day program, travel, a phone, a better chance at a job. The planning question is how to deliver that help alongside the benefits rather than instead of them. Figure 102.1 shows the two-part structure most plans use.

To make this concrete, this module follows Maya Walker, 58, a hospital billing manager in Columbus, Ohio, and her son Eli, 13, who has autism with an intellectual disability and will likely need support for life. Eli's father is not involved and pays no support. Maya earns $92,000 and has $400,000 saved, but she has no special needs trust, no ABLE account, no will naming a guardian and no life insurance beyond a group policy worth one year's salary. Today her income keeps Eli off SSI; at 18 that changes. Each of her gaps has a tool, and this module takes them in order.

What this module covers

  1. How SSI and Medicaid count income and resources, with 2026 figures, and why age 18 is a turning point.
  2. Medicaid waivers in Ohio and the county board waiting list.
  3. Special needs trusts: first-party, pooled and third-party.
  4. ABLE accounts, including Ohio's STABLE program and the 2026 expansion to disabilities that began before age 46.

  6. Guardianship under Ohio law, and less restrictive alternatives.

The benefits you are protecting

SSI and Medicaid: tight limits, and a turning point at 18

Supplemental Security IncomeSSI
What it is
A federal program run by the Social Security Administration (SSA) that pays a monthly cash benefit to people who are aged, blind or disabled and have very limited income and resources. It is based on need, not on work history.
Used for
Basic living costs, especially food and shelter. In Ohio, receiving SSI also brings Medicaid: since August 1, 2016, Ohio has accepted SSA's SSI decision as its Medicaid decision, so there is no second disability determination (Ohio Department of Medicaid).
What it means for you
For 2026 the maximum federal payment is $994 a month for an individual and $1,491 for a couple. Countable resources must stay at or below $2,000 for an individual and $3,000 for a couple.

What counts as a resource

SSA counts cash, bank accounts, stocks, funds, savings bonds, digital currency, land, life insurance and anything else that could be turned into cash for food or shelter. It does not count, among other things, the home the person lives in, household goods, one vehicle used for transportation, burial spaces, life insurance with a combined face value of $1,500 or less, up to $100,000 in an ABLE account and certain trusts. If countable resources exceed the limit at the start of a month, no SSI is paid for that month. Giving resources away or selling them for less than they are worth can cause up to 36 months of ineligibility (SSA, Understanding SSI: Resources, 2026).

How income reduces the payment

SSI is reduced by "countable income": SSA subtracts it from the $994 federal benefit rate. The first $20 of most income is not counted; for wages, the first $65 and half of the rest are also not counted. Since September 30, 2024, food received from others no longer counts as in-kind support; shelter still can (SSA).

SSI payment = $994 − [(unearned income − $20) + (wages − any unused part of the $20 − $65) ÷ 2]

With $300 a month of unearned income, such as a Social Security benefit, countable income is $280 and the federal SSI payment is $714, exactly SSA's own 2026 example. Because only half of wages above the exclusions count, a job always raises total income: each extra dollar earned lowers SSI by about 50 cents.

Why Eli gets nothing now, and what changes at 18

Parental deeming
What it is
SSA's rule that part of a parent's income and resources is treated as available to a child under 18 who lives at home. A stepparent's income counts too, as long as the parent lives in the home (SSA, Spotlight on deeming parental income and resources, 2026).
Used for
Deciding whether a disabled child can receive SSI, and how much, while the parents support the household.
What it means for you
SSA says deeming stops the month after a child turns 18, so "a child who could not receive SSI because of deeming may be able to get SSI when he or she turns age 18." From then on only the young adult's own income and resources count.

On a $92,000 salary, the part of Maya's pay that SSA would deem to Eli is far more than $994 a month, so he receives no SSI today. Eli turns 18 in 2031. At that point SSA evaluates him under the adult definition of disability, and his own finances decide eligibility: he will need countable resources of $2,000 or less, which is why nothing should be saved in his name. Where he lives matters too. SSA's 2026 living-arrangements spotlight says a person who lives in someone else's household and does not pay his shelter costs can have SSI reduced by up to one-third of the federal benefit rate, $331.33 a month for 2026; one who pays his share of shelter costs can receive the full amount. Figure 102.2 shows what that means for Eli at each stage.

Going furtherBenefits on a parent's record, and keeping Medicaid

An adult whose disability began before age 22 may qualify for a "child's" benefit on a parent's Social Security record once the parent starts retirement or disability benefits or dies. SSA calls this the Disabled Adult Child benefit; the person must be unmarried and 18 or older (SSA, Benefits for people with disabilities). While the parent is alive the child can receive up to half of the parent's full benefit; after the parent's death, children generally receive 75% (SSA). For Eli, $1,150 of countable unearned income would leave no SSI: $994 − ($1,150 − $20) is below zero.

Losing SSI could have meant losing Medicaid. It does not, if the order of events is right. Section 1634(c) of the Social Security Act, carried into Ohio's rule (Ohio Administrative Code 5160:1-3-02.6), keeps Medicaid for a disabled adult child who received SSI on a disability that began before 22, lost it solely because of the childhood benefit, and would still qualify for SSI without it. That is the strongest reason for Eli to apply for SSI at 18, even though the childhood benefit will later be larger.

Services, not just health care

Home and community-based services waivers in Ohio

For a person with a developmental disability, Medicaid is not only doctor visits and prescriptions. It can also pay for the daily support that lets someone live at home or in the community: help cooking, getting around, getting to work. In Ohio much of that support comes through waivers run by the Ohio Department of Developmental Disabilities (DODD), which says more than 40,000 Ohioans with developmental disabilities are enrolled in one.

Home and community-based services waiverHCBS waiver
What it is
A Medicaid program that pays for services supporting someone with a developmental disability "living on their own, with family, with a roommate, or with a provider through Ohio Shared Living" (DODD).
Used for
Person-centered services listed in a service plan, such as someone to help cook, help the person get around, or drive them to work (DODD, Waiver comparison).
What it means for you
Waivers have a waiting list. The assessment can be requested at any age, so families should start long before the services are needed.

Three DODD waivers

WaiverDODD's description of who it fits
Level One WaiverPeople who do not need a lot of paid staff; usually they need help with only a few things, or have people who can help with some of them
Self-Empowered Life Funding (SELF) WaiverPeople who want to be in charge of some of their services
Individual Options WaiverPeople who may need a lot of help in their home, or who need different kinds of services

For Maya, the practical step is a call to her county board of developmental disabilities (Columbus is largely in Franklin County) to ask for Eli's waiting list assessment now, at 13, and to learn which services the board can fund in the meantime. DODD notes that a person already enrolled keeps their waiver, and that anyone on the list is reassessed at least once a year or sooner if circumstances change. Waivers pay for services, not cash, so they complement SSI rather than replace it.

Going furtherWhy Ohio's 2016 change matters for waivers

Until mid-2016 Ohio used the "209(b)" option, with Medicaid limits for aged, blind and disabled people stricter than SSI's (income below $634 a month when SSI allowed $743, and assets below $1,500 rather than $2,000, at the time), and a second disability decision by the county. From August 1, 2016, Ohio became a "1634" state: SSI approval is Medicaid approval (Ohio Department of Medicaid, Ohio's Disability Determination Redesign). For Eli, an SSI approval at 18 therefore also settles the Medicaid eligibility that most waiver services require.

The central tool

Three kinds of special needs trust

A trust is a legal arrangement in which one party (the trustee) holds property for another (the beneficiary). SSA's rule is that a trust set up with the person's own assets generally counts as their resource. The law makes two exceptions for disabled individuals, and trusts funded by other people are judged differently again. That gives three types.

First-party special needs trust
What it is
A trust holding the disabled person's own assets, established for someone under 65 through the actions of the person, a parent, a grandparent, a legal guardian or a court. It must be for the person's sole benefit and must reimburse every state that provided Medicaid, from what remains at death.
Used for
Rescuing eligibility when the person already has money: an inheritance left outright by mistake, a personal-injury settlement, or retroactive benefits.
What it means for you
It is a repair tool. Planning ahead with a third-party trust avoids both the age limit and the payback.
Third-party special needs trustalso called a supplemental needs trust
What it is
A trust established with the assets of someone other than the beneficiary or their spouse, for example a parent's or grandparent's money, often created in a will or funded by life insurance.
Used for
Leaving an inheritance to a person with a disability, and letting relatives give during life, without the money counting as the person's resource.
What it means for you
This is the tool most families need. Name it, not the person, in wills and on beneficiary designations.

For Maya this is the central document. Her will can create the trust for Eli, or she can sign a standalone trust now so relatives can give to it during her lifetime. Either way, the trust — not Eli — should be the beneficiary of her $290,000 401(k), her $40,000 Roth IRA, her $40,000 taxable account (through a transfer-on-death registration or her will), her group life policy and any new term policy. She also needs a trustee she trusts with money and a backup trustee, because the trust may run for fifty years or more after her death.

How distributions affect SSI

Even an excluded trust can reduce SSI if it pays the wrong things. SSA's 2026 trust spotlight sets out the rules: money paid directly to the person reduces SSI; money paid to someone else for the person's shelter reduces SSI, but by no more than $351.33 a month in 2026; and payments to others for items other than food and shelter — medical care, phone bills, education, entertainment — do not reduce SSI. A trustee who understands these rules can do a great deal of good at little cost to benefits.

Going furtherRetirement accounts and a trust

Retirement accounts are often a family's biggest asset, as Maya's 401(k) is. Under the SECURE Act rules in IRS Publication 590-B, a disabled or chronically ill beneficiary is an "eligible designated beneficiary" who can stretch withdrawals rather than follow the 10-year rule, and an "applicable multi-beneficiary trust" can preserve that treatment when the trust is drafted for it. Naming the person directly on an IRA or 401(k) could put withdrawals straight into their countable income; naming a properly drafted trust avoids that. This is specialist drafting (INV-091, INV-087).

The person's own tax-advantaged account

ABLE accounts, Ohio's STABLE program and the 2026 expansion

ABLE accountsection 529A
What it is
A tax-advantaged account created by the Stephen Beck, Jr., Achieving a Better Life Experience Act of 2014 and run through state programs. The person with the disability owns it and is its beneficiary; each person may have only one. Earnings are tax-free when spent on qualified disability expenses.
Used for
Qualified disability expenses, broadly defined: education, housing, transportation, employment training and support, assistive technology, health, financial management, legal fees, funeral and burial, and basic living expenses.
What it means for you
SSI ignores the first $100,000 in the account. It gives the person control and flexibility that a trust does not, within an annual contribution limit. For a minor, or someone who cannot manage it, a person with signature authority opens and runs it (SSA).

What changed on January 1, 2026

Until the end of 2025, a person qualified only if the disability began before age 26. Effective January 1, 2026, the onset age is 46 (SSA, Spotlight on ABLE accounts, 2026). The person must receive SSI or Social Security disability benefits based on a disability that began before 46, or have a disability certification with a physician's diagnosis showing marked and severe functional limitations that began before 46. Eli, whose disability began in early childhood, qualifies under the old rule and the new one; the change brings many people disabled by injury or illness in their late 20s, 30s and early 40s into the program for the first time.

Ohio's program: STABLE Account

Any state's ABLE program can be used, but Ohio's own, STABLE Account, has features that suit Maya. According to the program, Ohio residents pay $0 to maintain an account (residents of states outside its partner group pay $12 a year); there are five investment options, four built from mutual funds and one FDIC-insured savings option, which can be changed twice a year; and an "Authorized Legal Representative," which includes a parent, can open and run the account for the beneficiary. Ohio taxpayers can deduct up to $4,000 a year per STABLE account they contribute to, with unlimited carryforward of larger contributions (STABLE Account; Ohio Revised Code 5747.78). That deduction is available to any Ohio taxpayer who contributes, so relatives in Ohio can use it too.

The defaults describe a plan for Eli: a new account at $0, an assumed $500 in his own bank account, an assumed $4,000 a year from Maya and relatives (the amount one Ohio contributor can deduct each year) and a 5% return. The balance passes $100,000 in year 17, when Eli is 30, and reaches about $132,000 after 20 years. From the year it passes $100,000, every dollar above that line counts toward the $2,000 SSI limit, and SSA suspends SSI cash payments (but not Medicaid, if only the ABLE excess causes it) until the countable total is back under. That is why many families use an ABLE account for flexible spending money and a third-party trust for long-term reserves.

What the documents cannot say

The letter of intent, and coordinating the whole family's plan

Letter of intent
What it is
A written, non-binding guide from parents or caregivers to the people who will care for a person with a disability after them: routines, health history, what calms and what upsets, friends, preferences, goals, and where every document and account is.
Used for
Passing on decades of knowledge to a future caregiver and trustee, so the person's life can continue with as little disruption as possible.
What it means for you
It is the most personal document in the plan and costs nothing to write. Review it every year and whenever something important changes.

Coordinating everyone's estate plan

  • Every relative who might leave money — parents, grandparents, siblings, aunts — should know to name the special needs trust, not the person, in wills and on beneficiary designations for life insurance and retirement accounts (INV-085).
  • Life insurance on a parent is one way to fund a third-party trust with a known amount at death (INV-023). Maya's group policy, one year's salary ($92,000), is tied to her job; a 20-year term policy owned by her and payable to the trust would cover Eli until she is 78.
  • Name successors: a trustee and backup trustee, a future caregiver, the agent under any power of attorney, and, for Eli, the person Maya would want as his guardian (see the next tab).
  • An equal split is not always fair. In families with more than one child, some leave more to the trust because the person's needs are lifelong; others leave less because benefits cover the basics. Either is legitimate; decide deliberately.

Decision-making as an adult

Guardianship under Ohio law, and less restrictive alternatives

When a child with a disability becomes a legal adult, parents generally no longer have automatic authority over that adult's medical, financial or legal decisions. Families then face a choice about how much support, or substitute decision-making, the person needs. The options range from informal help to a court-ordered guardianship, and state law governs each. Eli turns 18 in 2031, so Maya has about five years to decide.

GuardianshipOhio: guardian of the person, the estate, or both
What it is
A court order giving another person legal authority to make some or all decisions for an adult the court finds unable to make them. In Ohio the probate court appoints a guardian of the person, of the estate (money and property), or of both (Ohio Revised Code 2111.02).
Used for
Situations where the person cannot understand or communicate decisions even with support, and protection is needed.
What it means for you
It removes rights and is hard to undo. Consider the less restrictive options first, and a limited guardianship before a full one.

What Ohio's statute requires

  • A hearing and a high standard. Before appointing a guardian for an adult alleged to be incompetent, the probate court must hold a hearing, and incompetency must be proved by clear and convincing evidence. The person has the right to independent counsel and to have a friend or family member present (ORC 2111.02(C)).
  • Less restrictive alternatives count. Evidence of a less restrictive alternative must be considered if introduced, and the court may deny a guardianship because one exists (ORC 2111.02(C)(5)–(6)).
  • Limited guardianship. The court may appoint a limited guardian with specific powers; the person "retains all of the incompetent's or minor's rights in all areas not affected by the court order" (ORC 2111.02(B)(1)).
  • Nominating a guardian in advance. A parent may nominate, in a writing signed before two witnesses or acknowledged before a notary, a guardian or successor guardian for a minor or incompetent adult child, and may file it with the probate court for safekeeping (ORC 2111.121). For Maya, this is how she chooses who would step in for Eli if she dies or becomes unable to act.
Going furtherA representative payee is not a guardian

SSA's Representative Payment Program appoints a representative payee, usually family or a friend, to manage Social Security or SSI payments for a beneficiary who cannot manage them. The payee must use the money for the beneficiary's needs, follow SSA's payee rules and may need to report how it was spent. A representative payee may place benefits into the beneficiary's ABLE account when it is in their best interest, under the same payee rules (SSA). The appointment covers only those benefits; it gives no authority over medical care or other money.

Real law

Three changes that reshaped special-needs planning

Case study 1ABLE accounts open to disabilities that began before 46, 2026
What happened
The ABLE Act became law on December 19, 2014, for people whose disability began before age 26. Effective January 1, 2026, the SECURE 2.0 Act of 2022 raised the onset age to 46, and P.L. 119-21 then made the higher "ABLE to Work" contribution permanent and changed how the $20,000 standard limit is indexed.
What it teaches
People disabled as adults — by a stroke, an accident, multiple sclerosis — before 46 can now save in an account that SSI largely ignores. Families who ruled ABLE out before 2026 should check again.
Source
Social Security Administration. Spotlight on Achieving a Better Life Experience (ABLE) accounts (2026 edition); P.L. 119-21, secs. 70115–70117.
Case study 2Food no longer reduces SSI, September 2024
What happened
Effective September 30, 2024, SSA stopped counting food as in-kind support and maintenance. Before then, a relative who bought groceries for an SSI recipient, or a trust that paid for food, could reduce the monthly payment. Shelter can still count, up to $351.33 a month in 2026.
What it teaches
Families and trustees can now help with food without reducing SSI. Rules that were once good advice ("never let the trust buy groceries") can become outdated, so trustees need current guidance.
Source
Social Security Administration. Understanding SSI: Income, SSI Spotlight on trusts and SSI Spotlight on living arrangements (2026 editions).
Case study 3Ohio makes SSI approval a Medicaid approval, 2016
What happened
Ohio long used the federal "209(b)" option, which let it set Medicaid limits for aged, blind and disabled people stricter than SSI's — income below $634 a month versus $743 for SSI, and assets below $1,500 versus $2,000, when the change was proposed — and required people to prove disability twice. The 2015 state budget authorized a switch, and from August 1, 2016, Ohio accepted SSA's SSI decision as its Medicaid decision.
What it teaches
In Ohio, an adult child's SSI application is also the Medicaid application that opens the door to most waiver services. It is one reason to file for SSI at 18 even while a childhood Social Security benefit is still years away.
Source
Ohio Department of Medicaid. Ohio's Disability Determination Redesign; Ohio Administrative Code 5160:1-3-02.6.

Decisions

Four decisions for families planning lifelong support

Scenario 1 · Maya

Maya has no will. A coworker tells her the simplest plan is to name Eli as beneficiary on her 401(k), Roth IRA and life insurance, since he is her only child. What should Maya do?

Name Eli directlyWith no will, Ohio law already sends her probate estate to her children outright (ORC 2105.06), so naming Eli repeats the problem. As a minor, his inheritance would be held under a probate court guardianship of his estate; from 18, every month his countable resources exceed $2,000 would block SSI and the Medicaid that comes with it. A first-party trust could repair it, but only with a Medicaid payback at his death.
An informal promiseKeeps benefits intact but makes it legally the relative's money: exposed to that person's creditors, divorce or death, with no enforceable duty to Eli.
Will, trust and beneficiary formsRight. The money never becomes Eli's resource, no Medicaid payback applies, and Maya chooses the trustee, the backup trustee and who receives what is left. The same will is where she nominates a guardian for Eli.
The principle: give to the trust, not to the person, and never rely on an informal promise.
Scenario 2 · A relative's gift

A relative wants to give Eli $30,000 in 2026 toward his future. Eli has no ABLE account yet, and Maya's special needs trust is being drafted. How should the gift be made?

An account in Eli's nameMoney in Eli's name is his. It would be sitting there when he turns 18 and SSA looks only at his own resources, and anything over $2,000 at the start of a month would block SSI until it was spent down.
STABLE plus trustRight. $20,000 is the 2026 limit from all contributors combined; if the relative is an Ohio taxpayer, $4,000 is deductible on the Ohio return this year and the rest carries forward. The other $10,000 goes to the trust, which has no contribution limit.
The principle: route gifts through the ABLE account and the trust, within their limits.
Scenario 3 · Maya

Eli turns 18 in 2031. By then he can make many everyday choices with help but cannot handle money or medical paperwork on his own. What arrangement should Maya consider first?

Full guardianshipRemoves more of Eli's rights than he may need, and an Ohio court must find incompetency by clear and convincing evidence and may deny a guardianship if a less restrictive alternative exists. It remains available if lighter tools prove insufficient.
Less restrictive toolsRight, if they fit what Eli can do. Each tool covers one area — money, health, benefits — and a limited guardian's order leaves Eli every right it does not name (ORC 2111.02). A special-needs attorney can confirm what Ohio law allows and draft Maya's written nomination of a successor.
The principle: match the level of legal authority to the help the person actually needs.
Scenario 4 · Daniel and Priya Shah

The Shahs have $3.2 million across taxable, tax-deferred and Roth accounts. Their attorney is drafting a third-party special needs trust for Leena. Should they simply name the trust for one-sixth of today's portfolio and invest everything on a standard 30-year retirement schedule?

Fixed percentageEasy, but disconnected from the need. Leena's housing, care, health, transportation and advocacy costs may continue after both parents die; public benefits, inflation, taxes and trustee costs alter the amount. A share that looks generous today can be too small or unnecessarily large later.
Lifetime support planRight. Separate Leena's baseline and enhanced-care budgets, identify benefits that pay first, choose future caregivers and trustees, and test long lives, early death, inflation and market losses. Coordinate the will, trust and every beneficiary form. Tax-deferred accounts require special attention because trust taxation and inherited-account distribution rules can make an apparently equal bequest unequal; an estate-planning attorney and tax professional should model the actual documents.
The principle: fund a measured lifetime obligation, not a percentage convention, and make the legal, benefits, tax and investment plans agree.

Test the claims

Six things families hear

Apply it

What special-needs planning means for each household

Know it exists. If an accident or illness disabled Denise before 46, she could open an ABLE account herself under the 2026 rules. Her own disability insurance matters more today (INV-023).
Build the option into the wills. Parents of young children can write wills that let a trustee hold any child's share in a special needs trust if that child ever needs benefits. It costs little to include.
This is Maya's plan, and the clock is Eli's 18th birthday in 2031. Before then: a will nominating his guardian and creating a third-party special needs trust; the trust named on her 401(k), Roth IRA, taxable account, group policy and a new 20-year term policy; a STABLE account for gifts; a letter of intent; a waiting list assessment with her county board; and a decision on guardianship or its alternatives. At 18, an SSI application (up to $994 a month for 2026) protects his Medicaid for the day her claim at 67 brings him a childhood benefit of about $1,150.
Check every beneficiary form. If anyone the Harpers might leave money to receives SSI or Medicaid, their $820,000 of 401(k)s should name a properly drafted trust, not the person.
Gifts from grandparents and friends. If Ruth wanted to help a relative with a disability, a check could do harm; a contribution to their ABLE account or a gift to their trust would not.
Their retirement plan must outlive both parents. The Shahs need a third-party special needs trust, a lifetime support budget for Leena, successor caregivers and trustees, a letter of intent, and beneficiary forms coordinated across $1.2 million taxable, $1.5 million tax-deferred and $500,000 Roth. Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

In 2026 an individual on SSI also receives $300 a month of Social Security and has no wages. What is the federal SSI payment, in dollars?

Countable income: $300 − $20 = $280. Payment: $994 − $280 = $714, matching SSA's 2026 example.
Exercise 2

An SSI recipient has no unearned income and earns $1,085 a month in wages. What is the federal SSI payment, in dollars?

$1,085 − $20 − $65 = $1,000; half counts: $500. Payment: $994 − $500 = $494. Total income is $1,085 + $494 = $1,579.
Exercise 3

An SSI recipient has $104,500 in an ABLE account and $1,200 in a bank account. What are their countable resources for SSI, in dollars?

SSA disregards the first $100,000 of ABLE: $4,500 counts. Plus $1,200 = $5,700, over the $2,000 limit, so SSI cash payments are suspended. Medicaid continues, because it is the ABLE excess, not the $1,200 in the bank, that pushes the total over the limit (SSA).
Exercise 4

In 2026 Eli is 18, has no income, and lives in Maya's home without paying any share of the shelter costs. Suppose SSA applies the full one-third reduction. What is his federal SSI payment, in dollars and cents?

One-third of the $994 federal benefit rate is $331.33. Payment: $994 − $331.33 = $662.67. If Eli paid his share of the household's shelter costs, SSA's living-arrangements spotlight says he could receive up to the full $994.
