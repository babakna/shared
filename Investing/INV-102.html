Special-Needs Planning

When a family member has a disability, a well-meant gift or inheritance can cost them the benefits they rely on for income and health care. The tools that prevent this — special needs trusts and ABLE accounts — are well established, and ABLE eligibility widened substantially on January 1, 2026. This module explains how the benefit rules work and how to plan around them.

The question this module answersHow do we provide for someone with a disability, during our lives and after, without disqualifying them from SSI and Medicaid?

Start here

Why "just leave them the money" can backfire

For most heirs, receiving money is simply good news. For someone who depends on means-tested benefits, it can be the opposite. Programs such as Supplemental Security Income and, in most states, Medicaid are available only to people with very few assets. An inheritance received outright can push the person over the limit until it is spent down.

A special needs trust and an ABLE account are the two tools that solve this. The ordinary case still applies: families want to help, and extra money can pay for things benefits never will — therapy, travel, a better wheelchair, education, a phone, visits home. The planning question is how to deliver that help alongside the benefits rather than instead of them. Figure 102.1 shows the two-part structure most plans use.

To make this concrete, this module adds one person to Jordan's story: Jordan's younger brother Sam, 38, who has an intellectual disability that began in childhood. Sam receives SSI and Medicaid and lives with their mother, Helen, 72, in Columbus. Jordan expects to be Sam's main support one day.

The benefits you are protecting

SSI and Medicaid: tight limits on resources and income

Supplemental Security IncomeSSI
What it is
A federal program run by the Social Security Administration (SSA) that pays a monthly cash benefit to people who are aged, blind or disabled and have very limited income and resources. It is based on need, not on work history.
Used for
Basic living costs, especially food and shelter. In most states, receiving SSI also brings Medicaid eligibility; in some states Medicaid requires a separate application (SSA).
What it means for you
For 2026 the maximum federal payment is $994 a month for an individual and $1,491 for a couple. Countable resources must stay at or below $2,000 for an individual and $3,000 for a couple.

What counts as a resource

SSA counts cash, bank accounts, stocks, funds, savings bonds, digital currency, land, life insurance and anything else that could be turned into cash for food or shelter. It does not count, among other things, the home the person lives in, household goods, one vehicle used for transportation, burial spaces, life insurance with a combined face value of $1,500 or less, up to $100,000 in an ABLE account and certain trusts. If countable resources exceed the limit at the start of a month, no SSI is paid for that month. Giving resources away or selling them for less than they are worth can cause up to 36 months of ineligibility (SSA, Understanding SSI: Resources, 2026).

How income reduces the payment

SSI is reduced by "countable income": SSA subtracts it from the $994 federal benefit rate. The first $20 of most income is not counted; for wages, the first $65 and half of the rest are also not counted. Since September 30, 2024, food received from others no longer counts as in-kind support; shelter still can (SSA).

SSI payment = $994 − [(unearned income − $20) + (wages − any unused part of the $20 − $65) ÷ 2]

With $300 a month of unearned income, such as a Social Security benefit, countable income is $280 and the federal SSI payment is $714, exactly SSA's own 2026 example. Because only half of wages above the exclusions count, a job always raises total income: each extra dollar earned lowers SSI by about 50 cents.

Going furtherBenefits on a parent's record

An adult whose disability began before age 22 may qualify for a "child's" benefit on a parent's Social Security record once the parent starts retirement or disability benefits or dies. SSA calls this the Disabled Adult Child benefit; the person must be unmarried and 18 or older (SSA, Benefits for people with disabilities). Because it can change which programs someone qualifies for, contact SSA when a parent of an adult with a disability files for benefits.

The central tool

Three kinds of special needs trust

A trust is a legal arrangement in which one party (the trustee) holds property for another (the beneficiary). SSA's rule is that a trust set up with the person's own assets generally counts as their resource. The law makes two exceptions for disabled individuals, and trusts funded by other people are judged differently again. That gives three types.

First-party special needs trust
What it is
A trust holding the disabled person's own assets, established for someone under 65 through the actions of the person, a parent, a grandparent, a legal guardian or a court. It must be for the person's sole benefit and must reimburse every state that provided Medicaid, from what remains at death.
Used for
Rescuing eligibility when the person already has money: an inheritance left outright by mistake, a personal-injury settlement, or retroactive benefits.
What it means for you
It is a repair tool. Planning ahead with a third-party trust avoids both the age limit and the payback.
Third-party special needs trust
What it is
A trust established with the assets of someone other than the beneficiary or their spouse, for example a parent's or grandparent's money, often created in a will or funded by life insurance.
Used for
Leaving an inheritance to a person with a disability, and letting relatives give during life, without the money counting as the person's resource.
What it means for you
This is the tool most families need. Name it, not the person, in wills and on beneficiary designations.

How distributions affect SSI

Even an excluded trust can reduce SSI if it pays the wrong things. SSA's 2026 trust spotlight sets out the rules: money paid directly to the person reduces SSI; money paid to someone else for the person's shelter reduces SSI, but by no more than $351.33 a month in 2026; and payments to others for items other than food and shelter — medical care, phone bills, education, entertainment — do not reduce SSI. A trustee who understands these rules can do a great deal of good at little cost to benefits.

Going furtherRetirement accounts and a trust

Retirement accounts are often a family's biggest asset. Under the SECURE Act rules in IRS Publication 590-B, a disabled or chronically ill beneficiary is an "eligible designated beneficiary" who can stretch withdrawals rather than follow the 10-year rule, and an "applicable multi-beneficiary trust" can preserve that treatment when the trust is drafted for it. Naming the person directly on an IRA could put withdrawals straight into their countable income; naming a properly drafted trust avoids that. This is specialist drafting (INV-091, INV-087).

The person's own tax-advantaged account

ABLE accounts, and the 2026 expansion

ABLE accountsection 529A
What it is
A tax-advantaged account created by the Stephen Beck, Jr., Achieving a Better Life Experience Act of 2014 and run through state programs. The person with the disability owns it and is its beneficiary; each person may have only one. Earnings are tax-free when spent on qualified disability expenses.
Used for
Qualified disability expenses, broadly defined: education, housing, transportation, employment training and support, assistive technology, health, financial management, legal fees, funeral and burial, and basic living expenses.
What it means for you
SSI ignores the first $100,000 in the account. It gives the person control and flexibility that a trust does not, within an annual contribution limit.

What changed on January 1, 2026

Until the end of 2025, a person qualified only if the disability began before age 26. Effective January 1, 2026, the onset age is 46 (SSA, Spotlight on ABLE accounts, 2026). The person must receive SSI or Social Security disability benefits based on a disability that began before 46, or have a disability certification with a physician's diagnosis showing marked and severe functional limitations that began before 46. The change brings many people disabled by injury or illness in their late 20s, 30s and early 40s into the program for the first time.

The 2025 law also made several ABLE features permanent: the larger "ABLE to Work" contribution (sec. 70115), the Saver's Credit for a beneficiary's own ABLE contributions (sec. 70116) and limited rollovers from a 529 plan into an ABLE account (sec. 70117).

With the defaults — $18,000 today, $12,000 a year and a 5% return — the account passes $100,000 in year 6. From then on, every dollar above $100,000 counts toward the $2,000 SSI limit, and SSA suspends SSI cash payments (but not Medicaid, if only the ABLE excess causes it) until the countable total is back under. That is why many families use an ABLE account for flexible spending money and a third-party trust for long-term reserves.

What the documents cannot say

The letter of intent, and coordinating the whole family's plan

Letter of intent
What it is
A written, non-binding guide from parents or caregivers to the people who will care for a person with a disability after them: routines, health history, what calms and what upsets, friends, preferences, goals, and where every document and account is.
Used for
Passing on decades of knowledge to a future caregiver and trustee, so the person's life can continue with as little disruption as possible.
What it means for you
It is the most personal document in the plan and costs nothing to write. Review it every year and whenever something important changes.

Coordinating everyone's estate plan

  • Every relative who might leave money — parents, grandparents, siblings, aunts — should know to name the special needs trust, not the person, in wills and on beneficiary designations for life insurance and retirement accounts (INV-085).
  • Life insurance on a parent is one way to fund a third-party trust with a known amount at death (INV-023).
  • Name successors: a trustee and backup trustee, a future caregiver, and the agent under any power of attorney.
  • An equal split is not always fair. Some families leave more to the trust because the person's needs are lifelong; others leave less because benefits cover the basics. Either is legitimate; decide deliberately.

Decision-making as an adult

Guardianship, and less restrictive alternatives

When a child with a disability becomes a legal adult, parents generally no longer have automatic authority over that adult's medical, financial or legal decisions. Families then face a choice about how much support, or substitute decision-making, the person needs. The options range from informal help to a court-ordered guardianship, and state law governs each.

Guardianshipcalled conservatorship for finances in some states
What it is
A court order giving another person legal authority to make some or all decisions for an adult the court finds unable to make them.
Used for
Situations where the person cannot understand or communicate decisions even with support, and protection is needed.
What it means for you
It removes rights and is hard to undo. Courts can often grant a limited guardianship covering only specific areas. Consider the less restrictive options first.
Going furtherA representative payee is not a guardian

SSA's Representative Payment Program appoints a representative payee, usually family or a friend, to manage Social Security or SSI payments for a beneficiary who cannot manage them. The payee must use the money for the beneficiary's needs, follow SSA's payee rules and may need to report how it was spent. A representative payee may place benefits into the beneficiary's ABLE account when it is in their best interest, under the same payee rules (SSA). The appointment covers only those benefits; it gives no authority over medical care or other money.

Real law

Three changes that reshaped special-needs planning

Case study 1ABLE accounts open to disabilities that began before 46, 2026
What happened
The ABLE Act became law on December 19, 2014, for people whose disability began before age 26. Effective January 1, 2026, the SECURE 2.0 Act of 2022 raised the onset age to 46, and P.L. 119-21 then made the higher "ABLE to Work" contribution permanent and changed how the $20,000 standard limit is indexed.
What it teaches
People disabled as adults — by a stroke, an accident, multiple sclerosis — before 46 can now save in an account that SSI largely ignores. Families who ruled ABLE out before 2026 should check again.
Source
Social Security Administration. Spotlight on Achieving a Better Life Experience (ABLE) accounts (2026 edition); P.L. 119-21, secs. 70115–70117.
Case study 2Food no longer reduces SSI, September 2024
What happened
Effective September 30, 2024, SSA stopped counting food as in-kind support and maintenance. Before then, a relative who bought groceries for an SSI recipient, or a trust that paid for food, could reduce the monthly payment. Shelter can still count, up to $351.33 a month in 2026.
What it teaches
Families and trustees can now help with food without reducing SSI. Rules that were once good advice ("never let the trust buy groceries") can become outdated, so trustees need current guidance.
Source
Social Security Administration. Understanding SSI: Income and SSI Spotlight on trusts (2026 editions).
Case study 3Adults can set up their own first-party trust, 2016
What happened
Before December 13, 2016, a first-party special needs trust had to be established by a disabled person's parent, grandparent, legal guardian or a court; the person could not set it up themselves, even with full mental capacity. For trusts established on or after that date, the disabled individual can also establish it.
What it teaches
A competent adult who receives a settlement or inheritance no longer needs a court petition or a relative to protect their benefits, which saves time and cost.
Source
Social Security Administration. Program Operations Manual System, SI 01120.203, Exceptions to counting trusts established on or after January 1, 2000.

Decisions

Three decisions for Sam's family

Scenario 1 · Jordan and Helen

Helen's will leaves her $240,000 estate equally to Jordan and Sam. Jordan realizes Sam's half would be his outright. What should Helen do?

Do nothingSam's SSI would stop for every month his countable resources exceed $2,000, and Medicaid could be affected. A first-party trust could repair it, but only with a Medicaid payback at his death.
Leave it all to JordanKeeps benefits intact but is legally Jordan's money: exposed to Jordan's creditors, a divorce or Jordan's own death, with no enforceable duty to Sam.
Third-party trustRight. Sam's share never becomes his resource, no Medicaid payback applies, and Helen chooses who receives what is left. Jordan and a backup trustee manage it for Sam.
The principle: give to the trust, not to the person, and never rely on an informal promise.
Scenario 2 · Sam's aunt

Sam's aunt wants to give him $30,000 in 2026 for a used car and future expenses. Sam has an ABLE account with $4,000 in it. How should the gift be made?

A check to SamSSA counts cash from friends and relatives as unearned income in the month it is received, and money kept into the following month counts toward the resource limit. His SSI would stop.
ABLE plus trustRight. $20,000 is the 2026 limit from all contributors combined. A car used for his transportation would also be excluded as his one vehicle, so the trustee could buy it directly.
The principle: route gifts through the ABLE account and the trust, within their limits.
Scenario 3 · Jordan

Helen, now 72, has always handled Sam's affairs informally. Sam can make everyday choices with help but struggles with money and medical paperwork. What arrangement should the family consider first?

Full guardianshipRemoves more of Sam's rights than he needs and involves a court. It remains available if the lighter tools prove insufficient.
Less restrictive toolsRight, if Sam can understand and sign them. Each tool covers one area — money, health, benefits — and Sam keeps his voice. An attorney can confirm what Ohio law allows.
The principle: match the level of legal authority to the help the person actually needs.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

In 2026 an individual on SSI also receives $300 a month of Social Security and has no wages. What is the federal SSI payment, in dollars?

Countable income: $300 − $20 = $280. Payment: $994 − $280 = $714, matching SSA's 2026 example.
Exercise 2

An SSI recipient has no unearned income and earns $1,085 a month in wages. What is the federal SSI payment, in dollars?

$1,085 − $20 − $65 = $1,000; half counts: $500. Payment: $994 − $500 = $494. Total income is $1,085 + $494 = $1,579.
Exercise 3

An SSI recipient has $104,500 in an ABLE account and $1,200 in a bank account. What are their countable resources for SSI, in dollars?

SSA disregards the first $100,000 of ABLE: $4,500 counts. Plus $1,200 = $5,700, over the $2,000 limit, so SSI cash payments are suspended. Medicaid continues, because it is the ABLE excess, not the $1,200 in the bank, that pushes the total over the limit (SSA).
Exercise 4

In 2026 a working ABLE beneficiary in Ohio earns $24,000 and receives no employer retirement contributions. What is the most that can go into the ABLE account this year from all sources, including the beneficiary's own ABLE to Work contribution, in dollars?

Standard limit: $20,000. ABLE to Work adds the lesser of the beneficiary's pay ($24,000) or the continental US amount for 2026 ($15,960): $15,960. Total $35,960.
