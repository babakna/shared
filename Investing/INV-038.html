Roth Conversions

A Roth conversion moves money from a traditional IRA or 401(k) into a Roth account. You pay income tax on it now so that it, and everything it earns, is never taxed again. Whether that is a good trade depends on one comparison — your tax rate now versus later — and on getting every hidden rate into that comparison.

The question this module answersShould I pay tax on my retirement savings now, on purpose, to pay less later?

Start here

Tax now or tax later: it is a question of rates

Every dollar in a traditional IRA or 401(k) will be taxed someday — when you withdraw it, or when your heirs do. A conversion does not avoid that tax; it chooses when to pay it.

For many people conversions are not worth doing. If your tax rate now is the same as your rate later, converting changes nothing: paying 22% now or 22% later on the same growing balance gives exactly the same result. If your rate now is higher than later — common during peak earning years — converting loses money. Conversions pay only when you can pay a lower rate now than you, or your heirs, would pay later.

The one comparison

Compare your effective marginal rates

Roth conversion
What it is
Moving money from a traditional IRA (or a pre-tax 401(k), where the plan allows) into a Roth IRA or Roth account. The taxable part of the amount converted is added to your income for the year; there is no 10% early-withdrawal tax on the conversion itself (IRS Publication 590-A).
Used for
Paying tax in low-rate years instead of high-rate ones, shrinking future required distributions, and leaving heirs tax-free money.
What it means for you
It is a bet on rates, not on markets. A conversion made when the market is down converts more shares for the same tax, but it does not change the basic rate comparison.
Convert more when: effective marginal rate on the conversion now < effective marginal rate on that money later (yours or your heirs')

The word effective matters. Your bracket is only the start. A conversion's true marginal rate includes everything that extra income sets off:

Going furtherPaying the tax from outside money

Figure 38.1 paid the conversion tax out of the IRA. Paying it from a taxable account instead is like making an extra Roth contribution: the full converted amount keeps growing tax-free, and the taxable account, whose earnings are taxed every year, shrinks. That usually improves the result a little. Paying the tax from the IRA when you are under 59½ is worse: the part withheld for tax is an early distribution, subject to the 10% additional tax unless an exception applies.

Practitioner depthWhat changed in 2025

Before July 2025, the individual tax rates set in 2017 were scheduled to rise after 2025, and many planners converted in anticipation. Public Law 119-21 permanently extended those rates (IRS Publication 15 for 2026 notes the supplemental withholding rate "remains 22%" because P.L. 119-21 "permanently extended the individual tax rates"). The "convert before rates rise" argument is gone under current law; conversions now rest on your own rate path. The law also added the temporary $6,000 senior deduction for 2025 through 2028, which lowers taxable income for people 65 and older in those years, but whose phase-out adds to marginal rates between about $75,000 and $175,000 of modified AGI for a single filer.

How much to convert

Convert up to the top of a bracket, not beyond

Because tax brackets are progressive, the cost of each additional conversion dollar rises in steps. The usual approach, called bracket filling, is to convert exactly enough each year to reach the top of a chosen bracket and stop.

A married couple with no other income can convert $133,000 in 2026 and stay in the 12% bracket ($100,800 + $32,200); the federal tax is $11,600, an average of 8.7%. Filling to the top of the 22% bracket takes $243,600 and costs $35,932, an average of 14.8%. Those are the Harpers' numbers in their first years after retiring, before Social Security starts.

Going furtherWhy a smaller conversion over more years usually wins

Filling a low bracket every year for a decade often converts as much in total as filling a high bracket for two or three years — at a much lower average rate. The planner in tab 7 shows this for the Harpers: filling the 12% bracket from 62 to 74 converts about $1.30 million in total; filling the 24% bracket converts about $1.27 million but in only three years, at far higher rates. Starting early and converting steadily is the core of most good plans.

Timing

The low-income years between paycheck and required distributions

For many retirees there is a stretch of years when taxable income falls sharply: wages have stopped, but Social Security has not started and required minimum distributions (RMDs) have not begun. This conversion window is when the brackets are emptiest and conversions cheapest.

Required minimum distributionwhen it starts
What it is
A yearly withdrawal the law requires from traditional IRAs and most workplace plans. The applicable age is 73 for people who reach 72 after 2022 and 73 before 2033, and 75 for people who reach 74 after 2032 (26 U.S.C. §401(a)(9)(C)(v)). The amount is the prior year-end balance divided by a life-expectancy factor: 26.5 at age 73 and 24.6 at 75 in the Uniform Lifetime Table (IRS Publication 590-B).
Used for
Making sure tax-deferred money is eventually taxed.
What it means for you
RMDs cannot be converted: the year's required amount must come out first (Publication 590-A). Converting before RMDs begin shrinks them for life.

Without conversions, the Harpers' pre-tax savings would grow, in the planner's assumptions, to about $1.96 million by 75, making the first RMD about $82,800 in today's dollars. Added to $75,600 of Social Security, that would put them in the 22% bracket for the rest of their lives. Converting during the window is how they empty those later brackets.

Going furtherSocial Security timing and the window

Delaying Social Security keeps the window open longer and raises the benefit (INV-078), but the retiree must live on savings meanwhile. Many plans combine the two: spend from taxable savings, convert up to a chosen bracket, and claim Social Security later. The trade-offs are household-specific; Babak's Social Security Family Planner in the Resources tab models claiming ages.

The Medicare surcharge

Two rules make IRMAA a planning problem. First, the Social Security Administration uses "your most recent federal tax return the IRS provides," which for 2026 premiums is generally the 2024 return: income two years earlier. Second, your MAGI for this purpose is your adjusted gross income plus tax-exempt interest. A Roth conversion at 63 therefore raises premiums at 65.

Going furtherLife-changing events

If your income falls because of certain events, you can ask Social Security to use a more recent year. Its list includes marriage, divorce or a spouse's death; you or your spouse stopping or reducing work; loss of income-producing property; and certain pension plan terminations or settlements. The request uses form SSA-44 with documentation such as a death certificate or an employer's letter about your retirement. A voluntary Roth conversion is not on the list.

Practitioner depthIs paying IRMAA ever worth it?

Sometimes. A tier-1 surcharge costs $81.20 a month for Part B plus $14.50 for Part D: $1,148.40 a year per person. If crossing the threshold lets you convert, say, $40,000 more at 22% instead of paying 24% or 32% on it later, the surcharge is a small price. The planner below counts IRMAA explicitly. What rarely makes sense is crossing a threshold by a few hundred dollars.

The mechanics

Two separate five-year clocks, and no undo button

Roth five-year rulestwo different clocks
What it is
Clock 1, for tax-free earnings: a Roth IRA distribution is "qualified," and fully tax-free, only if it comes after the 5-year period beginning with the first tax year for which you made any contribution to a Roth IRA, and you are 59½, disabled, a beneficiary, or using up to $10,000 for a first home. Clock 2, for each conversion: if you are under 59½ and withdraw converted money within 5 years starting January 1 of the conversion year, the taxable part of the conversion can face the 10% additional tax. Each conversion has its own clock (Publication 590-B).
Used for
Stopping people from converting and withdrawing at once to dodge the early-withdrawal tax.
What it means for you
After 59½, clock 2 no longer matters, and clock 1 is met once any Roth IRA has been open five tax years. Opening a small Roth IRA early starts clock 1.

Other rules that matter

  • No recharacterization. Since 2018 a conversion cannot be undone (P.L. 115-97, section 13611; Publication 590-A). Convert late in the year, when income is known, or in stages.
  • Ordering rules. Roth IRA withdrawals come out in a fixed order: regular contributions first, then conversions on a first-in, first-out basis (taxable part first), then earnings (Publication 590-B).
  • The pro-rata rule. If any of your traditional IRAs hold after-tax (nondeductible) money, each conversion is part taxable and part not, in proportion to all your traditional, SEP and SIMPLE IRA balances (INV-032).
  • RMDs first. In a year you must take an RMD, that amount cannot be converted (Publication 590-A).
  • Withholding and estimates. Conversions have no tax withheld unless you elect it; plan estimated payments (Publication 505).

How the law shaped conversions

Three changes that rewrote the rules

Case study 12010: conversions opened to everyone
What happened
The Tax Increase Prevention and Reconciliation Act of 2005, signed in 2006, repealed the income limit on Roth conversions for years after 2009. It also let taxpayers who converted in 2010 spread the income ratably over 2011 and 2012 unless they elected otherwise.
What it teaches
Anyone can now convert, whatever their income. That also created the "backdoor Roth" route for high earners who cannot contribute directly (INV-032).
Source
Public Law 109-222, section 512 (2006).
Case study 22018: conversions became permanent decisions
What happened
Until 2017, a taxpayer could "recharacterize" a conversion — undo it — as late as the extended filing deadline, for example if the market fell after converting. The 2017 tax law repealed that option for conversions made in tax years beginning after December 31, 2017.
What it teaches
Convert only amounts you are sure of. Converting in December, once the year's income is known, or in several smaller steps, replaces the old safety valve.
Source
Public Law 115-97, section 13611 (2017); IRS Publication 590-A, "No recharacterizations of conversions made in 2018 or later."
Case study 32025: the rates stopped expiring
What happened
Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025, made the individual rate brackets permanent, added a $6,000 deduction for each person 65 or older for 2025 through 2028 (reduced by 6% of modified AGI above $75,000, or $150,000 joint), and raised the state and local tax deduction limit.
What it teaches
Conversion decisions now depend on each household's own future rates rather than a scheduled tax increase. For people 65 to 68 in 2026, the temporary senior deduction makes 2025–2028 conversions slightly cheaper at modest incomes and slightly dearer in the phase-out range.
Source
Public Law 119-21, sections 70103 and 70120; IRS, "Working Families Tax Cuts provisions"; IRS Publication 15 (2026).

Decisions

How much, and for whom?

Scenario 1 · The Harpers

Their advisor proposes converting $435,750 a year for three years after they retire, filling the 24% bracket, "to get it done before Medicare." What should they weigh?

Big and fastIn the planner, the 24% plan adds about $232,000 at 92 if heirs pay 24%, but loses about $86,000 if heirs pay nothing, and triggers IRMAA. It pays more tax than necessary.
Compare plansRight. Filling the 12% bracket every year from 62 to 74 converts a similar total and comes out ahead under both heir assumptions (+$515,000 or +$235,000). The Harpers should also add North Carolina's 3.99% and check the 0% gain room they would give up (INV-037).
Skip themWithout conversions their first RMD would be about $82,800, keeping them in the 22% bracket for life and leaving their son a large pre-tax balance to empty within 10 years.
The principle: convert where your effective marginal rate is lowest over the whole window, not fastest.
Scenario 2 · Ruth

Ruth, 68 and single, reads that conversions are "always smart before RMDs." Her daughter Anna is a nurse in a similar bracket. Should Ruth convert, and how much?

Fill the 22% bracketEach of those conversions costs about 23.3% federal, pushes her modified AGI to about $127,000 (IRMAA tier 1 two years later), and uses up her CDs paying the tax. The planner shows only about $4,000 of benefit by 92.
Modest or noneRight. Because Social Security taxation turns her 12% bracket into 22.2%, even small conversions are roughly break-even for her own taxes; they help mainly if Anna's rate will be higher. Pennsylvania does not tax her retirement income either way. QCDs from age 70½ (INV-039) may do more for her.
The principle: rules of thumb about conversions fail when hidden rates are large; compute the effective rate.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A married couple under 65 has $20,000 of other ordinary income in 2026 and takes the standard deduction. How much can they convert and stay within the 12% bracket, in dollars?

Room = bracket top + deduction − other income = $100,800 + $32,200 − $20,000 = $113,000.
Exercise 2

Ruth is in the 12% bracket, and each extra dollar of income makes $0.85 more of her Social Security taxable. What is her effective marginal rate on a conversion, in percent?

Each converted dollar adds $1.85 of taxable income at 12%: 0.12 × 1.85 = 22.2%.
Exercise 3

A single filer's 2024 modified AGI was $110,000. What extra amount will they pay for Medicare Part B and Part D in 2026, per year, in dollars?

Tier 1: Part B $81.20 and Part D $14.50 a month above standard: 12 × $95.70 = $1,148.40.
Exercise 4

An IRA owner turning 73 had $1,000,000 at the end of the prior year. What is the first RMD, in dollars?

$1,000,000 ÷ 26.5 = $37,735.85.
