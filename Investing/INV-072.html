The Math of Financial Independence

Financial independence is the point at which your investments, not your paycheck, can pay for the life you live. The arithmetic behind it is short enough to fit on an index card. This module builds it from first principles, shows how much the answer moves when the assumptions change, and explains why spending matters more than income.

The question this module answersHow much do I need to stop depending on a paycheck, and how long will it take to get there?

Start here

A paycheck you pay yourself

Most people already understand the goal. Work pays the bills today; retirement savings are supposed to pay them later. Financial independence (FI) simply asks when "later" can begin, and it answers with two numbers you control: how much you spend, and what share of your income you save.

For most households the ordinary path is fine: save steadily in a workplace plan, collect Social Security, and retire in your sixties. Nothing in this module says you must do otherwise. But the same arithmetic that sizes a traditional retirement also tells you what it would take to have the option earlier — to change careers, work part-time, care for family, or stop. Figure 72.1 is the whole idea in one line.

💡

Two levers, one of which pulls twice. Saving more adds to the pile. Spending less also shrinks the pile you need. That double effect is why the curve is so steep at low savings rates and why this module spends a whole tab on spending.

What this module covers

  1. The FI number: spending divided by a withdrawal rate, and where the famous "25 times" comes from.
  2. Savings rate math: the classic years-to-FI formula, derived step by step, with a calculator.
  3. Sensitivity: what happens when returns or the withdrawal rate differ from the assumption, using 98 years of US data.
  4. The role of spending: why a dollar not spent is worth more than a dollar earned.
  5. What the formula leaves out: taxes, health insurance, Social Security and the order of returns.

The target

How big does the pile need to be?

Start with a question you can answer today: how much does your household spend in a year? Everything else follows from that figure, because the portfolio only has to replace what you spend, not what you earn. Taxes you pay on a salary, payroll taxes, commuting and the money you save all disappear from the calculation once you stop working — though taxes on withdrawals do not, as the last tab explains.

Withdrawal ratea percentage
What it is
The share of a portfolio taken out in the first year of retirement. Under the classic rule, later withdrawals stay the same in real terms: the dollar amount rises each year with inflation, whatever markets do.
Used for
Converting a portfolio into a sustainable income, or, in reverse, converting a spending level into a portfolio target.
What it means for you
A 4% withdrawal rate on $1,000,000 means $40,000 in year one, then $40,000 adjusted for inflation every year after. The lower the rate, the larger the portfolio you need and the safer the plan.
FI numberyour target portfolio
What it is
The portfolio that would support your annual spending at a chosen withdrawal rate. At 4%, it is 25 times spending, because 1 ÷ 0.04 = 25.
Used for
Setting a savings target and measuring progress: your portfolio divided by your FI number is the share of the way you have come.
What it means for you
If you spend $50,000 a year, a 4% rate implies $1.25 million; a more cautious 3.5% implies about $1.43 million. The choice of rate is the single biggest judgment in the target.

Where does 4% come from? In 1994 the financial planner William Bengen tested every retirement start year in the US record from 1926 and found that a first-year withdrawal of 4%, raised with inflation afterward, from a portfolio of half stocks and half intermediate-term Treasuries, never ran out in fewer than 33 years. A 1998 study by three Trinity University professors (the Trinity study) reached similar conclusions using data from 1926 to 1995. Both studies were about 30-year retirements. INV-073 shows what happens over 50 or 60 years.

Going furtherSubtract guaranteed income first

The portfolio only needs to cover spending that no other source pays. If you will eventually receive Social Security or a pension, the long-run target is smaller:

FI number (after benefits begin) = (annual spending − guaranteed income) ÷ withdrawal rate

With $60,000 of spending and $24,000 a year of expected Social Security, the long-run target at 4% is ($60,000 − $24,000) ÷ 0.04 = $900,000, not $1.5 million. The catch for early retirees is timing: Social Security cannot start before 62, so the years before benefits need their own money. That bridge is the subject of INV-074, and Social Security itself of INV-078.

Practitioner depthGross up for taxes

"Spending" in the formula must include the income tax you will owe on withdrawals. If you expect about 10% of each withdrawal to go to tax, a $50,000 lifestyle needs $50,000 ÷ (1 − 0.10) = $55,556 of withdrawals, and the FI number at 4% becomes about $1.39 million. How much tax you pay depends heavily on which accounts the money sits in (Roth, pre-tax or taxable), which is why Stage 5 and INV-042 matter so much to early retirees.

Build the calculator

From savings rate to years, step by step

The classic years-to-FI calculation, popularized in 2012 by the blogger Mr. Money Mustache, has four ingredients and needs no knowledge of your income at all. Here is how to build it yourself.

Savings ratethe key input
What it is
The share of your after-tax income that you save and invest rather than spend. The cleanest definition for this calculation is: savings ÷ (savings + spending).
Used for
Measuring how fast you are converting income into independence.
What it means for you
Include every dollar that goes to investments, including 401(k) contributions and any employer match, and count every dollar you spend. Consistency matters more than the exact convention.

The four steps

  1. Spending. With take-home pay I and savings rate s, you spend (1 − s) × I.
  2. Target. The FI number is T = (1 − s) × I ÷ w, where w is the withdrawal rate.
  3. Growth. Saving S = s × I at the end of each year, with a real return r, a starting portfolio P0 grows to Pn = P0(1 + r)n + S[(1 + r)n − 1] ÷ r after n years (the future value of a growing pile plus the future value of a series of deposits, from INV-002).
  4. Solve. Set Pn = T and solve for n using logarithms.
n = ln[(T·r + S) ÷ (P0·r + S)] ÷ ln(1 + r)

Starting from zero (P0 = 0) and substituting T and S, income cancels completely:

n = ln[1 + r(1 − s) ÷ (s · w)] ÷ ln(1 + r)

Worked example. Save half your take-home pay (s = 0.50), earn 5% real (r = 0.05), plan on a 4% withdrawal rate (w = 0.04). Then r(1 − s) ÷ (s·w) = 0.025 ÷ 0.02 = 1.25, so n = ln(2.25) ÷ ln(1.05) = 0.8109 ÷ 0.04879 = 16.6 years. That matches the roughly 17 years in Mr. Money Mustache's 2012 table, which used the same 5% and 4% assumptions.

Going furtherWhy the first points of savings rate are worth the most

Moving from 10% to 20% cuts about 14.7 years from the timeline (51.4 to 36.7). Moving from 40% to 50% cuts 5.0 years, and 60% to 70% cuts 3.6. Each extra point matters less in years because the timeline is already short, but in proportion it matters just as much: every step both raises contributions and lowers the target. For someone saving 5% or less, the first few points are the most valuable financial decision available.

Practitioner depthAssumptions hidden in the formula

The formula assumes a constant real return, a constant savings rate, contributions at each year-end (contributing at the start of each year shortens the timeline slightly), and that spending before and after FI is the same. It works entirely in today's dollars, which is why the return must be real: a 7% nominal return with 2.5% inflation is a real return of 1.07 ÷ 1.025 − 1 = 4.39%, not 4.5%. A portfolio you already hold shortens the timeline and reintroduces income into the answer, because P0 is measured in dollars, not as a share of pay.

How sure is the answer?

Sensitivity to return and withdrawal rate

The formula gives one number, but its two market assumptions are guesses. Real returns over any 15 to 20 years have varied enormously, and the withdrawal rate is a judgment about the future. Use the grid below to see how far the answer moves.

At a 50% savings rate, the base case is 16.6 years. A pessimistic pairing, 2% real returns with a 3% withdrawal rate, stretches it to 25.8 years; an optimistic pairing, 7% with 5%, shrinks it to 12.9. At a 20% savings rate the same range runs from 27.9 to 65.6 years around a base of 36.7. The lower your savings rate, the more your timeline depends on markets. A high savings rate is a hedge against bad luck.

Real returnafter inflation
What it is
The growth of your purchasing power: (1 + nominal return) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1.
Used for
Every calculation in this module, because the FI number is stated in today's dollars.
What it means for you
A saver who happened to work from 1965 to 1981 earned almost nothing after inflation on a 75/25 mix, while one who worked from 1982 to 1998 earned almost 13% a year. You will not know which kind of period you are in until it is over.
Going furtherWhich assumption matters more?

At a 50% savings rate and 5% returns, moving the withdrawal rate from 4% to 3.5% raises the target by 14% (28.6 times spending instead of 25) and adds 1.6 years (16.6 to 18.2). Moving to 3% adds 3.5 years (to 20.1). Moving the return from 5% to 4% at a 4% withdrawal rate adds 1.1 years (to 17.7). The withdrawal rate is the assumption you choose; the return is the one the market chooses for you. Choose the former conservatively, because you cannot choose the latter at all.

Practitioner depthAccumulation risk and retirement risk are linked

Poor returns while you save lengthen the timeline, but they also tend to leave markets cheaper when you start withdrawing, which historically helped later retirees. The reverse is also true: a saver who reaches the FI number quickly because of a boom retires into a market that may be expensive. INV-006 introduced valuation measures such as the cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings (CAPE) ratio; some planners lower the withdrawal rate when valuations are high. The evidence is debated, and INV-076 and INV-077 cover it.

The strongest lever

Why a dollar not spent beats a dollar earned

Suppose your take-home pay is $100,000 and you save 20%: you spend $80,000 and, from zero, reach FI in 36.7 years (5% real, 4% withdrawal). Compare three changes of the same size.

The raise that is fully saved puts more dollars into the portfolio ($30,000 versus $28,000) but the spending cut still wins, because it also lowers the target from $2.0 million to $1.8 million. The raise that is fully spent changes nothing: spending and savings grow in proportion, so the target grows exactly as fast as the savings that feed it.

Lifestyle inflationalso called lifestyle creep
What it is
The tendency for spending to rise as income rises, so that raises are absorbed by a more expensive way of life rather than saved.
Used for
Explaining why many high earners are no closer to financial independence than moderate earners.
What it means for you
A simple defense is to decide in advance what share of each raise you will save, and to raise your automatic 401(k) or IRA contribution the same month the raise starts.
Each $1 of permanent annual spending adds 1 ÷ w dollars to the FI number: $25 at 4%, about $28.57 at 3.5%

That conversion rate makes everyday choices concrete. A $150 monthly bundle of subscriptions is $1,800 a year, or $45,000 of FI number at 4%. A $500-a-month car payment that becomes permanent (because the next car is always financed) is $6,000 a year, or $150,000. None of this says you should not spend on what you value; it says every recurring cost has a price measured in years.

Going furtherMeasure spending honestly

The most common error in FI planning is underestimating spending. Average your actual outflows over at least 12 months, and add the irregular items that do not appear every month: car replacement, roof and appliance repairs, travel, gifts, insurance deductibles. Early retirees must add costs an employer used to pay, above all health insurance (INV-074). INV-018 shows how to build a cash-flow record from bank and card statements.

Practitioner depthSpending changes over a life

Spending is rarely flat. A mortgage ends, children leave, health costs rise, travel falls off in late life. A single "spending" figure multiplied by 25 is a starting point; a serious plan models phases. Babak's Retirement Planner lets you enter multiple spending phases and test them with Monte Carlo and historical sequences, which is the natural next step once the simple formula has given you a ballpark.

What it leaves out

Six things the index-card math ignores

Social Security deserves a closer look, because early retirement changes it. The Social Security Administration computes benefits from "average indexed monthly earnings": it takes up to 35 years of a worker's wage-indexed earnings, chooses the highest years, and divides their total by the number of months in those years. Years without earnings still fill out the 35 if you have fewer good years, so someone who stops work at 45 after 22 years of earnings will average in 13 years of zero and a smaller benefit than the statement they received at 40 might suggest if it projected continued work. Download your record at my Social Security and ask how the estimate assumes you keep working.

Crossover pointa progress milestone
What it is
The moment when a safe withdrawal from your portfolio (for example, 4% of its value) first equals your annual spending. It is the FI number seen from the other side.
Used for
Tracking progress: many FI planners chart their "portfolio income" rising toward their spending line each year.
What it means for you
Reaching the crossover does not oblige you to stop working. It changes work from a necessity into a choice, which is the practical meaning of financial independence.
Going furtherDeterministic math versus a range of outcomes

Everything in this module is deterministic: one return, one answer. Real plans face a range of possible paths. Monte Carlo simulation (INV-077) and historical backtests replace the single return with thousands of possible sequences and report how often a plan succeeds. The simple formula is still useful, because it tells you which inputs matter; the simulation tells you how much uncertainty surrounds them.

Practitioner depthA "perpetual" withdrawal rate

A 30-year rule is designed to spend the portfolio down if returns are poor. An early retiree who wants the principal to last indefinitely needs a withdrawal rate at or below the long-run real return the portfolio can reliably earn after fees. Mr. Money Mustache's 2012 article described a portfolio meant to last forever, in which "you'll only be touching the gains"; history suggests that is optimistic for 50-year spans, as INV-073 shows with the data.

Where the numbers come from

Three sources of the FI arithmetic

Case study 1Bengen's 1994 withdrawal study
What happened
William Bengen, a planner in California, tested inflation-adjusted withdrawals against every US starting year using Ibbotson Associates' data from 1926. A 50/50 portfolio of stocks and intermediate-term Treasuries with a 4% first-year withdrawal never ran out in less than 33 years; 4.25% could have run out in 28. A 3% rate, and rates up to about 3.5%, always lasted at least 50 years. He advised stock allocations between 50% and 75%.
What it teaches
The "multiply by 25" rule came from a 30-year retirement problem. Bengen's own charts show that longer horizons call for lower rates, a point FI planners sometimes skip.
Source
Bengen, W. P. (1994, October). Determining withdrawal rates using historical data. Journal of Financial Planning; reprinted in the Journal, March 2004.
Case study 2"The shockingly simple math behind early retirement" (2012)
What happened
In January 2012 the blogger known as Mr. Money Mustache published a table of years to retirement by savings rate. It assumed 5% investment returns after inflation and a 4% withdrawal rate, and it became a founding text of the FIRE (financial independence, retire early) movement.
What it teaches
The table is exactly the formula built in this module; our 16.6 years at a 50% savings rate matches its roughly 17. Its power is the reframing: the savings rate, not income or investment skill, drives the timeline.
Source
Mr. Money Mustache (2012, January 13). The shockingly simple math behind early retirement [Blog post]. A popular source, used here for its assumptions, which we reproduce and test.
Case study 3The 1966 retiree, computed from the data
What happened
A retiree with $1 million in a 75/25 mix of the S&P 500 and 10-year Treasuries, taking $40,000 at the end of 1966 and raising it with inflation, ran out after 35 withdrawals. Taking the same withdrawals at the start of each year, which is how many people actually live, the money lasted 26 years. A 1982 retiree following the same rule ended 30 years later with about $6.1 million in 1982 dollars.
What it teaches
The same rule produced ruin and a fortune, depending on the start year. High inflation and weak real returns early in retirement (1966–1981) are the worst case in the US record.
Source
Computed from Damodaran, A. (2026), Historical returns on stocks, bonds and bills: 1928–2025, NYU Stern. Before taxes and fees.

Decisions

Choose the lever

Scenario 1 · Jordan

Jordan takes home about $48,000 a year (an illustrative figure) and saves 10%. A coworker says she should switch her 401(k) to an aggressive fund that "should earn 7% after inflation" instead of raising her savings. What should she do?

Chase the returnEven if 7% arrives, the timeline falls only to about 41 years, and a 7% real return is not something she can choose. Figure 72.4 shows 17-year windows as low as −0.8%.
Raise the savings rateRight. Doubling the savings rate cuts the timeline to about 36 years at the same 5% assumption, and it is entirely in her control. Her employer matches 50% of contributions up to 6% of pay, so contributing at least 6% is the first step (INV-022).
Wait for the loansHer 6.8% loans deserve attention (INV-020), but skipping the employer match gives up an immediate 50% return on those dollars. Paying down loans and capturing the match can happen at once.
The principle: pull the lever you control; treat the return as weather.
Scenario 2 · The Riveras

The Riveras take home roughly $114,000 (illustrative) and save 20% with $62,000 already invested. They also have $41,000 sitting idle in checking beyond their $9,000 emergency fund. Which change helps their FI timeline most?

Invest the idle cash onlyHelpful: adding $41,000 to their $62,000 moves the timeline from 34.1 to about 32.5 years at 5% real. Cash in checking loses purchasing power every year.
BothRight. The one-time deposit is worth about a year and a half; raising the savings rate to 25% is worth roughly four more years (34.1 to 29.8 from their current $62,000), because it works every year and lowers the target. A 529 plan for Sofia and Leo competes for the same dollars and should be decided at the same time (INV-034).
The principle: a lump sum helps once; a higher savings rate helps every year.
Scenario 3 · The Harpers

Tom (57) and Rachel (55) plan to retire around 62–63, a retirement that could last 30 years or more. A friend says they should use 4% "because that's the rule." What rate should they plan with?

Exactly 4%Reasonable for about 30 years in the US record, but it was the historical worst case boundary, not a comfortable middle. Their horizon, taxes on $820,000 of pre-tax money and a 1.25% advisory fee all argue for testing lower.
A range, testedRight. A range turns one fragile number into a plan with a margin. Their Social Security, which can begin at 62 but grows until 70, reduces the rate the portfolio must bear later (INV-078).
5%Portfolio size does not change the percentage that is sustainable. In the data used here, a 5% rate failed in 16% to 23% of 30-year start years for a 75/25 mix, depending on whether withdrawals come at the end or the start of each year.
The principle: the withdrawal rate is the assumption you pick; pick it with a margin.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A household spends $44,000 a year. What is its FI number at a 4% withdrawal rate, in dollars?

$44,000 ÷ 0.04 = $1,100,000, or 25 × $44,000.
Exercise 2

Same household, but it plans with a 3.5% withdrawal rate. What is the FI number, in dollars?

$44,000 ÷ 0.035 = $1,257,143 — about $157,000 (14%) more than at 4%.
Exercise 3

Starting from zero, how many years to FI at a 40% savings rate, 5% real return and 4% withdrawal rate?

r(1 − s) ÷ (s·w) = 0.05 × 0.60 ÷ (0.40 × 0.04) = 0.03 ÷ 0.016 = 1.875. n = ln(2.875) ÷ ln(1.05) = 1.0561 ÷ 0.04879 = 21.6 years.
Exercise 4

You cancel $150 a month of subscriptions for good. By how much does your FI number fall at a 4% withdrawal rate, in dollars?

$150 × 12 = $1,800 a year; × 25 = $45,000. The $1,800 a year you now save also shortens the timeline.
