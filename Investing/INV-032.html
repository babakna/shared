IRAs and Roth IRAs

An individual retirement account is the tax-advantaged account you open yourself, at any brokerage, with any investments you like. The rules are short to state and easy to trip over: who can deduct, who can contribute to a Roth, the two different five-year clocks, and the pro-rata rule that decides what a "backdoor" Roth really costs.

The question this module answersCan I use an IRA or Roth IRA in 2026, which one, and how do I avoid the traps?

Start here

The retirement account you open yourself

A workplace plan (INV-031) comes with a job. An individual retirement account (IRA) comes with nothing but earned income: anyone who works, or whose spouse works, can open one at a brokerage in a few minutes and invest in almost any fund.

For many people an IRA is a second step: after collecting the full employer match, the next dollars often go into a Roth IRA because it offers low-cost fund choices and unusual flexibility. For people without a workplace plan it is the first step. And for almost everyone who changes jobs, it is where old 401(k) money lands. The two kinds work like the two kinds of 401(k): a traditional IRA may give a deduction now and taxes withdrawals; a Roth IRA gives no deduction and qualified withdrawals are tax-free. What makes IRAs trickier is that income limits decide who may deduct and who may contribute to a Roth.

Nondeductible IRA contributionafter-tax basis
What it is
A traditional IRA contribution you do not deduct, because your income is too high or you choose not to. The amount becomes basis: money already taxed, which comes out tax-free later. You report it on Form 8606 every year you make one.
Used for
Mostly as step one of a backdoor Roth. Left in a traditional IRA, it still grows tax-deferred, but the growth is taxed as ordinary income on withdrawal.
What it means for you
Keep every Form 8606. Without it you may be unable to prove the basis and pay tax twice on the same money.
Spousal IRA
What it is
For a married couple filing jointly, a spouse with little or no earnings can contribute to an IRA in their own name based on the couple's combined compensation (IRS Publication 590-A, "Kay Bailey Hutchison Spousal IRA").
Used for
Letting a stay-at-home or part-time spouse build retirement savings of their own.
What it means for you
A one-earner couple can put $15,000 into two IRAs for 2026, or $17,200 if both are 50 or older.

Tax-free growth

Who can contribute, and how money comes out

A Roth IRA contribution is not deductible, but qualified withdrawals, growth included, are tax-free, and the original owner never has to take required distributions. Direct contributions are limited by modified AGI (Figure 32.1). The calculator checks both the Roth limit and the traditional deduction for 2026.

The defaults are the Harpers': married filing jointly, both covered by 401(k)s, MAGI of about $205,000. Many couples at their income assume they are "too rich" for a Roth IRA. They are not: $205,000 is below the $242,000 start of the joint phase-out, so each can contribute $8,600 directly for 2026. They are, however, above the $149,000 limit for deducting a traditional IRA.

The ordering rules: why Roth contributions are flexible

Money leaving a Roth IRA is treated as coming out in a fixed order, across all your Roth IRAs combined (IRS Publication 590-B):

  1. Regular contributions first. These can always be withdrawn tax-free and penalty-free, at any age, because you already paid tax on them.
  2. Conversion and rollover amounts next, oldest first, taxable part before nontaxable part. These can carry the 10% additional tax if withdrawn within five years and before 59½.
  3. Earnings last. Tax-free only in a qualified distribution; otherwise taxable and usually subject to the 10% additional tax before 59½.
Qualified distribution
What it is
A Roth IRA withdrawal made after the five-year period described in the next tab and on or after age 59½, because of disability, to a beneficiary after death, or for a first home (up to $10,000 lifetime).
Used for
Deciding whether earnings come out tax-free.
What it means for you
Before you qualify, you can still take out your contributions freely. That is why some families treat Roth contributions as a backup emergency or education fund, though money taken out cannot be put back.

Two different clocks

The Roth five-year rules

"The five-year rule" is really two rules with different purposes. Confusing them is one of the most common Roth mistakes.

Clock 1: the qualified-distribution rule
What it is
Earnings are tax-free only if five tax years have passed since January 1 of the first year for which you contributed to any Roth IRA, and you meet a qualifying event such as age 59½.
Used for
Preventing a quick in-and-out to harvest tax-free growth.
What it means for you
Start the clock early. Even a small Roth IRA contribution in your 20s or 30s means the clock is long satisfied by retirement. A Roth 401(k)'s clock is separate from a Roth IRA's.
Clock 2: the conversion rule
What it is
Each conversion has its own five-year period. Withdrawing the converted amount before that period ends and before age 59½ triggers the 10% additional tax on the part that was taxable at conversion.
Used for
Stopping people under 59½ from converting and withdrawing at once to avoid the early-withdrawal tax.
What it means for you
After 59½ this clock no longer matters. It is central to the "Roth conversion ladder" used by early retirees (INV-078).
Practitioner depthInherited Roth IRAs

Beneficiaries inherit the original owner's five-year clock. If it has not run out, earnings withdrawn by the beneficiary are taxable, though the 10% additional tax does not apply to distributions after death. Most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty an inherited Roth IRA within ten years (INV-095).

Roth conversion
What it is
Moving money from a traditional account into a Roth account and paying ordinary income tax on the pre-tax part in the year of the conversion.
Used for
Filling low brackets in low-income years, such as early retirement, and shrinking future required distributions.
What it means for you
Converting is a bet that today's rate is lower than the rate the money would otherwise face. INV-038 covers when and how much.

For higher earners

Backdoor Roth, mega backdoor Roth, and the pro-rata rule

Because conversions have no income limit, a person above the Roth IRA income range can still get money into a Roth IRA in two steps. This is the backdoor Roth IRA:

  1. Make a nondeductible contribution to a traditional IRA ($7,500, or $8,600 at 50+, for 2026) and report it on Form 8606.
  2. Convert it to a Roth IRA, usually soon after, so there is little growth to tax.

If that traditional IRA were the only one you owned, the conversion would be almost entirely tax-free. But the law does not let you choose which dollars you convert.

Pro-rata rule
What it is
For tax purposes, all your traditional, SEP and SIMPLE IRAs are treated as one account. Any conversion or withdrawal is made up of pre-tax and after-tax money in proportion to their shares of the total, measured using the value of all those IRAs on December 31 of the conversion year (Form 8606).
Used for
Figuring the taxable part of every IRA conversion and distribution when you have any basis.
What it means for you
Pre-tax money sitting in a rollover IRA makes a backdoor Roth mostly taxable. Workplace 401(k), 403(b) and 457(b) balances are not counted, so rolling pre-tax IRA money into your employer's plan before December 31, if the plan accepts it, can fix the problem.
nontaxable share = basis ÷ (Dec 31 value of all traditional, SEP, SIMPLE IRAs + amount converted during the year)

A worked example

Consider a hypothetical saver, Sam, 48, in the 24% bracket. Sam has $92,500 of pre-tax money in a rollover IRA from an old job, makes a $7,500 nondeductible contribution for 2026, and converts $7,500 to a Roth. At year-end the rollover IRA is still worth $92,500. The nontaxable share is 7,500 ÷ (92,500 + 7,500) = 7.5%. Of the $7,500 converted, only $562.50 is tax-free; $6,937.50 is taxable, about $1,665 of federal tax, and $6,937.50 of basis stays behind in the traditional IRA. Had Sam rolled the $92,500 into a current employer's 401(k) first, the conversion would have been fully tax-free.

Mega backdoor Roth
What it is
Using a workplace plan's after-tax, non-Roth contribution option to fill the gap between your deferrals plus employer money and the $72,000 total-additions limit for 2026, then moving those after-tax dollars to Roth through an in-plan conversion or an in-service rollover to a Roth IRA.
Used for
High savers who have already maxed their deferrals and want far more Roth money than an IRA allows.
What it means for you
It works only if your plan allows both after-tax contributions and a way to convert or withdraw them while employed. Under IRS Notice 2014-54, a single distribution can send the after-tax dollars to a Roth IRA and the pre-tax earnings on them to a traditional IRA; the IRS says a partial distribution must include a proportional share of pre-tax money.

Real law

How the Roth IRA got its rules

Case study 1The Taxpayer Relief Act of 1997
What happened
Section 408A, the Roth IRA, was added to the Internal Revenue Code by the Taxpayer Relief Act of 1997 (P.L. 105-34, signed August 5, 1997). It created an IRA with no deduction but tax-free qualified withdrawals, income limits on contributions, and a five-year holding rule.
What it teaches
The Roth idea is not new, and its core features have been stable for nearly three decades. What changed since are the limits and who can reach it.
Source
26 U.S.C. §408A and notes (Legal Information Institute).
Case study 22010: the conversion income limit disappears
What happened
Until 2009, no one with AGI over $100,000, and no married person filing separately, could convert a traditional IRA to a Roth. The Tax Increase Prevention and Reconciliation Act of 2005 (P.L. 109-222, May 17, 2006) struck that rule for tax years beginning after December 31, 2009.
What it teaches
Removing the conversion limit while keeping the contribution limit created the backdoor Roth: contribute nondeductibly, then convert. It is legal and widely used; the pro-rata rule is the price of admission.
Source
26 U.S.C. §408A statutory notes on the 2006 amendment (Legal Information Institute).
Case study 32018: no more undoing a conversion
What happened
Before 2018 you could "recharacterize" a Roth conversion back to a traditional IRA by the tax-filing deadline, useful if the market fell after converting. For conversions made in tax years beginning after December 31, 2017, that option no longer exists; regular contributions can still be recharacterized.
What it teaches
A conversion is now final. Size it carefully, and consider converting in several smaller steps rather than one large one.
Source
IRS Publication 590-A (2025), "No recharacterizations of conversions made in 2018 or later".

Decisions

Three IRA decisions

Scenario 1 · The Harpers

A friend tells the Harpers they need a backdoor Roth because "you make too much for a Roth IRA." Their 2026 MAGI will be about $205,000. What should they do?

BackdoorIt would work, since their pre-tax money is in 401(k)s rather than IRAs, but it is an unnecessary extra step and extra paperwork.
Direct Roth contributionsRight. $17,200 a year of new Roth money, on top of their $60,000 of Roth IRAs, adds tax diversification before retirement. If a big stock sale ever lifts their MAGI above $242,000, the backdoor route is the fallback.
Skip itAt $17,200 a year for the six years to retirement, the tax-free growth is meaningful, especially given their large pre-tax balances.
The principle: check the actual 2026 phase-out before assuming you need the back door.
Scenario 2 · The Riveras

Both Riveras are covered by 401(k)s, and their MAGI is about $136,300. They have $41,000 idle in checking. Traditional or Roth IRAs?

TraditionalTheir deduction is phased: $7,500 × (149,000 − 136,300) ÷ 20,000 = $4,762.50, rounded up to $4,770 each. The rest would be nondeductible, which adds Form 8606 complexity.
RothRight, for most families like them. The full $7,500 each is allowed. Part of the idle cash could fund both Roth IRAs for 2026, with contributions still withdrawable if they needed them.
The principle: when the deduction is partial and your bracket is modest, Roth contributions are usually simpler and at least as good.
Scenario 3 · Denise

After her 6% 401(k) contribution, Denise can save another $2,000 this year. Emergency fund first, Roth IRA, or more 401(k)?

Emergency fundA sound default (INV-019): $3,000 is less than two months of expenses.
Roth IRA as a hybridAlso defensible: contributions can be withdrawn tax- and penalty-free, so a Roth IRA can double as a second-line emergency reserve, though withdrawn contributions cannot be replaced later.
More 401(k)Fine eventually, but money in a 401(k) is hard to reach without tax and the 10% additional tax, which matters when her cushion is thin.
The principle: liquidity first; a Roth IRA's contribution basis is the most flexible retirement money there is.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A single filer, age 35, has 2026 MAGI of $160,500. What is the maximum direct Roth IRA contribution, in dollars?

The single phase-out runs from $153,000 to $168,000. $7,500 × (168,000 − 160,500) ÷ 15,000 = $3,750, already a multiple of $10.
Exercise 2

Sam's basis is $7,500, the December 31 value of all traditional IRAs is $92,500, and $7,500 was converted during the year. What amount of the conversion is taxable, in dollars?

Nontaxable share = 7,500 ÷ (92,500 + 7,500) = 7.5%. Nontaxable part = $562.50; taxable = $6,937.50.
Exercise 3

Marcus Rivera is covered by a 401(k); the couple files jointly with MAGI of $136,300. How much of a $7,500 traditional IRA contribution can he deduct for 2026, in dollars?

$7,500 × (149,000 − 136,300) ÷ 20,000 = $4,762.50, rounded up to the next $10: $4,770.
Exercise 4

Denise opens her first Roth IRA in April 2027 and designates the contribution for tax year 2026. On January 1 of what year is her five-year clock for qualified distributions first satisfied?

The clock starts January 1 of the tax year the contribution is for, 2026, and is satisfied from January 1, 2031. (Earnings are then tax-free once she is also 59½.)
