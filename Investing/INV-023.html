Insurance and Protection

An investment plan can survive a bad market. It usually cannot survive a death without life insurance, a disability without income protection, a hospital stay without health coverage, or a lawsuit without liability coverage. This module shows how to size life insurance, why term is the default, what disability, health, property and umbrella coverage do, and why long-term care is the risk that matters most later in life.

The question this module answersWhich risks should I insure, how much coverage do I need, and which should I simply carry myself?

Start here

Insure the losses you could not recover from; carry the rest yourself

Everyone already owns some insurance: car insurance because the law requires it, health insurance through work, perhaps renter's insurance because a landlord asked. Most people buy it policy by policy, without a rule for what to insure and how much.

The rule is simple. Insurance is a bet you expect to lose: on average, premiums exceed claims, because insurers must pay costs and earn a profit. It is worth buying anyway for losses that are rare but ruinous, the ones that would wreck your finances for years. Small, frequent losses are cheaper to pay from savings; that is what the emergency fund (INV-019) and high deductibles are for.

Life insurance

How much life insurance does a family need?

Life insurance replaces what a person's death would take away from people who depend on them: their income, and the costs their death would bring forward (paying off a mortgage, funding college, final expenses). Someone with no dependents, like Denise, usually needs little beyond final expenses.

Three ways to estimate the need
What it is
Rule of thumb: a multiple of income, often quoted as about 10 times; a heuristic. Income replacement: the present value of the income the family would need for a set number of years. Needs analysis: the present value of the family's yearly shortfall after other income (such as Social Security survivor benefits), plus lump-sum needs (debts, education, final expenses), minus assets already available.
Used for
Choosing a coverage amount. The needs method is the most complete because it counts both what the family needs and what it already has.
What it means for you
All three are estimates; the goal is the right order of magnitude. Being $50,000 off matters far less than having no policy.
Needs = present value of (yearly need − survivor income) over the years of support, at the real rate + debts + education + final expenses − savings − existing coverage

The defaults are Marcus Rivera: $85,000 income, the family needing 60% of it for 18 years (until Leo is 22), about $1,500 a month of average survivor benefits for the children (illustrative; the real figure is on his Social Security statement), the $310,000 mortgage, $100,000 for two children's education, $15,000 of final expenses, $50,000 of family savings, and $85,000 of group life through work.

The needs method says Marcus should add about $785,000 of coverage; income replacement says about $680,000; the 10× rule, $765,000. They agree on the order of magnitude: the Riveras need roughly $700,000–$800,000 more on Marcus. The present-value factor for 18 years at a 2% real return is 14.99, so each $1,000 of yearly need adds about $15,000 of coverage.

Going furtherSocial Security survivor benefits

If a worker dies, Social Security can pay monthly benefits to unmarried children aged 17 and younger (18–19 if in grades K–12 full time) and, at any age, to a surviving spouse caring for the worker's child. In January 2026 the estimated average for a widowed mother and two children was $3,898 a month after the 2.8% cost-of-living adjustment. A surviving spouse who works can see part of their own benefit withheld under the earnings test ($24,480 a year under full retirement age in 2026); INV-078 covers the rules.

Going furtherA dependent who will need support for life

The usual needs analysis stops when the youngest child turns 22. Maya Walker's son Eli, 13, has autism with an intellectual disability and will likely need support for life, so the analysis changes in three ways. The horizon is decades, not years: at 50% of her $92,000 pay for 30 years, less about $1,700 a month of survivor benefits he would receive on her record (an assumption), the present value at a 2% real rate is $25,600 × 22.40 = about $573,000. With the $95,000 mortgage and $15,000 of final expenses, minus $70,000 of taxable savings and cash and her $92,000 of group life, the calculator gives about $521,000. Second, the money is meant to supplement public benefits, not replace them: SSI and Medicaid cover the basics, and the trust pays for what they do not. Third, the payee matters as much as the amount. A policy paid to Eli outright could cost him SSI and Medicaid; paid to a third-party supplemental needs trust, it does not (INV-102). A 20-year term policy covers her until 78; after that her retirement accounts, also payable to the trust, are meant to carry the plan.

Practitioner depthChoosing the discount rate

Discounting at a real (after-inflation) rate keeps the income in today's dollars. At 0% the Marcus needs figure rises to $884,000; at 2% it is about $785,000. A conservative family choosing safe investments for the payout should use a low real rate, which raises the coverage needed.

Which kind

Term life insurance is the default; permanent is a specialist tool

Term life insurance
What it is
Coverage for a fixed period (often 10, 20 or 30 years) at a premium that is usually level for the term. It pays only if the insured dies during the term and has no savings component.
Used for
Covering the years when others depend on your income: until children are independent and the mortgage is paid.
What it means for you
It buys the most coverage per dollar, because most term policies never pay a claim: the insured outlives the term.
Permanent life insurancewhole, universal, variable
What it is
Coverage for life with a cash value that grows tax-deferred inside the policy. Premiums are much higher than term for the same death benefit, and commissions and costs are usually higher too.
Used for
Needs that last a lifetime: some estate-tax planning, funding a buy-sell agreement, a lifelong dependent with special needs (INV-102).
What it means for you
For most families, "buy term and invest the difference" in a 401(k), individual retirement account (IRA) or health savings account (HSA) gives the same protection at lower cost, with investments they control. Surrendering a permanent policy early often returns less than the premiums paid. INV-017 covers insurance products in depth.

That rising curve explains both products. A level-premium term policy charges more than the pure cost in early years and less in later years, then ends. A permanent policy charges far more than the pure cost early on, building cash value that helps pay for coverage in old age. Paying for coverage at 70 only makes sense if a need will still exist at 70.

Going furtherLaddering term policies

Needs usually fall over time as the mortgage shrinks, savings grow and children leave home. Instead of one large 30-year policy, some families buy two or three policies with different terms (for example a 20-year and a 30-year) so coverage steps down as needs fall. It can cost less than a single policy sized for the peak need.

Protecting your paycheck

Disability insurance: the coverage young workers most often skip

For a young worker, the largest asset is future earnings, what INV-004 called human capital. Denise's $62,000 salary over 40 years is worth well over $2 million in future pay. A disability that stops her working destroys that asset; life insurance does not help, because she is still alive and still has expenses.

Disability insurance protects that asset. The Social Security Administration states that studies show a 20-year-old worker has a 1-in-4 chance of developing a disability before reaching full retirement age. Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI) helps, but its definition is strict: benefits go to people who cannot work because of a condition expected to last at least one year or result in death, and SSA pays nothing for partial or short-term disability. There is generally a five-month waiting period.

Disability insurance terms to know
What it is
Benefit: a percentage of pay up to a monthly cap. Elimination period: the waiting time before benefits start (for example, 90 days). Definition of disability: "own occupation" (cannot do your job) is broader than "any occupation" (cannot do any suitable job). Benefit period: how long payments last, for example to age 65.
Used for
Comparing a group policy with an individual one, and deciding whether to buy supplemental coverage.
What it means for you
Group coverage is cheap and easy, but it usually ends with the job, may define disability narrowly after a few years, and is taxable if your employer paid for it. The emergency fund covers the elimination period.
Practitioner depthThe tax on disability benefits

IRS Publication 525: benefits from a plan paid for by your employer are generally income to you; if both of you pay, only the part due to the employer's payments is taxable; if you pay the entire cost with after-tax money, benefits are not taxable. A 60% benefit that is taxable may leave only about 50% of your former pay after tax. SSA's 2026 substantial gainful activity threshold, $1,690 a month for non-blind individuals, is the earnings level above which SSA generally considers a person able to work.

The largest insurance bill

Health insurance: how the costs are shared, and where they stop

Premium, deductible, coinsurance, out-of-pocket maximum
What it is
Premium: what you pay to have coverage. Deductible: what you pay for covered care before the plan starts sharing costs. Coinsurance or copay: your share after the deductible. Out-of-pocket maximum: the most you pay for covered in-network care in a year; after it, the plan pays 100%.
Used for
Comparing plans on total cost. Premiums are certain; the rest depends on how much care you use.
What it means for you
The out-of-pocket maximum is the number that protects your plan: it turns an unlimited risk into a known worst case, which your emergency fund should cover.

For context, in 2025 the average employer plan cost $9,325 a year for single coverage and $26,993 for family coverage, with workers contributing on average $1,440 and $6,850 (KFF). Losing that coverage with a job is one reason emergency funds need extra months for families (INV-019).

Going furtherChoosing between plans

Add, for each plan: 12 months of premiums, plus the out-of-pocket cost for a typical year and for a bad year, minus any employer HSA contribution and the tax saved on your own HSA contributions. INV-022 works this for Denise: the high-deductible plan cost less in both years in that illustration. Check that your doctors and prescriptions are covered in-network, because out-of-network costs may not count toward the cap.

Everything you own, and what you could owe

Auto, home, umbrella, and a first look at long-term care

Auto insurance. The most important part is liability coverage, which pays others when you cause an accident. Limits are written as three numbers, for example 100/300/100: $100,000 of bodily injury per person, $300,000 per accident, $100,000 of property damage. States set minimum limits that are often far below what a serious accident costs; a person with savings to protect usually needs much more than the minimum. Collision and comprehensive cover your own car; on an old car worth little, dropping them can make sense.

Home and renter's insurance. Covers the building (for owners), your belongings and personal liability. Replacement cost coverage pays to rebuild or replace; actual cash value pays replacement cost minus depreciation, often far less. Most homeowners insurance does not cover flood damage, according to the National Flood Insurance Program; flood coverage is a separate policy.

Umbrella insurance
What it is
Extra liability coverage, in amounts such as $1 million or more, that pays after your auto or home liability limits are used up.
Used for
Protecting savings and future earnings from a large lawsuit: a serious car accident, an injury on your property, a teenage driver's accident.
What it means for you
Ask the insurer what underlying auto and home liability limits it requires. It is most valuable for households with significant assets or high income, such as the Harpers.

Long-term care: the risk that grows with age

Long-term care is help with everyday activities (bathing, dressing, eating) over a long period, at home or in a facility. According to the Administration for Community Living, someone turning 65 today has almost a 70% chance of needing some type of long-term care services; women need care longer (3.7 years) than men (2.2 years), and 20% will need it for longer than five years. Medicare.gov is explicit: "Medicare doesn't pay for long-term care," and neither do most health insurance or Medigap policies.

Going furtherThree ways to pay for long-term care

Self-fund from savings and home equity, realistic for wealthier households. Insure with traditional long-term care insurance or a hybrid life/long-term care policy, typically bought in one's 50s or early 60s while still insurable. Medicaid generally pays for long-term care only after most assets are spent, under state rules. INV-083 covers the choice in depth.

Real rules and statistics

Three protections, and their limits

Case study 1How strict Social Security disability is
What happened
SSA's disability publication (July 2026 edition) states that a 20-year-old worker has a 1-in-4 chance of developing a disability before full retirement age, but that federal law requires a "very strict definition of disability": a condition that stops work and is expected to last at least a year or end in death. Partial or short-term disability is not covered, and payment generally starts only after a five-month waiting period. The average disabled worker's benefit was an estimated $1,630 a month in January 2026.
What it teaches
SSDI is a floor, not a plan. Private or group long-term disability coverage, plus an emergency fund, fills the gap in timing, definition and amount.
Source
Social Security Administration, Disability Benefits (Publication No. 05-10029, July 2026); 2026 Cost-of-Living Adjustment Fact Sheet.
Case study 2The out-of-pocket maximum as a legal cap
What happened
For 2026, most health plans cannot require more than $10,600 of in-network cost sharing for an individual or $21,200 for a family in a year; after that the plan pays 100% of covered benefits. Premiums, out-of-network care and non-covered services are not counted.
What it teaches
With in-network care, the worst case for a covered medical year is known in advance. That known number, not the unlimited "what if," is what to plan the emergency fund around.
Source
HealthCare.gov, "Out-of-pocket maximum/limit" (2026).
Case study 3Long-term care and the Medicare gap
What happened
Almost 70% of people turning 65 will need some long-term care, one in five for more than five years (ACL). Yet "Medicare doesn't pay for long-term care"; people "pay all costs for non-covered services, including most long-term care" (Medicare.gov).
What it teaches
The largest uninsured risk for many retirees is not a market crash but years of care. It needs a plan (savings, insurance or Medicaid) well before it is needed.
Source
Administration for Community Living; Medicare.gov, "Long-term care."

Decisions

Three protection decisions

Scenario 1 · The Riveras

An agent proposes a $500,000 whole life policy on Marcus for about $500 a month, "because term is throwing money away." The needs analysis says Marcus needs about $785,000 more coverage for about 18 years. What should they do?

Whole lifeIt covers less than the need ($500,000 against $785,000), for life when the need lasts about 18 years, at a premium that would crowd out their 529s and retirement saving.
Term on bothRight for most young families: coverage matched to the need and its duration, at a fraction of the cost. Elena's income and unpaid work matter too; a household with two earners needs coverage on both.
Group life only$85,000 and $60,000 are a fraction of the need, and both end if they change jobs.
The principle: match the amount to the need and the term to how long the need lasts.
Scenario 2 · Denise

Denise's employer offers free group long-term disability (60% of pay) and optional supplemental coverage. She is 24, single, healthy. What should she consider?

Skip itSSA puts the odds at 1 in 4 for a 20-year-old before full retirement age, and her future earnings are her largest asset.
Keep and checkRight. Taxable, a 60% benefit ($3,100 a month) would barely cover her $2,737 of essentials after tax. If her plan allows after-tax premiums, the benefit becomes tax-free; supplemental or individual coverage can raise the replacement rate.
The principle: insure your ability to earn before you insure anything else.
Scenario 3 · The Harpers

The Harpers have $1.1 million saved, a $650,000 home and two cars, and their son Ethan drives. They carry the state-minimum auto liability limits. What should change?

NothingState minimums can be far below the cost of a serious injury. A judgment above the limit can put their savings at risk.
Raise limits and add an umbrellaRight. Liability is the risk that most threatens a household with substantial assets, and umbrella coverage is designed for it.
Transfer assetsThat creates gift-tax, control and creditor problems of its own (Stage 12), and does not protect the family from a lawsuit arising now.
The principle: the more you have, the more liability coverage you need.

Apply it

A protection plan for each household

Disability first, life insurance barely. Keep group disability and check its terms; her group life covers final expenses. Renter's insurance with liability coverage costs little.
Term life on both, sized by needs. About $785,000 more on Marcus and $585,000 on Elena, on 20-year terms that cover the 18-year need; update beneficiaries (INV-085) and name a guardian for Sofia and Leo in their wills (INV-084).
Everything depends on one person, and Eli's need does not end at 22. The needs method puts Maya about $521,000 short of her $92,000 of group life. A personal 20-year term policy of roughly $525,000 would survive a job change and run to her 78th birthday, when Eli is 33 and her own retirement savings are meant to take over. The payout should go to a third-party supplemental (special) needs trust, never to Eli directly, because assets in his name can cost him SSI and Medicaid (INV-102); her 401(k) and Roth beneficiary forms should name the trust too (INV-085). At the IRS uniform rate for ages 55–59, the pure cost of $525,000 is about $2,700 a year; real quotes depend on her health. Disability is the other half: as the only earner at 58, a disability would end both her paycheck and her 401(k) saving, so she should check her group long-term disability benefit, cap and definition, and keep the emergency fund topped up to cover the elimination period.
Liability, then long-term care. Raise auto and home liability limits and add an umbrella. In their late 50s they are at the age when many people price long-term care insurance, while still insurable. Life insurance needs are shrinking as savings grow.
Plan for care. At 68, Ruth's largest risk is long-term care. Her $780,000 IRA and $380,000 house may allow her to self-fund; she should decide now how she would pay, and whether a hybrid policy is still available and affordable (INV-083).

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A plan has a $1,700 deductible, 20% coinsurance and a $5,000 out-of-pocket maximum. For a $12,000 covered in-network bill, how much do you pay, in dollars?

$1,700 + 20% × ($12,000 − $1,700) = $1,700 + $2,060 = $3,760, below the $5,000 cap.
Exercise 2

A family needs $30,000 a year for 18 years. At a 2% real rate the present-value factor is 14.992. What lump sum covers it, in dollars?

$30,000 × 14.992 = $449,760. Without discounting it would be $540,000; the difference is the growth the payout earns while it is spent.
Exercise 3

A disability policy pays 60% of Denise's $62,000 salary. What is the monthly benefit before tax, in dollars?

$62,000 × 0.60 = $37,200 a year; ÷ 12 = $3,100 a month, taxable if her employer paid the premium.
Exercise 4

Using the IRS uniform rate of $0.09 per $1,000 per month for ages 35–39, what is the yearly cost of $500,000 of coverage, in dollars?

500 × $0.09 × 12 = $540. At 65–69 ($1.27) the same coverage would be $7,620.
