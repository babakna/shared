Short-Term Rentals, House Hacking and BRRRR

Three popular strategies promise to make real estate faster, cheaper or more profitable: renting by the night, living in one unit and renting the others, and recycling your cash through a renovation and refinance. Each works under specific conditions and fails in predictable ways. This module gives you the rules, the math and the failure points.

The question this module answersWhen does one of these strategies actually beat an ordinary long-term rental, or simply buying an index fund?

Start here

Three shortcuts, three kinds of work

The ordinary way to own a rental is simple to describe: save a sizable down payment (Fannie Mae's standard matrix finances at most 85% of a one-unit investment property, so at least 15% down), buy a house, lease it to a tenant for a year at a time, and collect rent. It works, but it is slow and capital-hungry, which is why these three shortcuts are so popular online.

Short-term rentals (STRs) try to earn more rent from the same property by renting by the night. House hacking tries to buy with a small down payment by living in one unit of a small multi-unit building. BRRRR — buy, rehab, rent, refinance, repeat — tries to get your cash back out of a property so you can buy another. None is a trick. Each swaps money for something else: your time, your privacy, or your exposure to a single appraisal.

Concept 1

More rent per night, more cost per night

A long-term tenant pays one rent, cleans the place and pays most utilities. A short-term guest pays more per night, but every stay needs cleaning, laundry and supplies, the booking platform takes a cut, the owner pays the utilities and furnishes the home, and many nights sit empty. The question is not "is nightly rent higher?" — it always is — but whether what is left after those costs beats a lease.

Occupancy and average daily rate
What it is
Occupancy is the share of available nights that are booked. Average daily rate is the average price per booked night. Revenue per available night is the two multiplied together.
Used for
Projecting short-term rental revenue: 365 nights × occupancy × average rate, plus cleaning fees per stay.
What it means for you
Projections from listing sites and sellers can assume strong occupancy. Test your plan at lower occupancy and rate; a property that only works at 75% booked nights is fragile.
Kept share k = 1 − platform % − manager %
Net per booked night = rate × k + (cleaning fee × k − cleaning cost) ÷ average stay

With the defaults — $180 a night, 60% occupancy, 3-night stays, a $90 cleaning fee that costs $80 to deliver, a 3% platform fee and $9,000 of extra yearly costs — the short-term rental earns about $22,770 of net operating income, against $13,976 for a $2,000-a-month lease with 5% vacancy and an 8% manager. It breaks even with the lease at about 46% occupancy. Hire a short-term manager at 20% of bookings and the advantage vanishes: $13,572, with break-even near 61%. The difference was the owner's own labor.

Going furtherSeasonality and concentration

Nightly demand usually swings with seasons, local events and travel cycles, so monthly income is uneven even in a good year. Your competition is every other listing nearby, and supply can grow quickly. A lease gives you one tenant's credit risk; a short-term rental gives you the whole local travel market's.

Concept 2

The city decides whether your plan is legal

Short-term rentals are regulated mainly by cities, counties and states, and the rules range from "register and pay tax" to "only in your own home, with you present." A plan that is legal in one city can be illegal a few miles away, and rules can change after you buy. Three real examples show the range.

PlaceCore rule for rentals under 30 daysSource
New York CityRenting a whole apartment or home for fewer than 30 days is not allowed. A short-term rental is legal only if the host lives in the same unit with the guests, with no more than two paying guests at a time. Since Local Law 18, hosts must register, and booking platforms may not process transactions for unregistered rentals; enforcement began September 5, 2023.NYC Mayor's Office of Special Enforcement
DenverA license is required to rent for one to 29 days at a time, and the property must be the host's primary residence — "the place where a person's habitation is fixed … and is the person's usual place of return." Rentals of 30 days or more need a residential rental property license instead.City and County of Denver, Business Licensing
Arizona (statewide)State law says "a city or town may not prohibit vacation rentals or short-term rentals." Cities may regulate for health and safety, require emergency contacts, liability insurance of at least $500,000, and a local permit whose fee cannot exceed the lesser of cost or $250.Arizona Revised Statutes §9-500.39
⚠️

Regulation is a price risk. If a property was priced on short-term income and the rules tighten, its value falls to what a long-term tenant will pay. Run the long-term numbers as your fallback before you buy.

Concept 3

The 7-day rule and why it matters

INV-068 explained that rentals are passive by default, so most owners with jobs cannot use rental losses against wages. Short-term rentals can fall outside that rule entirely, because the passive activity regulations say an activity is not a rental activity at all if the average period of customer use is 7 days or less.

Average period of customer useIRS Publication 925
What it is
Total days in all rental periods divided by the number of rentals in the year. If it is 7 days or less, the activity is not a rental activity for the passive rules. If it is 30 days or less and you provide significant personal services, it is also not a rental activity.
Used for
Deciding which passive-loss rules apply. A short-term rental with a 3-night average stay is treated like a business: its losses are nonpassive if you materially participate, and passive if you do not. The $25,000 allowance does not apply either way.
What it means for you
A high earner who materially participates — for example more than 100 hours and more than anyone else, including cleaners, or more than 500 hours — may deduct depreciation losses, including bonus depreciation on furnishings and cost-segregated items, against salary. Without material participation, nothing changes. Keep a log.

Three more rules short-term hosts meet

  • Personal use. If you use the property yourself more than the greater of 14 days or 10% of the days it is rented at a fair price, it counts as a home, and if the rental shows a loss you may not be able to deduct all of its expenses (Pub. 527, Worksheet 5-1).
  • Rented fewer than 15 days. If you rent a home you also use for fewer than 15 days in the year, you do not report the rent, and you do not deduct rental expenses (Pub. 527).
  • Hotel-like services. If you provide substantial services primarily for guests' convenience, such as regular cleaning during stays, changing linen or maid service, you report on Schedule C and may owe self-employment tax (Pub. 527).
Going furtherWhy the short-term route attracts audits of hours

Material participation is decided on facts, and you must be able to prove your hours. Test 3 (more than 100 hours and at least as much as any other individual) is often failed because a cleaner or co-host worked more hours than the owner. Hours are counted per activity, and a spouse's participation counts toward yours for material participation. A written log made at the time is far more credible than an estimate made later.

Concept 4

Buying a small building to live in

Loans for a home you will live in are cheaper and need less down than loans for investment property. House hacking uses that: you buy a building with two to four units, live in one, and rent the others. The same rules that make it possible also constrain it.

FHA loanHUD Handbook 4000.1
What it is
A mortgage insured by the Federal Housing Administration. It can finance an existing one- to four-unit residence. For purchases, the maximum loan-to-value (LTV) ratio is 96.5% — a 3.5% minimum investment — for borrowers with a credit score of 580 or higher; scores from 500 to 579 are limited to 90%.
Used for
Owner-occupants only: at least one borrower must move in within 60 days of closing and intend to live there at least one year. FHA charges an upfront mortgage insurance premium of 1.75% of the base loan and an annual premium, paid monthly — for loans longer than 15 years with LTV above 95% and a base loan up to $726,200, 0.55% a year for the life of the loan.
What it means for you
For a three- or four-unit property, FHA adds a self-sufficiency test: the full monthly payment (principal, interest, taxes and insurance) divided by the appraiser's fair market rent for all units, minus the greater of the vacancy-and-maintenance estimate or 25%, may not exceed 100%.

The tax side follows IRS Pub. 527's duplex example: expenses for the whole building, such as mortgage interest and property tax, are split between the rented unit (deducted on Schedule E) and your own unit (deductible only if you itemize). You depreciate only the rented part. When you sell, the home sale exclusion can cover the part you lived in, but depreciation on the rented part is taxed (INV-068).

Going furtherA fourplex that fails the self-sufficiency test

A $700,000 fourplex with four units renting at $1,600 each: fair market rent $6,400 a month, minus 25%, is $4,800 of net self-sufficiency rental income. With 3.5% down, the upfront premium financed, 6.5% interest, a 0.55% annual premium and assumed taxes and insurance, the monthly payment is about $5,254 — 109% of that income. FHA would not insure it on these numbers; a larger down payment, a lower price or higher rents would be needed.

Concept 5

Buy, rehab, rent, refinance, repeat

BRRRR starts with a property that is worth less than it could be because it needs work. You buy it, usually with cash or a short-term loan, renovate it, rent it, and then refinance into a long-term mortgage based on the new, higher appraised value. If the appraisal is high enough, the refinance returns most or all of your cash, which you use for the next property.

After-repair value and cash-out refinance
What it is
The after-repair value (ARV) is what the property appraises for once renovated. A cash-out refinance replaces the existing financing with a larger loan and pays you the difference. For an investment property, Fannie Mae's Eligibility Matrix (August 2026) caps cash-out refinances at 75% of value for one unit and 70% for two to four units.
Used for
Pulling your equity back out. Fannie Mae's Selling Guide generally requires at least one borrower to have been on title for six months before the new loan, with a "delayed financing" exception for all-cash purchases that limits the new loan to what you actually invested.
What it means for you
The whole strategy rests on one number you do not control: the appraisal. A lower ARV leaves more of your money stuck in the property; a higher rate on the new loan eats the rent.
Cash left in the deal = (price + rehab + closing + holding costs) − ARV × LTV × (1 − refinance costs %)

Coverage of 1.06 means a 6% drop in net operating income wipes out the cash flow, and a lender using a debt service coverage test would see almost no cushion (INV-066). Recycling cash multiplies properties; it also multiplies thin margins.

Concept 6

Four ways BRRRR goes wrong

  1. The appraisal comes in low. Comparable sales, not your spreadsheet, set the value. A 10% lower appraisal in the default turns $23,125 left in the deal into $41,313.
  2. The rehab runs over. A 20% overrun on a $45,000 rehab adds $9,000 of cash in; the refinance does not grow to match. Hidden problems in older buildings are the usual cause.
  3. Rates rise before you refinance. At 8% instead of 7%, the default's cash flow falls from $871 to about −$670 a year, and coverage to 0.96. At 6%, it improves to about $2,350.
  4. Time. The six-month title requirement, contractor delays and the search for a tenant mean months of holding costs with no rent. If you borrow short-term to buy, that loan must be repaid on schedule whether or not the refinance happens.
💡

The margin of safety test. Before buying, run the deal with a 10% lower appraisal, a 20% higher rehab and a rate one point higher, all at once. If you can live with that result, including cash left in the deal, the plan is robust. If it only works in the base case, it is a bet on everything going right.

Real rules

Three governments, three answers

Case study 1New York City's Local Law 18
What happened
New York City adopted Local Law 18, the Short-Term Rental Registration Law, on January 9, 2022. It requires hosts to register with the Office of Special Enforcement and bars booking platforms from processing transactions for unregistered rentals. Enforcement began September 5, 2023. Registration is limited to a person who permanently occupies the unit, and is denied in rent-regulated and public housing units and in buildings whose owners prohibit short-term rentals.
What it teaches
The city did not change the underlying rule — whole-unit stays under 30 days had long been restricted — it changed enforcement, by making the platforms the checkpoint. Income that depends on weak enforcement can end quickly.
Source
NYC Mayor's Office of Special Enforcement, Short-term rental registration and What is Local Law 18.
Case study 2Denver's primary-residence rule
What happened
Denver requires a license for rentals of one to 29 days and allows them only in the host's primary residence. The city lists factors it considers, such as where the applicant votes, registers vehicles and files taxes, and states that returning only to clean or turn the unit over between stays does not count as living there.
What it teaches
In a primary-residence city, the short-term rental is a way to earn from your own home — a room, or your whole home while you travel — not a way to buy investment properties.
Source
City and County of Denver, Business Licensing, Short-term rentals.
Case study 3Arizona's state preemption
What happened
Arizona law bars cities and towns from prohibiting vacation or short-term rentals or regulating them by classification or use, while allowing health-and-safety rules, local permits with fees capped at $250 or cost, emergency contacts, at least $500,000 of liability coverage, neighbor notification, and permit suspensions for verified violations.
What it teaches
State law can override a city. In Arizona the risk of a city ban is removed by statute, though cities keep real tools to police problem properties.
Source
Arizona Revised Statutes §9-500.39.

Decisions

Three households, three strategies

Scenario 1 · Jordan

Jordan wants to buy a duplex in Denver with an FHA loan, live in one unit and rent the other on Airbnb to earn more. What is realistic?

Short-term the other unitDenver licenses short-term rentals only in the host's primary residence, and its guidance describes an STR location as a single dwelling unit. The unit she does not live in would not qualify.
Long-term lease, when readyA sound version of the plan. With $3,000 of savings, she is not ready yet: FHA needs at least 3.5% down plus closing costs and reserves, and a $450,000 duplex would cost about $3,367 a month before repairs.
Wait and buildAlso reasonable. Her employer match and her 6.8% loans come first (INV-020); house hacking will still exist when she has a cushion.
The principle: check the city's rules and your cash cushion before the spreadsheet.
Scenario 2 · The Riveras

When they move, the Riveras could rent their Phoenix home short-term instead of leasing it. Arizona does not let cities ban short-term rentals. Should they?

Go short-termLegal is not the same as profitable. With two young children and two jobs, they would likely pay a manager, which in the default example erases the advantage.
Compare, check, then decideRight. They may also need a city permit and must comply with the state's transaction privilege tax licensing, and their $500,000 exclusion lasts only if they sell within about three years of moving out.
The principle: price your own time, or the manager who replaces it.
Scenario 3 · The Harpers

The Harpers hear that a short-term rental plus a cost segregation study could create losses that offset their wages. Rachel could manage it. What must be true?

Only the 7-day testThat removes rental-activity treatment, but without material participation the losses are still passive and suspended at their income.
All of itRight. If a cleaner works more hours than Rachel, test 3 fails; test 1 needs more than 500 hours. Bonus depreciation on 5-year items is recaptured as ordinary income when they sell (INV-068).

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A host had 24 bookings totaling 84 nights this year. What is the average period of customer use, in days?

84 ÷ 24 = 3.5 days — 7 or fewer, so it is not a rental activity under the passive rules.
Exercise 2

What is the minimum FHA down payment on a $450,000 duplex for a borrower with a 640 credit score, in dollars?

Maximum LTV 96.5%, so the minimum investment is 3.5% × $450,000 = $15,750, plus closing costs.
Exercise 3

You put $205,000 into a BRRRR deal. It appraises at $225,000; the lender allows 75% LTV and refinance costs are 3% of the loan. How much cash is left in the deal, to the nearest dollar?

Loan $168,750; after 3% costs, $163,687.50. $205,000 − $163,687.50 = $41,313.
Exercise 4

A fourplex's four units have a fair market rent of $1,600 each. The monthly payment would be $5,254. Using 25% for vacancy and maintenance, what is the self-sufficiency ratio, in percent?

Net rent = 4 × $1,600 × 0.75 = $4,800. Ratio = $5,254 ÷ $4,800 = 109.5%, above FHA's 100% limit.
