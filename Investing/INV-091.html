Inherited IRAs and the 10-Year Rule

A retirement account is often the largest thing a parent leaves, and the only one that arrives with a built-in income tax bill. Since 2020 most heirs must empty an inherited IRA within ten years, and since 2025 many must also take money out every year along the way. How you schedule those withdrawals can change the tax by tens of thousands of dollars.

The question this module answersIf I inherit an IRA, what must I withdraw and when — and what schedule leaves me with the most after tax?

Start here

Inheriting an IRA is inheriting a tax bill

If you inherit a parent's stocks or house, the basis steps up and you can usually sell with little tax (INV-090). An inherited IRA is different. The money in a traditional IRA has never been taxed, and every dollar you take out is ordinary income to you.

That by itself would not be a problem if you could take the money out slowly over your own lifetime. Before 2020, most heirs could do exactly that: the "stretch IRA." The SECURE Act, enacted December 20, 2019, ended the stretch for most beneficiaries of owners who die after 2019. Now most heirs must empty the account by the end of the tenth year after the year of death. Final Treasury regulations published in July 2024 added a second requirement for many of them: if the owner had already reached the age at which required withdrawals begin, the heir must also take a minimum amount every year in years one through nine.

Ten years sounds generous. But the rule interacts with the heir's own income, which is often at its peak in their 40s and 50s. Take it all at once, or wait and take it all in year ten, and much of it can land in a high bracket. Three categories

The beneficiary form decides the rule

An IRA does not pass under a will. It goes to whoever is named on the account's beneficiary designation (INV-085). The rules that follow depend on which of three categories that person or entity falls into, determined as of September 30 of the year after the owner's death (IRS Publication 590-B).

Designated beneficiary
What it is
An individual named as beneficiary of the account (or the individual beneficiaries of a trust that meets the IRS's requirements). Estates and charities are not designated beneficiaries.
Used for
Determining which post-death distribution rule applies. Most designated beneficiaries are subject to the 10-year rule.
What it means for you
Naming a person, not your estate, keeps the more flexible rules available. Naming no one can force faster withdrawals.
Eligible designated beneficiaryIRC 401(a)(9)(E)(ii)
What it is
A designated beneficiary who, on the date of death, is one of five kinds: (1) the owner's surviving spouse; (2) the owner's child who has not reached the age of majority, which the final regulations define as the 21st birthday; (3) a disabled individual; (4) a chronically ill individual; or (5) someone not more than 10 years younger than the owner.
Used for
These beneficiaries may still take distributions over their own life expectancy instead of the 10-year rule.
What it means for you
An adult child such as Anna is not an eligible designated beneficiary. A spouse, a young child, a sibling close in age, or a disabled relative may be.
Going furtherSeveral beneficiaries on one account

If an IRA names several people, Publication 590-B's separate-account rules let each person's share be treated under its own rule once the account is properly divided into separate inherited IRAs; the custodian can explain the deadline. Dividing promptly lets a spouse use spousal options and a sibling close in age use their own life expectancy, instead of everyone being held to the least favorable rule. Custodians handle the paperwork; heirs should ask for it early.

The deadline, and the minimums along the way

How the 10-year rule actually works

The 10-year rule requires the account to be empty by December 31 of the year containing the tenth anniversary of the owner's death. If Ruth dies in 2036, Anna's deadline is December 31, 2046. What happens before that depends on one date.

Required beginning date
What it is
The date by which an IRA owner must take their first required minimum distribution: April 1 of the year after the year they reach the required age, currently 73 for people born in Ruth's cohort.
Used for
Deciding whether an heir under the 10-year rule must also take annual minimums in years 1 through 9.
What it means for you
Ruth, born in 1958, turns 73 in 2031, so her required beginning date is April 1, 2032. If she dies before then, Anna has no annual minimums; if after, Anna does.
  • Owner died before the required beginning date (or the account is a Roth IRA): no distribution is required in years 1–9. The heir may take any amounts at any time, as long as the account is empty by the end of year 10.
  • Owner died on or after the required beginning date: the heir must take an annual minimum in each of years 1–9, and the rest by the end of year 10. This was the most contested point of the new regime; the final regulations adopted it, applying for distribution years beginning in 2025 (T.D. 10001).
Minimum, year t = prior Dec. 31 balance ÷ (L − (t − 1))
L = the longer of:
  beneficiary's life expectancy in year 1, or
  owner's life expectancy in year of death − 1

For Anna at 51 in the first year, the IRS Single Life Table gives 35.3. Ruth at 78 in 2036 has 12.6, minus 1 = 11.6. Anna's is longer. The table is consulted only once; after that the divisor falls by exactly one each year, so Anna's divisors are 35.3, 34.3, 33.3 and so on. On a $650,000 balance, her first minimum is $650,000 ÷ 35.3 = $18,414 — only about 2.8%. The minimums are small; the year-10 balloon is not.

⚠️

Missing a required minimum is expensive. The shortfall is subject to a 25% excise tax, reduced to 10% if you take the missed amount and report it within the correction window, generally by the end of the second year after the year it was due. You can also ask the IRS to waive the tax for reasonable error on Form 5329 (IRS Publication 590-B). Also check the year of death itself: if the owner had not yet taken that year's minimum, the beneficiary must.

Practitioner depthFour years of penalty relief

Proposed regulations in February 2022 first said annual minimums would be required when the owner died after the required beginning date. Many heirs of 2020 and 2021 deaths had not taken them. The IRS responded with Notice 2022-53 (no excise tax for "specified RMDs" missed in 2021 and 2022), Notice 2023-54 (extended to 2023) and Notice 2024-35 (extended to 2024), and said the final rules would apply no earlier than 2025. The final regulations, T.D. 10001, were published July 19, 2024 (89 Fed. Reg. 58886) and apply to distribution calendar years beginning on or after January 1, 2025. Heirs who inherited in 2020–2023 still face the original ten-year deadline; the relief waived only the missed annual amounts, it did not extend the ten years.

Options no one else has

A surviving spouse can make the IRA their own

A spouse who is the sole beneficiary has the most flexibility of any heir. IRS Publication 590-B describes the main choices.

The switch works in one direction. A spouse can remain a beneficiary for a few years, take what they need penalty-free, and later elect to treat the account as their own once they pass 59½. Publication 590-B also notes a trap: a spouse who makes a contribution to the inherited IRA, or fails to take a required minimum as a beneficiary, is treated as having chosen to make it their own.

The Harpers as an example. Suppose Tom died at 60 with about $500,000 in his own 401(k) (an assumed share of the couple's $820,000 of pre-tax savings), Rachel as sole beneficiary. At 58, Rachel could roll it into her own IRA and wait until her own required age, or keep it as an inherited account so she can draw on it before 59½ without the 10% additional tax. If she needs income before 59½, the second choice first is usually better.

Going furtherWhy "own" usually wins later

Once a surviving spouse treats the IRA as their own, the ordinary owner rules apply: minimums start at the spouse's own required age and use the Uniform Lifetime Table (Table III), as for any owner, and the spouse can name new beneficiaries whose own rules start at the spouse's death. The trade-off is the loss of penalty-free early access, which is why age decides the order of moves.

Brackets, not deadlines

Choose a schedule that keeps income in lower brackets

The legal deadlines are the outer walls. Inside them, the goal is usually to pay the lowest average tax rate on the whole account. Because the federal income tax is progressive, a $650,000 withdrawal in one year is taxed at up to 35% or 37%, while ten withdrawals of about $65,000 each, stacked on the same other income, may stay in the 22% and 24% brackets.

Two lessons come out of the table. First, the minimum-then-balloon plan, the one people fall into by doing nothing, costs Anna about $47,400 compared with equal slices. If Ruth had died before her required beginning date, doing nothing would mean the whole account in year 10 and a gap of about $75,800. Second, the bracket plan pays the least tax but leaves slightly less money, because it moves money out of the tax-deferred account sooner. Minimizing tax and maximizing wealth are close cousins, not twins.

Going furtherLife events change the best schedule

The best plan is rarely level in real life. Withdraw more in low-income years — a sabbatical, a job change, early retirement — and less in peak-earning years. Coordinate with other bracket-sensitive items: withdrawals raise adjusted gross income, which can affect health-insurance subsidies, college financial aid, the 3.8% net investment income tax and, for older heirs, Medicare premiums (IRMAA). State income tax applies too, and rules differ by state.

Practitioner depthThe owner can pre-pay the tax

If the owner's own tax bracket is lower than the heir's will be, converting part of a traditional IRA to a Roth IRA during life shifts the tax to the lower rate (INV-038). Ruth, with $2,900 a month of Social Security and no wages, may sit in the 12% or 22% bracket in her late 60s, while Anna may be in the 24% bracket at her peak. Converting in Ruth's lower brackets before RMDs begin in 2031 both shrinks her own future RMDs and hands Anna tax-free money. The trade-offs: the conversion tax is paid now, the extra income can raise Ruth's Medicare premiums through IRMAA (INV-079), and it only helps if the rates really differ.

Special cases

Inherited Roth IRAs, young children and trusts

Inherited Roth IRAs: wait

After a Roth IRA owner dies, the final regulations apply the rules as though the owner died before the required beginning date. A non-spouse designated beneficiary therefore has the 10-year rule with no annual minimums. Qualified withdrawals are tax-free once the account has met the Roth five-year holding period, which runs from the first year the original owner contributed. Since withdrawals will not be taxed, the usual best plan is the opposite of a traditional IRA: leave the money to grow tax-free until late in year 10.

Minor children: a stretch that ends at 21

A child of the owner who has not reached 21 is an eligible designated beneficiary and takes small annual withdrawals based on their life expectancy. On the child's 21st birthday the 10-year clock starts. Under the final regulations, annual withdrawals continue and the account must be empty by the end of the tenth year after the year the child turns 21 (in effect, by 31). The rule applies only to the owner's own child; a grandchild is not an eligible designated beneficiary on that basis.

Trusts as beneficiaries

A trust cannot itself be a designated beneficiary, but its beneficiaries can be treated as designated beneficiaries if the trust is valid under state law, is irrevocable (or becomes irrevocable at the owner's death), has identifiable beneficiaries, and provides the required documentation to the IRA custodian (IRS Publication 590-B). Trusts are used when a beneficiary is young, has special needs, or should not control a large sum. A special category, the applicable multi-beneficiary trust, lets a trust for a disabled or chronically ill beneficiary use that person's life expectancy (INV-102).

Practitioner depthConduit versus accumulation trusts

A trust that must pass every IRA withdrawal straight out to the beneficiary (often called a conduit trust) is treated much like naming the beneficiary directly. A trust that may keep withdrawals inside (an accumulation trust) protects the money but pays trust income-tax rates, which reach 37% above $16,000 of taxable income for 2026 (Rev. Proc. 2025-32, Table 5), against $640,600 for a single individual. Accumulating large IRA withdrawals inside a trust can therefore cost a great deal of tax. The drafting belongs with an estate attorney who works with retirement accounts regularly.

Real law, real numbers

Three stories about inherited retirement accounts

Case study 1The five-year argument over "ten years"
What happened
The SECURE Act replaced the stretch with a 10-year period for most beneficiaries of owners who died after 2019. Many heirs read it like the old 5-year rule: nothing due until the last year. The February 2022 proposed regulations said that when the owner died on or after the required beginning date, annual minimums were also required. After public comments, the IRS waived the excise tax on those minimums for 2021 through 2024 and finalized the rule in July 2024, effective for 2025.
What it teaches
Read the statute and the regulations, not summaries. Heirs of 2020–2024 deaths who relied on the relief still have their original ten-year deadlines, and from 2025 must take annual amounts if the owner had reached the required beginning date.
Source
IRS Notice 2024-35 (reviewing Notices 2022-53 and 2023-54); Treasury Decision 10001, 89 Fed. Reg. 58886 (2024).

Minimums of about $18,400 to $23,300 in years 1–9 leave a $632,000 balloon in 2046. Equal slices of about $65,000–$85,000 cost roughly $75,000 less federal tax than the balloon plan and leave about $47,400 more after tax.

The law sets the minimum; the heir should set the plan. A first-year conversation with a tax preparer, before the first December 31, is worth more than any investment choice inside the account.

Case study 3Sofia and Leo as beneficiaries (illustration)
The setup
The Riveras name each other first and their children second on their 401(k)s. If both parents died while Sofia is 7 and Leo 4, the children would be eligible designated beneficiaries as the owner's minor children.
The result
Each child would take small life-expectancy withdrawals until 21, then empty the account within the following ten years. A 4-year-old could not manage that money, so a custodian or trust would have to act.
What it teaches
For young children, the beneficiary form should name a trust or custodian arrangement drafted for retirement accounts, coordinated with the will's choice of guardian (INV-084, INV-087).

Decisions

Three inherited-account choices

Scenario 1 · Anna, year one

Anna has just inherited $650,000. Her required minimum this year is about $18,400. A friend suggests taking only the minimum "to let it grow." What should she do?

Minimum onlyGrowth is real, but it grows into a year-10 balloon taxed mostly at 35%. In the illustration this costs about $47,400 of after-tax money compared with equal slices.
Equal slices, adjustedRight. It keeps most of the income in the 22%–24% brackets. Anna can front-load in any year her other income dips.
Cash out nowSimple, but it puts most of $650,000 into the 35% and 37% brackets in a single year, the most expensive choice in the illustration.
The principle: treat an inherited IRA as ten years of taxable income to be spread, not a lump to be grabbed or ignored.
Scenario 2 · Rachel, widowed at 58 (illustration)

Suppose Tom dies and Rachel, 58, is sole beneficiary of his 401(k), assumed here to hold about $500,000 of the couple's $820,000. She will need about $40,000 a year from them until she turns 62. What is her best first move?

Roll over nowHer own-IRA withdrawals before 59½ would face the 10% additional tax unless another exception applies: about $4,000 on each $40,000.
Inherited first, own laterRight. Beneficiary distributions are exempt from the 10% additional tax. After 59½, making it her own delays minimums to her own required age and lets her name new beneficiaries.
The principle: a surviving spouse should sequence the options by age, not pick one on the day of the funeral.
Scenario 3 · Ruth, planning ahead

Ruth, 68, is in a lower tax bracket than Anna is likely to be. Her RMDs start in 2031. Should she do anything now about the tax Anna will one day pay?

Do nothingEvery dollar Ruth leaves in the traditional IRA will be taxed at Anna's rate, likely higher than Ruth's own.
Convert in low bracketsRight, if the numbers work. Conversions pay tax at Ruth's lower rate, shrink her own future RMDs, and pass tax-free Roth money to Anna with no annual minimums (INV-038, INV-089 for the Pennsylvania side).
The principle: the cheapest time to pay tax on an IRA is in whichever family member's lowest bracket comes first.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

Anna inherits $650,000 (the December 31 balance) from Ruth, who died after her required beginning date. Anna's single life expectancy in the first year is 35.3, longer than Ruth's remaining 11.6. What is Anna's first required minimum, in dollars?

Use the longer life expectancy: $650,000 ÷ 35.3 = $18,414. Next year the divisor is 34.3.
Exercise 2

An IRA owner dies in 2026 before the required beginning date. Their adult son is the beneficiary. By December 31 of which year must the account be empty?

The year containing the tenth anniversary of the death: 2036. No annual minimums in the meantime, because the owner died before the required beginning date.
Exercise 3

A beneficiary misses a $20,000 required minimum, then takes it and files the correcting return within the correction window. What excise tax applies, in dollars?

Corrected in time, the rate falls from 25% to 10%: 0.10 × $20,000 = $2,000. Uncorrected it would be $5,000, and a waiver can be requested for reasonable error.
Exercise 4

A single filer with $73,900 of other taxable income in 2026 withdraws $300,000 at once. Using the 2026 brackets, how much federal tax does the withdrawal add, in dollars?

Tax on $73,900 = $1,240 + 12% × ($50,400 − $12,400) + 22% × ($73,900 − $50,400) = $1,240 + $4,560 + $5,170 = $10,970. Tax on $373,900 = $58,448 (the tax at $256,225) + 35% × ($373,900 − $256,225) = $58,448 + $41,186 = $99,634. The withdrawal adds $99,634 − $10,970 = $88,664, an average of about 29.6%.
