Employer Benefits

Your pay is more than your salary. A retirement match, health coverage, a health savings account, discounted company stock, life and disability insurance, and sometimes a pension or deferred compensation can add thousands of dollars a year, but only if you enroll and choose well. This module walks through each benefit with the 2026 limits, using Jordan's and Monica's employers as examples.

The question this module answersWhich workplace benefits should I use, how much are they worth, and what traps come with them?

Start here

Total compensation: the part of your pay that hides in the benefits portal

Most people judge a job by its salary, and that is sensible: salary is the largest part of pay and the part you can spend. But employers also spend money on your behalf: they pay most of the health-insurance premium, match retirement savings, and buy insurance you might otherwise purchase yourself.

Some of that value arrives automatically. Much of it does not: a 401(k) match is paid only on money you contribute, a health savings account (HSA) contribution requires the right health plan, and an employee stock discount requires enrolling. Every year a large amount of workplace pay goes unclaimed simply because nobody filled in a form.

The first benefit to claim

The 401(k) match, and when it is really yours

Employer match
What it is
Money your employer adds to your retirement plan in proportion to what you contribute, for example "50% of contributions up to 6% of pay."
Used for
Encouraging employees to save. It is part of your pay that you receive only if you contribute.
What it means for you
Jordan's formula gives her 3% of pay ($1,860 a year) for contributing 6% ($3,720): an immediate 50% return before any investment growth.
Vesting
What it is
The schedule on which employer contributions become permanently yours. Your own contributions are always 100% vested.
Used for
Rewarding employees who stay. For employer contributions to a qualified defined contribution plan, the IRS describes two common schedules: 100% after three years of service (cliff), or graded from 20% after two years to 100% after six. All employees must be fully vested at the plan's normal retirement age or if the plan terminates.
What it means for you
Leaving a job a few months before a vesting date can cost thousands. Check the date before you resign.

At the defaults, Jordan contributes 6% and receives the full $1,860 match, but in her first year she is 0% vested under her plan's graded schedule (illustrative): if she left now, she would keep her own contributions and none of the match.

Going furtherMatching your student-loan payments

Since plan years beginning after 2023, SECURE 2.0 lets employers treat an employee's qualified student-loan payments as if they were 401(k) contributions for the purposes of the match, provided the employee certifies the payments each year (IRS Notice 2024-63). For a borrower who cannot afford both, it means loan payments can still earn the match. Separately, an employer can pay up to $5,250 a year toward an employee's student loans tax-free under Section 127, now permanent. Jordan should ask whether her plan offers either.

Practitioner depthPer-paycheck matching and the "true-up"

Many plans calculate the match each pay period. If you front-load contributions and hit the $24,500 limit in September, you contribute nothing in the last months and may receive no match for them. Some plans make a year-end "true-up" contribution to fix this; others do not. The plan's summary plan description says which. INV-031 covers workplace plans in depth.

Health accounts

Health savings accounts and flexible spending accounts

Health savings account (HSA)
What it is
An account you own, available only with an HSA-eligible high-deductible health plan (HDHP). Contributions are deductible or excluded from pay, earnings are tax-free while held, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free.
Used for
Paying medical costs with pre-tax money, and, if you can pay current costs from cash, saving for health costs in retirement.
What it means for you
It stays with you when you change jobs. Contributions made through payroll are generally not subject to Social Security and Medicare taxes either.
2026 HSA-eligible plan requirementsSelf-onlyFamily
Minimum annual deductible$1,700$3,400
Maximum out-of-pocket (deductibles, copays, not premiums)$8,500$17,000

The 2025 law also made two changes that took effect January 1, 2026: bronze and catastrophic plans are treated as HSA-compatible even if they do not meet the usual HDHP definition, and people in certain direct primary care arrangements can still contribute (IRS Notice 2026-5).

Flexible spending account (FSA)
What it is
An employer account funded from pre-tax pay for medical costs (health FSA) or for child and dependent care (dependent-care FSA).
Used for
Paying predictable costs such as prescriptions, glasses or daycare with untaxed money.
What it means for you
Unlike an HSA, an FSA is generally "use it or lose it": money not spent by the deadline is forfeited, apart from any carryover or grace period your plan allows. Elect only what you are confident you will spend.
Going furtherThe HSA as a retirement account

Withdrawals not used for medical expenses are taxed and, before 65, face a 20% additional tax; after 65, disability or death there is no additional tax, so the HSA then behaves like a traditional IRA for non-medical spending and stays tax-free for medical costs. Contributions must stop once you enroll in Medicare. INV-033 covers HSA strategy, including saving receipts to reimburse yourself years later.

Company stock and later pay

Employee stock purchase plans and deferred compensation

Employee stock purchase plan (ESPP)
What it is
A plan that lets employees buy company stock through payroll deductions, often at a discount. Under a tax-qualified (Section 423) plan the price may be as low as 85% of the stock's value, and no employee may buy more than $25,000 of stock (valued at the grant date) per calendar year.
Used for
Encouraging ownership. A "lookback" applies the discount to the lower of the price at the start of the offering period or on the purchase date.
What it means for you
A 15% discount is an immediate gain of about 17.6% on the money you put in (1 ÷ 0.85 − 1), more with a lookback in a rising market. The risk is keeping the shares and becoming concentrated in your employer.

Suppose an employer runs a Section 423 plan with a 15% discount, six-month offering periods and a lookback (illustrative details). The defaults below use $3,000 of deductions, a $40 starting price and a $46 purchase-date price.

With the lookback, the employee buys at $34.00 (85% of $40), receives 88.24 shares worth $4,058.82, a gain of $1,058.82 or 35.3% in six months. Without a lookback the price would be $39.10 and the gain 17.6%. Selling at once is a "disqualifying disposition": the gain is taxed as ordinary wages, but the concentration risk disappears the same day.

Going furtherQualifying versus disqualifying dispositions

To get the more favorable tax treatment, shares must be held until the later of two years after the option was granted and one year after the shares were transferred to you (IRS Publication 525). The price of waiting is 12 to 24 months of concentrated risk in one company. For most employees, the discount is the reliable part of the benefit; the holding-period tax saving is not worth betting a year of savings on one stock. INV-040 covers ESPP taxes in detail.

Nonqualified deferred compensation (NQDC)
What it is
A plan, usually for higher earners, that lets you postpone receiving (and being taxed on) part of your pay until a future date, such as retirement.
Used for
Deferring income from high-tax working years into lower-tax later years, beyond the 401(k) limits.
What it means for you
In an unfunded plan the promise is, in the IRS's words, “unfunded and unsecured”: any money the employer sets aside stays subject to the claims of its general creditors, so if the company fails, the deferred pay may be lost. Plans must follow Section 409A timing rules; failures can trigger a 20% additional tax. Payout dates are generally fixed when you elect.

Defined benefit plans

Pensions: a promised income, and its insurance

Pensiondefined benefit plan
What it is
A plan that promises a monthly income in retirement based on a formula, typically years of service × a percentage × final or average pay. The employer invests the money and bears the investment risk.
Used for
Providing lifetime income, often with an option for a spouse to continue receiving part of it.
What it means for you
A pension works like a bond in your portfolio: steady, predictable income. Anyone with one should include it when setting their stock and bond mix (INV-025).
Annual pension (typical formula) = years of service × accrual rate × average pay

Example. Rachel Harper has a small frozen pension from an early employer (illustrative): 8 years × 1.5% × $48,000 average pay = $5,760 a year, payable at 65. It will not grow, but it is a guaranteed layer of income for the Harpers' retirement plan.

Going furtherLump sum or annuity?

Many plans offer a one-time lump sum instead of monthly payments. The choice depends on the plan's interest-rate assumptions, your health and longevity, whether you need survivor income, and whether you would invest the lump sum well. INV-080 analyzes the trade-off; the short version is that the monthly income is valuable insurance against a long life.

Group coverage and the yearly decision

Life and disability insurance at work, and open enrollment

Group term life insurance
What it is
Life insurance provided through your employer, often one or two times salary, sometimes with extra "supplemental" coverage you can buy.
Used for
A base layer of protection for dependents. Employer-paid coverage up to $50,000 is tax-free; the cost of coverage above $50,000 is added to your taxable wages as "imputed income," using an IRS table by age.
What it means for you
It usually ends when the job ends, and one or two times salary is rarely enough for a family (INV-023). Treat it as a supplement to your own policy, not a replacement.

Imputed income, worked. Monica, 58, has employer-paid coverage of one times salary: $92,000. The first $50,000 is excluded. The IRS Table 2-2 cost for ages 55–59 (age at the end of the year) is $0.43 per $1,000 per month, so Monica's taxable imputed income is 42 × $0.43 × 12 = $216.72 a year, adding only a few dollars of tax per month. The table rises with age: at 60–64 ($0.66) the same coverage would add $332.64 a year.

Disability insurance. Group long-term disability pays a set percentage of pay, up to a monthly cap, if illness or injury stops you working; Jordan's plan (illustrative) pays 60%. Tax treatment depends on who pays the premium: benefits from a plan your employer pays for are generally taxable income, while benefits from a plan whose entire cost you paid with after-tax money are not (IRS Publication 525). Some employers let you choose to pay the premium after tax for exactly this reason.

Law and real rules

Three rules that changed what workplace benefits are worth

Case study 1Student-loan payments that earn a 401(k) match
What happened
Section 110 of the SECURE 2.0 Act allowed employers, for plan years beginning after December 31, 2023, to make matching contributions on account of employees' qualified student-loan payments, if the employee certifies the payments annually. The IRS issued guidance in Notice 2024-63.
What it teaches
Young workers with loans no longer have to choose between the loan and the match, if their employer adopts the feature. Ask HR; it is optional for employers.
Source
Internal Revenue Service (2024). Notice 2024-63.
Case study 2The 2025 law changes workplace health and family benefits
What happened
Public Law 119-21 (July 4, 2025) raised the dependent-care assistance exclusion from $5,000 to $7,500 starting in 2026; made HSA-eligible any bronze or catastrophic plan from 2026 and allowed HSA contributions with certain direct primary care arrangements; made telehealth before the deductible permanently compatible with HSAs; and made the $5,250 tax-free employer student-loan payment permanent, indexed for inflation after 2026.
What it teaches
Benefit rules change. Re-read your options at every open enrollment instead of carrying last year's elections forward.
Source
IRS Publication 15-B (2026); IRS news release on Notice 2026-5; Revenue Procedure 2025-32.
Case study 3When a pension plan fails
What happened
Private single-employer pension plans pay premiums to the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation, a federal agency. If a plan terminates without enough money, the PBGC pays benefits up to a legal limit, which for plans ending in 2026 is $7,789.77 a month for a straight-life annuity starting at 65 and less for earlier starts.
What it teaches
Most pensioners are fully protected; those with very large benefits or early retirement ages can lose part of theirs. Deferred compensation has no such insurance.
Source
Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation, Maximum monthly guarantee tables (2026).

Decisions

Three benefits decisions

Scenario 1 · Jordan

Jordan's employer offers a PPO ($150 a month, low deductible) and an HSA-eligible HDHP ($60 a month, $1,700 deductible, $500 employer HSA contribution). She is healthy. Which should she choose?

PPOPredictable, but in this illustration it costs $1,580 more in a light year and $580 more even in a worst-case year, before counting the HSA's tax savings.
HDHP and HSAUsually the better value for a healthy young worker: lower premiums, the employer's $500, and tax-free money that stays hers. The risk is a large bill before the HSA has grown, which is one more reason to build the emergency fund (INV-019).
The principle: compare total yearly cost (premiums plus likely care minus employer money), not the premium or the deductible alone.
Scenario 2 · Monica

Monica, 58, contributes 12% of her $92,000 salary ($11,040) and already receives the full $3,680 match (her plan matches contributions dollar for dollar up to 4% of pay). For 2026 she may also add an $8,000 catch-up contribution. What should she do?

Stop at the matchThe match is the first benefit to claim, not the last. With about nine working years left, the years in which she can shelter an extra $8,000 are running out, and unused limits cannot be made up later.
Use the catch-upRight for most late-career savers. Her wages are under $150,000, so the catch-up can be pre-tax: the full $8,000 costs about $6,820 of take-home pay at her 12% federal and 2.75% Ohio rates (Social Security and Medicare taxes still apply). In the years she turns 60 through 63, 2028 to 2031, the limit is $11,250.
Taxable insteadA taxable account is flexible, and some reachable money makes sense once her emergency fund is full. But it gives up the up-front tax break, and she already has $40,000 there plus a Roth IRA whose contributions can come out without tax or penalty.
The principle: in the last working decade, the catch-up limit is a benefit to claim, just like the match.
Scenario 3 · The Harpers

Tom's employer offers a deferred compensation plan. He could defer $40,000 of salary a year until he retires at 62, when his tax bracket may be lower. What should he weigh?

Defer the maximumThe tax saving is not guaranteed (rates and his retirement income may be higher than he expects), and the money is an unsecured promise from one employer, on top of his salary and any company stock.
Defer moderately, with conditionsSensible. The 401(k) comes first because it is protected. Staggered payouts spread the income over several lower-tax years, which also fits their Roth-conversion planning (INV-038).
The principle: deferred compensation trades tax savings for employer credit risk; size it to what you could afford to lose.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A plan matches 75% of contributions up to 4% of pay. Salary is $80,000 and you contribute 5%. What is the annual match, in dollars?

Only the first 4% is matched: $80,000 × 4% = $3,200; × 75% = $2,400.
Exercise 2

Under the graded schedule (20% after 2 years, rising 20 points a year to 100% after 6), what percentage of the match is vested after 3 years of service?

Year 2: 20%; year 3: 40%; year 4: 60%; year 5: 80%; year 6: 100%.
Exercise 3

Monica, 58, has $92,000 of employer-paid group term life insurance. Using $0.43 per $1,000 per month (ages 55–59), what is the yearly imputed income, in dollars?

($92,000 − $50,000) ÷ $1,000 = 42; 42 × $0.43 × 12 = $216.72.
Exercise 4

An ESPP buys at 85% of the purchase-date price (no lookback) and you sell immediately at that price. What is your gain as a percentage of the money you put in?

Each $0.85 buys $1.00 of stock: 1 ÷ 0.85 − 1 = 17.6%, before tax on the gain as ordinary income.
