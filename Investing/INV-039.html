Charitable Giving Strategies

If you already give to causes you care about, how you give can matter as much as how much. Giving shares instead of cash, grouping several years of gifts into one, or giving straight from an individual retirement account (IRA) after 70½ can each let the same gift cost you less — and the rules changed in 2026.

The question this module answersI give every year. Is there a smarter way to give the same amount?

Start here

Give because you want to; then give efficiently

No tax strategy makes giving free. A deduction returns part of a gift, never more than the gift itself. The point of this module is narrower: if you are going to give anyway, some methods leave more for you, or let you give more, at the same cost.

For most households, most of the time, the simplest approach is fine: give cash and move on. Since 2018, most taxpayers have taken the standard deduction, which means their charitable gifts produced no federal tax benefit at all. Starting in 2026 that changes a little: non-itemizers may deduct up to $1,000 of cash gifts ($2,000 on a joint return). For larger givers, three techniques do most of the work: giving appreciated stock, bunching gifts (often through a donor-advised fund), and, from age 70½, giving through qualified charitable distributions from an IRA.

What changed

Public Law 119-21 and charitable deductions

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, Public Law 119-21, changed the charitable deduction in four ways that took effect for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025. All four are in the statute text; the IRS summarizes them on its "Working Families Tax Cuts" pages.

Change (section of P.L. 119-21)What it does from 2026Who it affects
Deduction for non-itemizers (§70424)Up to $1,000 of cash gifts ($2,000 joint) deductible without itemizing. Cash only; gifts to donor-advised funds and supporting organizations do not qualify; no carryover.Most households, who take the standard deduction
0.5% floor (§70425)Itemizers can deduct gifts only to the extent they exceed 0.5% of their "contribution base" (essentially AGI). Amounts lost to the floor can carry forward only from years in which a percentage limit was exceeded.All itemizers; on $236,000 of AGI, the first $1,180 of gifts

Itemizing versus the standard deduction2026
What it is
Each year you subtract the larger of the standard deduction ($16,100 single, $32,200 joint, $24,150 head of household for 2026) or your itemized deductions: state and local taxes (capped at $40,400 for 2026, less for incomes above $505,000), mortgage interest, charitable gifts and a few others.
Used for
Deciding whether a gift reduces your tax at your full marginal rate, at the non-itemizer $1,000/$2,000 level, or not at all.
What it means for you
A gift saves tax at your marginal rate only on the part of your itemized deductions that exceeds the standard deduction. Below that, it saves nothing beyond the non-itemizer allowance.
Practitioner depthOrder of the percentage limits

Section 170(b)(1) applies separate limits: cash to public charities up to 60% of the contribution base, appreciated capital-gain property to public charities up to 30%, and lower limits for gifts to private foundations. The statute also specifies the order in which the categories of gifts absorb the new 0.5% floor, and lets amounts lost to the floor carry forward only from years in which a percentage limit was exceeded. Amounts over the percentage limits carry forward for up to five years (IRS Publication 526). Gifts of $250 or more need a contemporaneous written acknowledgment from the charity.

The first technique

Give shares, not cash

Suppose you own shares worth $10,000 that you bought for $3,000. If you sell them and give the cash, you owe capital-gains tax on $7,000 first. If you give the shares directly to the charity, the charity sells them tax-free, you owe no tax on the gain, and — if you itemize — you deduct the full $10,000 market value.

Qualified appreciated stockheld more than one year
What it is
Publicly traded stock (or fund shares) you have held for more than one year that has risen in value. Publication 526 treats such "capital gain property" as deductible at fair market value when given to a public charity, up to 30% of AGI.
Used for
Giving away the gain along with the shares, so that no one pays tax on it.
What it means for you
Your most-appreciated shares are your cheapest gifts. Keep your cash, and if you like the investment, buy it back with that cash: your new basis is today's price.

Rules to know

  • Hold more than one year. Shares held a year or less are "ordinary income property"; the deduction is limited to your basis (Publication 526).
  • Never give shares at a loss. Sell them, deduct the capital loss (INV-037), and give the cash.
  • Transfer the shares; do not sell first. Ask the charity for its brokerage details, or give through a donor-advised fund, which accepts shares routinely.
  • The 30% limit. Gifts of appreciated property to public charities are deductible up to 30% of AGI; excess carries forward five years.
Going furtherUsing gifts to diversify a concentrated position

The Harpers' taxable account holds 25 technology stocks with large gains. Giving their lowest-basis shares, year after year, removes the most heavily taxed gains from the portfolio while they sell higher-basis lots or harvest losses elsewhere to diversify. For an employee holding employee stock purchase plan (ESPP) shares, gifting them is more complex, because shares from a disqualifying disposition carry ordinary income (INV-040); qualifying-disposition shares held more than a year work like any other appreciated stock.

The second technique

Two or three years of gifts in one

Bunching means concentrating several years of planned giving into one tax year, itemizing that year, and taking the standard deduction in the others. The total given is unchanged; the deductions all land in the year when they count.

Donor-advised fundDAF
What it is
An account at a sponsoring charity. You contribute cash or shares, take the deduction in the year you contribute, and later recommend grants to the charities you choose. Publication 526 defines it as "a fund or account in which a donor can, because of being a donor, advise the fund how to distribute or invest amounts held in the fund" (IRC §170(f)(18)).
Used for
Bunching: one large, deductible contribution, followed by grants spread over several years so your charities still receive steady support.
What it means for you
The contribution is irrevocable. DAF gifts do not qualify for the new non-itemizer deduction, and IRAs cannot fund a DAF through a QCD.

At the defaults — the Harpers giving $10,000 a year — bunching three years of appreciated stock into a DAF saves about $6,522 of federal tax over the three years, against $1,320 for writing checks: $3,106 from the single year of itemizing, $3,150 of avoided gains tax, and $266 of avoided net investment income tax on gains that would have pushed their income above $250,000. A five-year bunch saves about $13,554.

Going furtherBunching versus the new rules

The 2026 changes cut both ways. The 0.5% floor applies every year you itemize, so fewer itemizing years means fewer years of lost floor — bunching loses the floor once instead of three times. But the non-itemizer deduction rewards cash gifts in standard-deduction years: a couple who bunches can still give up to $2,000 of cash directly to charities in their off years and deduct it. A DAF grant in those years does not count.

The third technique, from age 70½

Qualified charitable distributions

Qualified charitable distributionQCD
What it is
A distribution made directly by the trustee of your IRA to a charity, once you are at least 70½. It is not included in your income, up to an annual limit of $111,000 per person for 2026 (IRS Notice 2025-67; $108,000 for 2025). It counts toward your required minimum distribution. Not allowed from ongoing SEP or SIMPLE IRAs, and not to donor-advised funds or supporting organizations.
Used for
Giving from pre-tax IRA money without it ever appearing in your adjusted gross income.
What it means for you
A QCD works even if you take the standard deduction, and because it keeps income out of AGI, it can also lower the tax on your Social Security, preserve the senior deduction, and keep you under a Medicare income-related monthly adjustment amount (IRMAA) threshold.

How to do it right

  1. The check must go from the IRA custodian to the charity. A withdrawal to you, then a gift, is not a QCD.
  2. Timing with RMDs. The first dollars out of an IRA in a year count toward the RMD; make the QCD before taking the rest.
  3. No double benefit. A QCD excluded from income cannot also be deducted (Publication 590-B).
  4. Reporting. The IRA reports a normal distribution on Form 1099-R; you report the QCD on your return and keep the charity's written acknowledgment.
  5. One-time split-interest option. A single lifetime QCD of up to $55,000 for 2026 can fund a charitable remainder annuity trust, a charitable remainder unitrust or a charitable gift annuity (Notice 2025-67; Publication 590-B).
Going furtherQCDs versus giving appreciated stock

For someone 70½ or older who has both an IRA and appreciated taxable shares, which is better? A QCD removes ordinary income taxed at the full marginal rate (plus its side effects); a gift of stock removes a capital gain taxed at 0%, 15% or 20% and gives a deduction only if you itemize. For most standard-deduction retirees, the QCD wins; the appreciated shares can instead be left to heirs, who receive a step-up in basis (INV-090).

A preview

Charitable remainder trusts

Some givers want to support a charity eventually but need income now. A charitable remainder trust (CRT) does both. INV-093 on charitable legacies covers these in depth; here is what you need to know to recognize when one might fit.

FeatureCharitable remainder annuity trust (CRAT)Charitable remainder unitrust (CRUT)
PaymentFixed dollar amount, 5%–50% of the initial valueFixed percentage, 5%–50%, of each year's value
Additional contributionsNot allowedAllowed
Remainder testCharity's remainder must be worth at least 10% of the value contributedSame: at least 10% of the value of each contribution
Your tax benefitA deduction for the present value of the remainder; no tax when the trust sells the shares; payments taxed to you as they come outSame as an annuity trust

CRTs involve legal and trustee costs, irrevocable decisions and yearly tax filings. They are usually considered only for large, highly appreciated assets — a concentrated stock position or a business. A simpler cousin, the charitable gift annuity, is a contract with a single charity that pays a fixed income for life.

How the law got here

Three laws that shaped giving

Case study 12006: giving straight from an IRA
What happened
The Pension Protection Act of 2006 added section 408(d)(8): up to $100,000 a year of distributions made directly from an IRA to a public charity after age 70½ would not be included in income. It excluded supporting organizations and donor-advised funds, and it was set to expire after 2007. Congress extended it several times until the PATH Act of 2015 struck the expiration date, making it permanent.
What it teaches
A tax break that began as temporary is now a permanent, inflation-adjusted planning tool — one of the most valuable available to retirees who give.
Source
Public Law 109-280, section 1201 (2006); Public Law 114-113, division Q, section 112 (2015); IRS Notice 2025-67.
Case study 22020–2026: a deduction for non-itemizers, gone and back
What happened
The CARES Act allowed a deduction of up to $300 of cash gifts for people who did not itemize, for 2020. Section 170(p) later provided $300 ($600 joint) for 2021. P.L. 119-21 section 70424 struck "$300 ($600" and "beginning in 2021," replacing them with $1,000 ($2,000) for tax years beginning after 2025, with no end date.
What it teaches
For the large majority who take the standard deduction, cash gifts now carry a modest federal benefit again — up to $220 a year on a $1,000 gift in the 22% bracket.
Source
Public Law 116-136, section 2204 (2020); Public Law 119-21, section 70424 (2025); IRS Topic 506.
Case study 32026: the first 0.5% no longer counts
What happened
For the first time, individual itemizers face a floor on charitable deductions: contributions are allowed "only to the extent that the aggregate of such contributions exceeds 0.5 percent of the taxpayer's contribution base."
What it teaches
Regular, modest giving by itemizers is now slightly less deductible, and bunching slightly more attractive, because the floor is lost once per itemizing year rather than once per gift year.
Source
Public Law 119-21, section 70425 (amending IRC §170(b)(1)).

Decisions

Choosing how to give

Scenario 1 · The Harpers

The Harpers give about $10,000 a year to their church and their alma mater, by check. They also want to trim their technology stocks. What should they change?

Keep writing checksThey save only the $2,000 non-itemizer deduction's worth each year — about $440 — because their itemized deductions do not reach $32,200.
Shares into a DAF, bunchedRight. Three years at once saves about $6,522 in the calculator; five years about $13,554. It also removes their most-appreciated shares from the concentrated account. The charities still receive $10,000 a year in grants.
Sell, then give cashThey would pay 15% (plus North Carolina's 3.99%) on the gain first. Giving the shares directly avoids that entirely.
The principle: give your most-appreciated shares, and give in the years your deductions count.
Scenario 2 · Ruth

Ruth gives $5,000 a year to her parish. She takes the standard deduction and withdraws $30,000 a year from her IRA. What should she do now, and at 70½?

Bunch into a DAFA $25,000 DAF gift (five years of giving) would save about $790 once (more if her property taxes were added to her itemized deductions), more than five years of $120 cash deductions. But a $10,000 bunch covering only the years before 70½ saves nothing, because it does not clear her $18,150 standard deduction, and money given from her IRA as QCDs from 70½ saves about $1,110 every year.
Cash, then QCDsRight. From 2026 she deducts $1,000 of cash gifts ($120 saved). Once she is 70½, a $5,000 QCD saves about $1,110 a year in today's rules and keeps her AGI lower for Social Security taxation and IRMAA. From 2031 QCDs also count toward her RMD.
The principle: for retirees over 70½ who give and do not itemize, the IRA is usually the best checkbook.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

An itemizer's contribution base (AGI) is $236,000. How much of their 2026 gifts is not deductible because of the 0.5% floor, in dollars?

0.005 × $236,000 = $1,180.
Exercise 2

You give shares worth $10,000 that cost you $3,000, held for years. At a 15% long-term rate, how much capital-gains tax do you avoid compared with selling first, in dollars?

Gain = $7,000; 15% × $7,000 = $1,050.
Exercise 3

In a bunched year the Harpers have $14,000 of state and local taxes, $3,500 of mortgage interest and $28,820 of deductible gifts after the floor. By how much do their itemized deductions exceed the $32,200 joint standard deduction, in dollars?

$14,000 + $3,500 + $28,820 = $46,320; $46,320 − $32,200 = $14,120, saving $3,106 at 22%.
Exercise 4

What is the most one IRA owner can give through qualified charitable distributions in 2026 and exclude from income, in dollars?

$111,000 (IRS Notice 2025-67), up from $108,000 in 2025. A married couple can each give up to that amount from their own IRAs.
