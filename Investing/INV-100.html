Job Loss, Career Change and Sabbaticals

A layoff, a buyout offer or a planned break all raise the same questions at once: how long the money will last, how to keep health coverage, what to do with the 401(k), and what to sign. The decisions in the first 60 days are the costly ones to get wrong. This module takes them in order.

The question this module answersMy paycheck has stopped, or I want it to. How do I protect my household and my retirement savings until the next one starts?

Start here

A gap between paychecks is a planning problem you can solve

You already know the basic arithmetic of a household: money comes in, bills go out. A job loss removes the biggest inflow while most of the outflows continue. Everything in this module is about the size and length of that gap, and about not turning a temporary problem into a permanent one by raiding retirement savings or losing health coverage.

Gaps are often longer than people expect. The Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) reports that in August 2026 the median spell of unemployment had lasted 11.4 weeks and the average 26.3 weeks, and 27.0% of unemployed people had been looking for 27 weeks or more. The average is pulled up by long spells, which is exactly why planning for the average is not enough, and why an emergency fund is measured in months, not weeks.

The last paycheck

Severance: taxed like wages, and often negotiable

Severance pay
What it is
Money an employer pays when a job ends, often a number of weeks of pay per year of service, sometimes with continued health coverage or outplacement help. It is usually offered in exchange for signing a release of legal claims.
Used for
Bridging the gap to the next job, and, for the employer, closing off lawsuits.
What it means for you
It is wages for tax purposes. Plan around the amount after withholding, and read the release before you sign it.

IRS Publication 525 states that severance pay is included in income, and that severance payments are wages subject to Social Security and Medicare taxes and income tax withholding. Employers often withhold federal income tax on severance at the flat supplemental-wage rate, which IRS Publication 15 (2026) keeps at 22% (37% on supplemental wages above $1 million in a year). That 22% may be more or less than your actual rate.

What to look at before signing

  • The release. Workers 40 and older have extra protection under the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act: the EEOC explains that a valid waiver of age-discrimination claims must give at least 21 days to consider it (45 days in a group layoff, with written information about who was selected) and 7 days to revoke after signing.
  • Timing. Payment split across two tax years can keep more income in lower brackets, especially late in the year.
  • Health coverage. An employer contribution toward COBRA premiums can be worth more than the same amount in cash.
  • Equity and bonuses. Ask what happens to unvested stock, a pending bonus and any employee stock purchase plan (ESPP) purchase period (INV-040).
  • Outplacement. Publication 525 notes that if you accept a reduced severance to receive outplacement services, you must still include the unreduced amount of severance in income.
Going furtherSeverance and unemployment benefits together

Whether severance delays or reduces unemployment benefits is decided by each state's law, not federal law, because states determine eligibility and benefit amounts (U.S. Department of Labor). File your claim anyway, report the severance accurately, and let the state decide. Delaying the claim can cost weeks of benefits.

The public safety net

Unemployment insurance: file first, ask questions second

Unemployment insuranceUI
What it is
A federal-state program, run separately by each state within federal guidelines, that pays temporary benefits to eligible workers who are unemployed through no fault of their own. In most states it is funded entirely by a tax on employers.
Used for
Replacing part of lost wages while you look for work.
What it means for you
It is insurance you have already paid for through your employer. The amount and length are set by your state; the benefit is taxable.

The U.S. Department of Labor's summary of unemployment insurance sets out the main features. You must have earned enough wages during a base period, which in most states is the first four of the last five completed calendar quarters before you file. Benefits are generally a percentage of your earnings over a recent 52-week period, up to a state maximum, and can be paid for a maximum of 26 weeks in most states, with extra weeks sometimes available when unemployment is high. The first payment generally takes two to three weeks; some states have a one-week waiting period. You must file weekly or biweekly claims, report any earnings and job offers, and meet your state's work-search rules.

Unemployment benefits are subject to federal income tax and must be reported on your return; you may choose to have tax withheld by the state agency (U.S. Department of Labor; IRS Publication 525). If you do not, set some aside or raise your estimated payments.

Going furtherQuitting, firing and gig work

If the separation was for a reason other than lack of work — a resignation or a firing for misconduct — the state decides whether you qualify under its law. Regular state programs are built on wages that employers report and pay unemployment tax on, so independent contractors often do not qualify; check with your state. INV-101 covers building your own cushion when self-employed.

Do not go uninsured

COBRA or a Marketplace plan

COBRA continuation coverage
What it is
A federal right, under the Consolidated Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act, to keep your employer's group health plan for a limited time after coverage would otherwise end. It generally applies to employers with 20 or more employees in the prior year.
Used for
Keeping the same plan, doctors and deductible progress through a job change.
What it means for you
You may pay the full cost, up to 102% of what the plan costs, instead of the employee share you paid before. Coverage usually lasts up to 18 months (36 in some cases), and you have 60 days to elect it.
Marketplace special enrollment
What it is
Losing job-based coverage opens a Special Enrollment Period: you can enroll in a HealthCare.gov or state Marketplace plan within 60 days of losing coverage (or in the 60 days before).
Used for
Buying individual coverage, with a premium tax credit if your income qualifies.
What it means for you
Premiums are based on your expected income for the year, which a job loss may lower. Compare before choosing COBRA.
⚠️

The trap: dropping COBRA later. HealthCare.gov warns that if you choose COBRA and then voluntarily cancel it or stop paying outside Open Enrollment, that does not qualify you for a Special Enrollment Period. You would have to wait for the next Open Enrollment unless another life event occurs. Decide carefully inside the first 60 days.

Premium tax credits changed for 2026. The enhanced credits that removed the 400%-of-poverty income cap from 2021 through 2025 expired at the end of 2025 (Congressional Research Service, R48290), so households above 400% of the federal poverty line generally receive no credit in 2026 unless Congress acts again. Check HealthCare.gov for the rules in force when you apply.

Going furtherHow long COBRA lasts, and Medicaid as a third option

The DOL describes COBRA as temporary coverage for you and your dependents, usually up to 18 months, which certain life events can extend to 36 months. The 60-day election window runs from when job-based coverage ends or when you receive the election notice, whichever is later. If a job loss drops household income far enough, Medicaid or CHIP may be available; HealthCare.gov notes you can enroll in those at any time, and advises waiting for a final Medicaid decision before ending COBRA. When COBRA runs out at the end of its term, that expiration does open a new 60-day Special Enrollment Period.

Protect the retirement money

Four choices for the old 401(k), and one to avoid

When you leave a job, your vested 401(k) balance stays yours. You generally have four choices: leave it in the old plan, roll it to an IRA, roll it to a new employer's plan, or cash it out. The first three keep it invested and tax-deferred. The fourth is usually the most expensive thing a job-seeker can do.

Direct rollover
What it is
Moving a distribution straight from the old plan to an IRA or new plan, or by a check made payable to the new account, so it never passes through your hands.
Used for
Avoiding the 20% withholding and the risk of missing the 60-day deadline of an indirect rollover.
What it means for you
If a check is made out to you, you must deposit the full amount, including the 20% withheld, within 60 days, using other money for the withheld part, or the shortfall is taxed.

Small balances and the Rule of 55

Plans may move small balances out without your consent. SECURE 2.0 section 304 permits a plan to raise its involuntary cash-out limit to $7,000, from $5,000 (IRS Notice 2024-03); balances between $1,000 and the limit are generally rolled into an IRA in your name, and balances of $1,000 or less can be paid to you. At the other end, the Rule of 55 lets you take penalty-free withdrawals from the plan of the employer you leave in or after the year you turn 55 — but only from that plan. Roll it to an IRA and the exception is lost. The IRS also lists an IRA-only exception for health insurance premiums paid by someone who received unemployment compensation for 12 consecutive weeks.

An outstanding 401(k) loan

Plan loan offset
What it is
When you leave with a 401(k) loan unpaid, many plans reduce (offset) your account by the unpaid balance. The IRS treats the offset as an actual distribution: taxable, and subject to the 10% additional tax if you are under the applicable age, unless it is rolled over.
Used for
Closing out the loan when repayment through payroll is no longer possible.
What it means for you
Since 2018, if the offset is due to leaving the job or the plan ending, you have until your tax return's due date, including extensions, to deposit the same amount into an IRA or plan and avoid the tax (IRS, Retirement plans FAQs regarding loans).

With the defaults (Maya's $290,000 401(k), no loan, leaving at 58, which is after the year she turns 55, a 29% federal rate and an assumed 3% state rate), cashing out would produce a $232,000 check after 20% withholding but only about $197,200 after all taxes. The 29% is about the average 2026 federal rate on a $290,000 lump sum added to $90,000 of pay and severance for a head of household: roughly $84,000 of extra tax, with the top slice in the 35% bracket (Rev. Proc. 2025-32). Because she leaves at 55 or older, the Rule of 55 spares her the 10% additional tax, but only on withdrawals from this plan. Left invested for nine years at 6%, until she is 67, the $290,000 would grow to about $490,000. Enter a loan balance to see how an unpaid loan becomes a plan loan offset.

Measure the runway

How many months can you last?

Pilots talk about runway: how much distance is left before the ground runs out. For a household between jobs, runway is the number of months your cash and benefits cover essential spending. It turns a vague fear into a number, and the number tells you how urgently to cut spending or find bridge income.

Runway (months) ≈ (Savings + severance after tax) ÷ (Essential spending + health coverage − benefits − other income)

The defaults are Maya's: her $30,000 of cash savings, about $14,900 of severance after withholding (12 weeks of pay, Figure 100.2), $5,200 of essential spending, an assumed $1,300 a month for a Marketplace plan covering her and Eli, and an assumed $500 a week of unemployment benefits for 26 weeks. The cushion lasts about 8.9 months, so if her search takes nine months Maya is about $600 short. Switch the health line to a COBRA premium of $1,734 (102% of an assumed $1,700 plan cost) and the runway shortens to about 8.4 months. Without the severance, $30,000 would last about 6.6 months. For Maya the lesson is that a real emergency fund buys time for a careful search, and closing her $4,200 shortfall buys a little more.

Going furtherKeep paying what protects you

While cutting, protect three things: health coverage, the minimum payment on every debt (a missed payment damages credit for years, INV-021) and any term life insurance your dependents rely on. Stop investing new money in taxable accounts, but keep the emergency fund's cash in an insured account rather than moving it into stocks for "higher returns" while you may need it next month.

Gaps you choose

Planning a career change or a sabbatical

A voluntary gap — retraining, starting a business, caring for family or a sabbatical — uses the same arithmetic as a layoff, with one advantage: you control the start date. That means you can build the runway first, line up health coverage in advance and choose a quarter that suits your taxes.

Sabbatical fund
What it is
Money saved in advance, on top of the emergency fund, to cover the income a planned break will cost.
Used for
Taking time off without draining the emergency fund or touching retirement accounts.
What it means for you
The emergency fund is for the unplanned. A planned break should be paid for by money saved for it, so a layoff after the break is still covered.
  • Keep retirement saving alive. If one spouse has no pay for a whole year, the Kay Bailey Hutchison spousal IRA rule lets a married couple filing jointly base an IRA contribution for the non-earning spouse on the other spouse's compensation (IRS Publication 590-A).
  • Mind the health coverage gap. Leaving voluntarily still triggers COBRA and a Marketplace Special Enrollment Period, because both are based on losing coverage, not on why.
  • Unemployment benefits generally will not apply if you quit, since eligibility requires being unemployed through no fault of your own under state law.
  • Starting a business? INV-101 covers self-employment taxes, quarterly estimates and retirement plans for the self-employed.

Real rules

Three laws that shape a job exit

Case study 1The Older Workers Benefit Protection Act, 1990
What happened
Congress amended the Age Discrimination in Employment Act to require that any waiver of age-discrimination claims be "knowing and voluntary." A waiver must give at least 21 days to consider the offer, which restarts if the final offer changes materially, and 7 days to revoke after signing; in a group layoff the employer must allow 45 days and disclose the job titles and ages of those selected and not selected.
What it teaches
If you are 40 or older, a severance offer that demands a same-day signature is not a valid age waiver. Use the time: have the agreement reviewed, and use the group-layoff disclosures to see whether age played a part.
Source
U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. Understanding waivers of discrimination claims in employee severance agreements.
Case study 2More time to rescue a 401(k) loan, 2018
What happened
Before 2018, a worker who left a job with a 401(k) loan outstanding had 60 days to roll over the offset amount. Effective January 1, 2018, when the offset is due to severance from employment or plan termination, the deadline became the due date, including extensions, of the tax return for the year of the offset.
What it teaches
A job-seeker who cannot find the money in 60 days now has until the following October, with an extension, to deposit it, perhaps from a new job's pay or a tax refund, and avoid tax and the 10% additional tax.
Source
Internal Revenue Service. Retirement plans FAQs regarding loans.
Case study 3SECURE 2.0 raises the small-balance limit, 2024
What happened
Section 304 of the SECURE 2.0 Act of 2022 permitted plans to raise their involuntary cash-out limit from $5,000 to $7,000 for distributions after 2023. The IRS's own rollover page still describes the older $5,000 figure, which shows how guidance can lag the law.
What it teaches
If you leave a job with a small balance, expect a letter. If you do nothing, a balance above $1,000 is generally moved to an IRA the plan sets up in your name. Choose where it goes, and how it is invested, yourself.
Source
Internal Revenue Service. Notice 2024-03: 2023 cumulative list of changes in plan qualification requirements.

Decisions

Three households, three exits

Scenario 1 · Maya

Maya, 58, is laid off with an offer of 12 weeks' severance in exchange for signing a release "by Friday." Her son Eli, 13, is on her health plan. What should Maya do first?

Sign and cash outAt 58, Maya is entitled to at least 21 days to consider an age waiver, so a Friday deadline is not binding for that waiver. Cashing out her $290,000 401(k) would add it to one year's income: roughly $84,000 of extra federal tax, with the top slice in the 35% bracket, even though the Rule of 55 spares her the 10% additional tax.
File, review, compareRight. Benefits start two to three weeks after filing, so every day of delay costs money. The release deadline and the 60-day health windows allow time to decide well, and choosing within them keeps Eli's coverage continuous. Retirement money is the last resort, not the first.
Refuse and sueOccasionally justified, but it gives up certain money for an uncertain outcome. A lawyer's review of the release is the proportionate first step.
The principle: claim what is yours immediately; sign and spend only after you have used the time the law gives you.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers

Tom, 57, is offered a voluntary separation package. The Harpers plan to live partly on Tom's 401(k) until they claim Social Security. An advisor suggests rolling the whole 401(k) to an IRA right away. What should they consider?

Roll it allAn IRA has no Rule of 55. Withdrawals before 59½ would generally owe the 10% additional tax unless another exception applies, which could cost thousands a year for two and a half years.
Keep the bridge money in the planRight. Leaving in or after the year he turns 55 lets Tom withdraw from this plan without the 10% tax. They can roll the rest later. They should also ask the plan which kinds of withdrawals it allows after separation.
The principle: know which exceptions belong to which account before moving money.
Scenario 3 · The Riveras

Marcus wants a six-month sabbatical in 2028 to retrain. Losing his pay would leave the family about $5,000 a month short. They have a $9,000 emergency fund and $41,000 idle in checking. How should they fund it?

Emergency fund and a loanLeaves the family exposed if Elena's job is hit during the break, and a 401(k) loan becomes taxable if Marcus's employment ends with it unpaid.
A separate sabbatical fundRight. Six months × $5,000 = $30,000 from money that is currently idle. They should also ask Elena's plan whether, and within how many days, Marcus could join it if he drops his own coverage during the break.
The principle: pay for a planned gap with money saved for it, and keep the emergency fund for the unplanned.

What a job loss would mean for each household

A one-month cushion is the biggest risk. Denise's $3,000 covers about a month. Building it toward three months is more urgent than extra investing, and her 401(k) should never be the backup.
Two incomes are a hedge, not a guarantee. With $50,000 in cash the Riveras could ride out one job loss for many months. Checking whether each spouse can join the other's health plan is a free, five-minute task.
One income, and a son who depends on it. Maya's $30,000 covers about 5.8 months of essential spending, second only to the Riveras here, and severance and benefits would stretch it to about 8.9 months. Her three checks: close the $4,200 gap to her $34,200 target, remember that the Rule of 55 lets her draw on this employer's 401(k) without the 10% additional tax (not after a rollover to an IRA), and keep Eli's health coverage continuous.
A buyout could be an early retirement. At 57, a package could become the start of retirement. The Rule of 55 and health coverage until Medicare at 65 (INV-079) are the two numbers to check.
Helping others through it. Ruth has no job to lose, but may be asked to help if Anna's job ends. A written gift or loan decision, sized so it does not dent her own income plan, protects both.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A family health plan costs the employer plan $1,200 a month in total. What is the most the plan can charge for COBRA coverage, in dollars per month?

COBRA may charge up to 102% of the cost to the plan: $1,200 × 1.02 = $1,224.
Exercise 2

You have $24,000 saved, spend $4,000 a month on essentials and receive $1,800 a month in benefits for 6 months. How many months does the money last?

Months 1–6 burn $4,000 − $1,800 = $2,200 a month: 6 × $2,200 = $13,200, leaving $10,800. Then $4,000 a month: $10,800 ÷ $4,000 = 2.7 months. Total 8.7 months.
Exercise 3

A $50,000 401(k) distribution is paid to you by check, with 20% withheld. To roll over the full $50,000 within 60 days, how much of your own money must you add, in dollars?

You received $40,000. Depositing $50,000 requires $10,000 of other money; the $10,000 withheld comes back as a credit on your tax return. A direct rollover would have avoided the problem.
Exercise 4

Your state estimates a weekly benefit of $450. About how much is that per month, in dollars?

$450 × 52 weeks ÷ 12 months = $1,950 a month, before any tax withheld. Multiplying by 4 ($1,800) understates it.
