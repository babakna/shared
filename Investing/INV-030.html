How Investment Income Is Taxed

A dollar of interest, a dollar of dividends and a dollar of profit on a stock sale can each carry a different federal tax, from 0% to more than 40%. This module sorts investment income into its tax families, gives the 2026 capital-gains thresholds, explains the two extra taxes that reach higher earners, and follows the Harpers as they think about selling part of a concentrated stock portfolio.

The question this module answersHow is each kind of investment income taxed, and how can I keep more of it without changing what I own?

Start here

Not all investment income is taxed alike

Your wages are taxed through the brackets in INV-029. Most people assume the money their investments earn is taxed the same way. Some of it is. Much of it is not.

For money inside a 401(k), individual retirement account (IRA) or other retirement account, that is the right assumption to make: nothing inside is taxed until it comes out, and then withdrawals from traditional accounts are ordinary income whatever produced them (INV-031, INV-032). But in an ordinary taxable account, every kind of return has its own tax treatment every year. The law favors long-term ownership of stocks: profits from shares held more than a year, and most dividends from US companies, are taxed at lower rates than wages. Interest and short-term trading profits get no such break.

The Harpers make this concrete. They are in the 22% bracket, with $220,000 in a taxable account holding 25 technology stocks. Figure 30.1 shows what $10,000 of each kind of investment income would cost them in federal tax in 2026.

The simplest case

Interest is ordinary income, with two important exceptions

Interest from bank accounts, CDs, corporate bonds and most bond funds is taxed as ordinary income, at the same rates as wages, in the year it is paid or credited to you. You receive a Form 1099-INT for it. Money market funds pay "dividends" that are reported as dividends but, economically, are interest, and they are not qualified dividends.

Tax-equivalent yield
What it is
The taxable yield that would leave you with the same after-tax income as a tax-free municipal bond.
Used for
Comparing a municipal bond with a taxable bond of similar quality and maturity.
What it means for you
Municipal bonds make more sense the higher your bracket. In the 12% bracket they rarely beat taxable bonds; in the 32%–37% brackets they often do.
tax-equivalent yield = municipal yield ÷ (1 − marginal tax rate)
Going furtherInterest you do not receive in cash

Some interest is taxable before you see it. A zero-coupon bond or a Treasury bill bought at a discount accrues interest ("original issue discount") that is generally taxed as it accrues on longer bonds. The inflation adjustment on the principal of Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) is taxable each year even though it is paid only at maturity. US savings bonds (EE and I) are the main exception: you can defer the interest until you cash them. These rules are why TIPS and zero-coupon bonds are usually better held in tax-advantaged accounts (INV-036).

Profits paid out

Qualified dividends get capital-gains rates

A dividend is a share of company profits paid to owners (INV-011). Since 2003, most dividends from US companies, and from many foreign companies, have been taxed at the lower long-term capital-gains rates if you meet a holding-period test. These are qualified dividends, shown in box 1b of Form 1099-DIV. Other dividends are "ordinary" and taxed like interest.

The holding-period testmore than 60 days in 121
What it is
To count as qualified, you must have held the stock for more than 60 days during the 121-day period that begins 60 days before the ex-dividend date. Count the day you sold but not the day you bought.
Used for
Denying the lower rate to someone who buys just before a dividend and sells just after.
What it means for you
Long-term owners and fund investors meet it almost automatically. Frequent traders often do not.

Dividends that are never qualified

  • Capital gain distributions from mutual funds and real estate investment trusts (REITs) (box 2a): these are taxed as long-term capital gains, however long you owned the fund.
  • "Dividends" from credit unions, savings banks and similar institutions, which are really interest.
  • Fund distributions reported only in box 1a and not box 1b. Money market and bond funds earn interest, so their distributions generally are not qualified.
  • Dividends on shares you were obligated to deliver elsewhere, as in a short sale.
Going furtherReinvested dividends are still taxed

A dividend reinvested in more shares is taxed exactly as if you had received it in cash, and the amount reinvested becomes the cost basis of the new shares. Keeping track of that basis prevents paying tax twice when you sell (INV-035). Funds that trade a lot also distribute capital gains each year, which is part of the tax drag in INV-007.

Profit on a sale

Short-term, long-term, and the 2026 thresholds

A capital gain is the difference between what you sell an asset for and its cost basis. Nothing is taxed while you simply hold an asset that has risen; the gain is "realized" when you sell. How it is taxed depends on how long you held it.

One year or lessShort-termOrdinary income rates, 10% to 37%

To count the holding period, start the day after you bought and include the day you sold. Shares bought on March 10, 2025 become long-term on March 11, 2026.

Special maximum rates: 25% on unrecaptured depreciation on real estate (INV-068), 28% on collectibles such as coins and art and on the taxable part of qualified small business stock gains (IRS Topic 409).

Stackingwhy the thresholds refer to total taxable income
What it is
Long-term gains and qualified dividends are placed on top of your ordinary taxable income. The 0% rate applies only to the part of the gains that fits under the 0% ceiling after ordinary income has filled the space below it.
Used for
Working out which rate each dollar of gain pays.
What it means for you
Low-income years, such as early retirement or a year between jobs, can be the time to realize gains at 0%. Gains never push your wages into a higher bracket, but they can reduce credits and trigger the extra taxes in the next tab.

Losses: netting, the $3,000 limit and carryovers

Capital losses first offset capital gains of the same type, then the other type. If losses exceed gains, up to $3,000 a year ($1,500 if married filing separately) can offset ordinary income, and the rest carries forward indefinitely. Losses on personal-use property, such as your car or home, are not deductible. Buying back the same security within 30 days disallows the loss under the wash-sale rule (INV-035, INV-037).

Practitioner depthThe netting order

Net short-term gains against short-term losses, and long-term against long-term (including loss carryovers of each type). If one net is a gain and the other a loss, net them. A net short-term gain is taxed at ordinary rates; a net long-term gain ("net capital gain") at 0/15/20%. A net loss of up to $3,000 reduces ordinary income, using short-term losses first. The Schedule D worksheets follow exactly this order (IRS Topic 409; Publication 550).

Try it

What will a sale cost?

The calculator starts with the Harpers. Their ordinary taxable income is about $170,800 (wages minus pre-tax deferrals and the standard deduction). They are considering selling half of their taxable account: stock worth $110,000 that cost $50,000, a $60,000 long-term gain, plus their usual $1,800 of qualified dividends.

With the default inputs the gains add $9,832 of federal tax: 15% of $61,800 ($9,270) plus $562 of net investment income tax (NIIT), because the sale lifts their modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) to about $264,800, $14,800 above the $250,000 threshold. Had the same stock been held for less than a year, the tax would have been $14,582, because the gains would be taxed at 22% and 24% instead of 15%. Try splitting the sale into two years of $30,000 each (enter $31,800 including dividends): each year's tax is $4,770, and no NIIT is due.

Going furtherState tax is on top

Most states tax capital gains as ordinary income, with no special rate, so a sale also costs state tax at your state's rate. A few states have no income tax, and a few give partial breaks. INV-041 compares state treatment.

Two extra layers

The 3.8% net investment income tax and the alternative minimum tax

Net investment income tax (NIIT)3.8%, since 2013
What it is
A 3.8% tax on the smaller of (a) your net investment income or (b) the amount by which modified AGI exceeds $200,000 (single or head of household), $250,000 (joint) or $125,000 (married filing separately).
Used for
Investment income such as interest, dividends, capital gains, rents and royalties, passive business income, and certain annuities. It does not apply to wages, unemployment compensation, Social Security benefits, distributions from 401(k), 403(b), 457(b) plans and IRAs, tax-exempt interest, or the excluded gain on a home sale.
What it means for you
It turns a 15% long-term rate into 18.8% and a 20% rate into 23.8% for affected income. Retirement-account withdrawals are not investment income for the NIIT, but they do raise MAGI and can push other investment income over the threshold.
NIIT = 3.8% × min(net investment income, MAGI − threshold)
Alternative minimum tax (AMT)
What it is
A second, parallel calculation with a large exemption, fewer deductions (no state and local tax deduction, for example) and two rates, 26% and 28%. You pay whichever of regular tax or AMT is higher.
Used for
Ensuring people with many tax preferences pay a minimum. Long-term gains and qualified dividends keep their 0/15/20% rates under the AMT, but they raise income used to phase out the exemption.
What it means for you
With the much larger exemptions in place since 2018, the most common trigger for ordinary investors is exercising incentive stock options and holding the shares (INV-040).

Practitioner depthThe ISO trap

When you exercise an incentive stock option (ISO) and keep the shares past year-end, the spread between the option price and market value is not regular income, but it is an AMT adjustment. IRS Publication 525 gives the example of 100 shares exercised at $9 when the stock is worth $16 when the rights become transferable: a $700 AMT adjustment. On thousands of shares that can create a large AMT bill even if the stock later falls. The AMT paid can become a credit in later years, but only slowly.

Real law and a real decision

Three cases

Case study 1The 2003 law that created "qualified dividends"
What happened
The Jobs and Growth Tax Relief Reconciliation Act of 2003 (P.L. 108-27, May 28, 2003) added section 1(h)(11) to the Internal Revenue Code, taxing qualified dividends at the same rates as long-term capital gains, starting with tax years after December 31, 2002. Before that, all dividends were taxed as ordinary income.
What it teaches
The lower rate on dividends is a legislative choice, not a law of nature, and it has survived every major tax law since. It makes dividend-paying stocks and stock funds relatively tax-efficient in taxable accounts, while bonds, whose interest is ordinary income, are usually better sheltered in retirement accounts (INV-036).
Source
26 U.S.C. §1(h)(11) and statutory notes (Legal Information Institute); IRS Publication 550.
Case study 2The net investment income tax, 2010
What happened
The Health Care and Education Reconciliation Act of 2010 (P.L. 111-152, March 30, 2010) added section 1411, a 3.8% tax on net investment income above $200,000 / $250,000 of modified AGI, effective for tax years beginning after December 31, 2012. The thresholds are written into the statute and are not adjusted for inflation.
What it teaches
Fixed dollar thresholds quietly reach more people each year. The additional 0.9% Medicare tax (INV-029) uses the same fixed thresholds, and the $3,000 annual limit on deducting capital losses is also a fixed dollar amount. When you see a threshold, check whether it is indexed.
Source
26 U.S.C. §1411 and notes; IRS, "Topic no. 559, Net investment income tax".
IllustrationThe Harpers' 25 technology stocks
The setup
$220,000 in 25 technology stocks that cost about $100,000, so about $120,000 of unrealized long-term gain. They want to diversify (INV-004) before retiring around 62–63.
The numbers
Selling everything in 2026 would realize $120,000 of gain: about $18,000 at 15% plus NIIT of 3.8% on the part of MAGI above $250,000, roughly $2,800, for about $20,800 in federal tax. Selling half in 2026 and half in 2027 costs about $19,100 in total, and spreading the sale over three or four years keeps each year under the NIIT threshold, for $18,000 at 15%.
What it teaches
Timing can save a few thousand dollars, but staying concentrated in one sector for four more years is a risk that could cost far more. Waiting only makes sense when the tax saving is large relative to that risk. INV-037 and INV-039 cover tools such as giving appreciated shares to charity, which avoids the gain entirely.

Scenario 1 · Ruth

Ruth keeps $60,000 in bank CDs. A Treasury bill ladder at her brokerage yields about the same. Does the tax treatment matter?

Interest is interestFor federal tax, yes. But Pennsylvania taxes CD interest and cannot tax Treasury interest.
Treasuries, modestlyRight. On $2,400 of interest the state saving is small, about $74 a year at Pennsylvania's 3.07% rate, but free. Her real decision is safety and access; both are safe (FDIC insurance versus US government backing, INV-008).
Municipal bondsIn the 12% federal bracket, a tax-free yield must be quite high to beat a taxable one: 3.0% tax-free equals only 3.4% taxable. Municipal bonds are mainly for higher brackets.
The principle: compare yields after the taxes you actually pay, federal and state.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

The Harpers' MAGI with the sale is $264,800 and their net investment income is $61,800. What is their 2026 net investment income tax, in dollars?

The tax applies to the smaller of net investment income ($61,800) and MAGI above the $250,000 joint threshold ($14,800): 0.038 × $14,800 = $562.40.
Exercise 2

A municipal bond yields 3.0% tax-free. What is its federal tax-equivalent yield for someone in the 24% bracket, in percent?

3.0 ÷ 0.76 = 3.95%. A taxable bond must yield more than that to leave more after federal tax.
Exercise 3

A single filer has $45,000 of ordinary taxable income and realizes $12,550 of long-term gains in 2026. What is the federal tax on the gains, in dollars?

The 0% ceiling for a single filer is $49,450. Ordinary income fills $45,000, so $4,450 of gains are taxed at 0% and the remaining $8,100 at 15%: $1,215.
Exercise 4

You have $5,000 of capital gains and $17,000 of capital losses this year. After deducting the maximum against ordinary income, how much loss carries forward, in dollars?

Losses first wipe out the $5,000 of gains, leaving a $12,000 net loss. Up to $3,000 offsets ordinary income; $9,000 carries forward.
