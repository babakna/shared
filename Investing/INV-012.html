Mutual Funds, Index Funds and ETFs

Most Americans own stocks and bonds through funds: at the end of 2025, US-registered investment companies managed $45.1 trillion, and index funds held a majority (52%) of long-term fund assets. This module explains how funds are built, priced and taxed, why an exchange-traded fund (ETF) and a mutual fund holding the same stocks can leave you with different tax bills, and how to read the documents that tell you what you are buying.

The question this module answersHow do I choose a fund, and what should I check before I buy one?

Start here

Pooling: one purchase, thousands of holdings

If you have a 401(k), you almost certainly already own funds. You picked from a short menu, money came out of each paycheck, and a balance appeared. That is a fund doing its job: letting ordinary amounts of money own a professionally run, diversified portfolio.

Mutual fund
What it is
The definition used by the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC): "an SEC-registered open-end investment company that pools money from many investors" and invests it in stocks, bonds, money market instruments or other assets. Each share is a proportional ownership of the whole portfolio.
Used for
Diversification, professional management and convenience, often with small minimum investments and automatic contributions.
What it means for you
You own a slice of everything the fund owns and pay its costs indirectly. Whether you should own it depends on three things: what it holds, what it costs, and how it is taxed in your account.

ETFexchange-traded fund
What it is
A fund registered with the SEC (usually as an open-end investment company) whose shares trade on a stock exchange at market prices throughout the day. Since the SEC adopted Rule 6c-11 on September 26, 2019, most ETFs operate under a single rule instead of the more than 300 individual exemptive orders the SEC had issued since 1992.
Used for
Low-cost, tax-efficient, diversified investing in any brokerage account; intraday trading if you want it.
What it means for you
For a long-term investor, an index ETF and an index mutual fund on the same index are close substitutes. The differences that matter are cost, tax efficiency in taxable accounts, and whether your platform supports automatic investing.
Going furtherNot everything that trades like an ETF is an ETF

The SEC's investor site notes that its ETF guidance covers only funds registered under the Investment Company Act of 1940, not other "exchange-traded products" such as exchange-traded commodity trusts or exchange-traded notes. Those products can carry very different risks; an exchange-traded note, for example, is an unsecured debt of its issuer. Leveraged and inverse funds are also a special case (INV-016).

Pricing

Net asset value, forward pricing and market prices

Net asset value (NAV)
What it is
The value of everything the fund owns, minus what it owes, divided by the number of shares.
Used for
The price at which mutual fund shares are bought and redeemed, and the benchmark for an ETF's market price.
What it means for you
A NAV of $25 is not "cheaper" than a NAV of $250. Only the percentage change matters.
NAV per share = (market value of assets − liabilities) ÷ shares outstanding

Worked example. A fund holds $500 million of securities and cash, owes $2 million (accrued fees and pending payments), and has 20 million shares outstanding. NAV = (500 − 2) ÷ 20 = $24.90.

Forward pricing. SEC Rule 22c-1 requires mutual fund purchases and redemptions to happen at a price based on the NAV "next computed after receipt" of the order. Place an order at 10 a.m. or 3 p.m. and you get the same price: that day's closing NAV. You cannot know the exact price when you order, and nobody can trade on a stale price.

Premiums and discounts. If an ETF's NAV is $50.00 and it trades at $50.10, you pay a 0.20% premium. Arbitrage by authorized participants (next tab) works to keep the gap small. SEC Rule 6c-11 requires ETFs to post each day's NAV, market price and premium or discount on their websites, with a count of days at a premium or discount; if the premium or discount exceeds 2% for more than seven consecutive trading days, the fund must say so and explain why. Check those figures, and use limit orders (INV-005).

Why ETFs are tax-efficient

Creation, redemption and capital gains

When you buy ETF shares on an exchange, you usually buy them from another investor; the fund itself is not involved. New ETF shares are created, and old ones retired, only in large blocks with a few big institutions.

Authorized participant (AP)
What it is
A large financial firm that has an agreement with an ETF to create and redeem its shares directly, in blocks that ICI describes as often 50,000 shares or more, at NAV.
Used for
Keeping the ETF's market price close to NAV: if shares trade above NAV, APs create new ones and sell them; if below, they buy shares and redeem them.
What it means for you
You never deal with an AP, but their arbitrage is why you can usually trade near NAV, and the way they redeem is why many ETFs rarely distribute capital gains.

The tax rule behind this is short. Section 852(b)(6) of the Internal Revenue Code says that the ordinary rule taxing a corporation on distributing appreciated property "shall not apply to any distribution by a regulated investment company ... if such distribution is in redemption of its stock upon the demand of the shareholder." The SEC's investor site summarizes the effect: because many ETFs use in-kind exchanges, "they typically have fewer capital gains distributions — and thus lower taxes — than mutual funds."

Capital gains distribution
What it is
A payout of the net gains a fund realized by selling securities. In the SEC's words, "At the end of the year, the fund distributes these capital gains, minus any capital losses, to investors."
Used for
Passing the fund's taxable gains through to its shareholders, who owe the tax even if they reinvest.
What it means for you
In a taxable account, a fund can hand you a tax bill in a year its price fell. ICI reports that mutual fund shareholders reinvested $704 billion of capital gains distributions in 2025. In an individual retirement account (IRA) or 401(k), distributions are not taxed when paid.

Worked example (illustration). You buy $10,000 of an actively managed stock fund in November at a NAV of $40. In December it distributes $2 a share of capital gains. Your 250 shares receive $500; the NAV drops to $38. You own the same $10,000 of value, but $500 of it is now taxable income for the year. In a taxable account, check a fund's estimated year-end distributions before buying in the fall.

What you pay for

Index versus active, and the share-class maze

Index fund
What it is
The SEC's definition: a mutual fund or ETF "that seeks to track the returns of a market index," such as the S&P 500, the Russell 2000 or a total bond market index. It may hold every security in the index or a representative sample.
Used for
Owning a whole market cheaply. The SEC notes that passive management "usually translates into less trading..., more favorable income tax consequences..., and lower fees."
What it means for you
You give up the chance to beat the market in exchange for reliably getting close to it, after very low costs. INV-043 to INV-045 examine the evidence on active versus passive.

INV-007 showed what the typical invested dollar paid in expense ratios in 2025: 0.40% for equity mutual funds, 0.14% for index equity ETFs and 0.05% for index equity mutual funds (ICI, asset-weighted). A fund's cost is one of the few things you know for certain before you buy, and every dollar of it comes straight out of your return.

Share classes

A single mutual fund often comes in several share classes: the same portfolio, different fees, sold through different channels. Two terms come up repeatedly: a sales load is a commission paid when you buy (front-end) or sell (deferred), and a 12b-1 fee is a yearly distribution and marketing charge paid out of fund assets and included in the expense ratio.

Going furtherETF share classes of mutual funds

On November 17, 2025 the SEC issued an order to Dimensional Fund Advisors and its funds granting the exemptions needed for a fund to offer "one class of exchange-traded shares that operates as an exchange-traded fund" alongside one or more mutual fund classes (Investment Company Act Release No. 35786). Such structures let one portfolio serve both mutual fund and ETF investors. Before buying, check whether the class you are offered is the ETF or a mutual fund class, and compare their expense ratios.

All-in-one funds

Target-date and balanced funds

Target-date fund
What it is
A fund of stock and bond funds whose mix becomes more conservative as a target year approaches. The SEC's example: a "Lifecycle 2060 Fund" is designed for people who plan to retire in or near 2060. The schedule of the changing mix is its glide path.
Used for
A complete, automatically rebalanced retirement portfolio in one fund; often the default investment in 401(k) plans.
What it means for you
Pick the fund, keep contributing, and do not add other funds that undo its mix. Check its glide path, its stock share at the target date, and its expense ratio; the SEC warns a fund that invests in other funds "may have a double layer of fees."

Target-date funds have become the core of workplace saving. ICI reports that target-date funds (mutual funds, collective investment trusts and other pooled products) rose from 7% of 401(k) plan assets at year-end 2008 to 42% at year-end 2023.

Balanced fund
What it is
A fund that holds a roughly fixed mix of stocks and bonds, such as 60/40, and rebalances back to it.
Used for
A one-fund portfolio for investors who want a steady mix that does not change with age.
What it means for you
Simple and disciplined. The mix is yours to change as your needs change; a target-date fund changes it for you.
Going furtherTarget-date funds in taxable accounts

Target-date and balanced funds rebalance internally, which can realize gains, and hold bonds whose interest is taxed as ordinary income. That is fine inside an IRA or 401(k) but can be tax-inefficient in a taxable account. The Vanguard case in the Case studies tab shows how large the distributions can get. INV-036 (asset location) and INV-050 (target-date funds and glide paths) go deeper.

Before you buy

How to read a fact sheet and a prospectus

Every fund publishes a prospectus, a legal document with its objective, strategies, risks, fees and management, and many also publish a shorter summary prospectus and a short fact sheet. Before investing, the SEC's investor site recommends carefully reading the fund's available information, "including its prospectus and most recent shareholder report"; both are free on the fund's website and on the SEC's EDGAR (Electronic Data Gathering, Analysis, and Retrieval) database.

The prospectus, section by section

  1. Investment objective and principal strategies: what the fund is trying to do and what it may own. A "total market" fund should say it tracks a total market index; a vague "growth" objective leaves the manager wide latitude.
  2. Fee table: shareholder fees (loads, redemption fees) and annual operating expenses, with an example of costs on $10,000 over 1, 3, 5 and 10 years (INV-007).
  3. Principal risks: read them; they are specific to the fund.
  4. Performance: a bar chart of annual returns and a table against a benchmark index, before and after taxes.
  5. Portfolio turnover: the share of the portfolio traded in a year. High turnover means more trading costs and, in taxable accounts, more taxable gains.
  6. Management, purchase and sale, taxes: who runs it, minimums, and how distributions are taxed.

Compare two funds, cost by cost

With the defaults — $50,000 for 20 years at 7% before costs — Fund A (a 0.04% index fund with 0.3% a year of tax cost) grows to about $181,600. Fund B (0.95% expense ratio, a 5.75% load, and 1.0% a year of tax cost from active trading) grows to about $126,200: about $55,300 less, and 35% less than the no-cost result. Switch the account to an IRA and the gap narrows to about $39,500, because only the load and expense ratio remain.

Real events

When a fund's structure mattered

Case study 1Vanguard's Target Retirement Funds, 2021
What happened
On December 11, 2020 Vanguard announced that it had lowered the minimum initial investment for its Institutional Target Retirement Funds from $100 million to $5 million. Many retirement plans then redeemed shares of the Investor Target Retirement Funds to switch to the cheaper institutional versions. According to the SEC, retail investors who stayed in the Investor funds in taxable accounts "faced historically larger capital gains distributions and tax liabilities." On January 17, 2025 the SEC announced that Vanguard would pay $106.41 million to settle charges that its prospectuses were misleading about this potential; Vanguard neither admitted nor denied the findings.
What it teaches
Mutual funds pool taxes as well as investments. Target-date funds are designed for tax-advantaged retirement accounts; in a taxable account, a broad index ETF usually leaves you more in control of when you pay tax.
Source
U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission. (2025, January 17). Vanguard to pay more than $100 million to resolve violations related to target date retirement funds (Press release 2025-21).
Case study 2The ETF rule, 2019
What happened
Until 2019 each ETF needed its own exemptive order from the SEC; the SEC had issued more than 300 since 1992. On September 26, 2019 it adopted Rule 6c-11, allowing ETFs that meet its conditions to operate under one standard rule. Among its conditions: daily website disclosure of the ETF's NAV, market price and premium or discount.
What it teaches
The rule lowered the cost of launching ETFs; ICI counts 4,576 new ETFs in the decade to 2025, and 1,425 closed or merged. Choice is abundant, which makes screening more important: a new, tiny ETF may close and return your money at an inconvenient time.
Source
U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission. (2019, September 26). Press release 2019-190; ICI, 2026 Investment Company Fact Book.

Decisions

Choosing funds for three households

Scenario 1 · The Riveras

The Riveras' $62,000 is spread across six overlapping large-cap funds in two 401(k)s, with no bonds or international stocks. What is the simplest improvement?

Add a seventhMore labels, same problem. Adding a fund can help only if it adds a missing piece, and they are missing bonds and international stocks, not a seventh US stock fund.
ConsolidateRight. One target-date fund per account, or three broad index funds, covers every dimension of INV-004 and usually costs less. Inside a 401(k) there is no tax cost to switching.
Keep all sixPast returns reflect a strong decade for large US stocks. Their plan should not depend on that repeating (INV-011's lost decade).
The principle: fewer, broader, cheaper funds usually beat many overlapping ones.
Scenario 2 · Maya

Once her emergency fund is full, Maya plans to add $300 a month to her taxable brokerage account, money she wants to be able to reach before retirement if Eli's care costs rise. The choice for the new money: an actively managed mutual fund a coworker likes, or a total-market index ETF.

Last year's winnerOne good year says little; high turnover in a taxable account also means yearly capital gains distributions, even when the fund loses money.
Index ETFRight. In a taxable account, cost and tax efficiency compound together. Run both through the comparison calculator with the funds' actual expense ratios and tax-cost ratios.
The principle: in taxable accounts, tax efficiency is part of the fund's cost.
Scenario 3 · The Harpers

A broker suggests the Harpers buy Class C shares of a balanced fund for $100,000 of their taxable account "since there's no up-front charge."

No up-front chargeClass C shares typically carry higher annual fees, including 12b-1 fees, every year you hold them. Over 10 or 20 years that usually exceeds a one-time load.
Read the fee tableRight. A balanced fund also rebalances internally in their taxable account, which can realize gains; holding bonds in their 401(k)s may be more tax-efficient (INV-036).
The principle: the question is total cost over your holding period, not whether a fee is visible today.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A fund has $500 million of assets, $2 million of liabilities and 20 million shares. What is its NAV per share, in dollars?

(500,000,000 − 2,000,000) ÷ 20,000,000 = $24.90.
Exercise 2

An ETF's NAV is $50.00 and it trades at $50.10. What premium are you paying, in percent?

50.10 ÷ 50.00 = 1.002, a premium of 0.20%.
Exercise 3

You own 250 shares of a fund in a taxable account. It distributes $2 a share of capital gains in December, and you reinvest. How many dollars of taxable distribution do you report?

250 × $2 = $500, taxable even though reinvested. Your cost basis rises by the reinvested $500 (INV-035).
Exercise 4

You invest $25,000 in Class A shares with a 5.75% front-end load. How many dollars go to the sales charge?

$25,000 × 0.0575 = $1,437.50. At $25,000 many funds offer a breakpoint that reduces the load; ask before you buy.
