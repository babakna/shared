Reviewing and Adjusting Your Plan

A good plan changes rarely, but it does change: when your life changes, when the law changes, and gradually as retirement approaches. This module gives you a once-a-year review you can finish in an afternoon, a clear test for what should and should not change the plan, and a way to keep your glide path on course.

The question this module answersHow do I keep my plan current without tinkering it to death?

Start here

A checkup, not a rebuild

You have probably heard that the best investors "set it and forget it." That is mostly right about the investments. It is wrong about the plan around them.

The funds in a well-built portfolio (INV-055) rarely need changing. But a plan is built on facts about your life — your income, your goals, your family, your tax rules, your age — and those facts change. A plan that ignores them slowly stops fitting: the beneficiary is an ex-spouse, the contribution rate was set three raises ago, the allocation still assumes a retirement date that has moved.

At the same time, a plan that changes every time markets move is not a plan. Morningstar estimates that poorly timed buying and selling cost the average fund dollar about 1.2 percentage points a year over the ten years ended 2024 (Ptak, 2025). The review in this module is designed to catch the first kind of change and screen out the second.

What this module covers

  1. An annual review checklist, with 2026 figures, and a calendar for spreading the work across the year.
  2. A two-question test for whether something should change your plan.
  3. What to review after each common life event.
  4. Glide paths: planning the gradual shift from stocks toward bonds, and checking where you are.
  5. A worked 2026 review for each household.

Once a year

A ten-item review you can finish in an afternoon

Annual review
What it is
A scheduled, once-a-year check of your whole financial plan against a fixed list, done on the same date each year and recorded in writing.
Used for
Catching the slow changes — outdated beneficiaries, contribution rates, allocations and tax items — before they become expensive.
What it means for you
Most years, the review ends with "no change." That is a successful review, not a wasted afternoon.

2026 numbers to update during the review

  • Workplace plans: $24,500 deferral limit; $8,000 catch-up at 50 and over; $11,250 for ages 60–63. If your 2025 wages from the employer that were subject to Social Security tax (FICA, Federal Insurance Contributions Act) exceeded $150,000, your 2026 catch-ups must be Roth (IRS, IR-2025-111; Notice 2025-67).
  • IRAs: $7,500, or $8,600 at 50 and over. Roth IRA phase-outs: $153,000–$168,000 single or head of household, $242,000–$252,000 married filing jointly.
  • Required minimum distributions generally begin at age 73 (IRS). Roth IRAs have no RMDs during the owner's lifetime.
  • New senior deduction: for 2025 through 2028, people 65 and older may deduct an extra $6,000 each, phasing out above $75,000 of modified adjusted gross income ($150,000 for joint filers), under the One, Big, Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21) (IRS).
Going furtherWrite the review down

A one-page record — date, net worth, allocation, what you checked, what you changed and why — turns the review into a history. In five years it shows whether your savings rate rose, whether costs fell, and whether you changed course at sensible moments or in panics. Keep it with your investment policy statement (INV-028) and your family financial file (INV-059).

The test

Two questions before any change

When something makes you want to change the plan, ask two questions. The first: has a fact about my life, my goals or the rules changed? The second: would I have written the plan differently if I had known this fact when I wrote it? If both answers are yes, change the plan. If the only thing that changed is the market, the answer to the first question is no, and your existing rules already cover it.

Stocks fall 30%NoRebalance into stocks if the band is breached (INV-056)

Going furtherWhat staying the course was worth in 2008–2013

Using calendar-year data: $100,000 in a 60/40 portfolio at the start of 2008 was worth $86,110 at year-end. Kept at 60/40 and rebalanced yearly, it reached $148,121 by the end of 2013. Moved to Treasury bills at the end of 2008 and back to 60/40 only at the start of 2011, it reached $119,072 — about $29,000 less. Planned change

A glide path is change you decide in advance

One change is supposed to happen every year: as the date you start spending gets closer, most plans shift gradually from stocks toward bonds, because there is less time to recover from a crash. Deciding that shift in advance, and writing it down, turns it from a yearly judgment call into a schedule.

Glide path
What it is
A schedule of how your stock share falls with age. The Securities and Exchange Commission's (SEC's) investor bulletin on target-date funds describes the glide path as the timing of the shift, typically from investing more in stock funds to more in bond funds as the target date nears.
Used for
Reducing the risk of a large loss just before or early in retirement, when it would do the most damage (sequence risk, INV-077).
What it means for you
If you own a target-date fund, the glide path is built in. If you build your own portfolio, write your glide path in your policy statement and check it at each annual review.

"To" or "through" retirement

Target-date glide paths come in two broad shapes. A "to" glide path reaches its most conservative mix at the target date and stays there. A "through" glide path keeps reducing stocks for years after the target date, on the view that retirement money must last decades. The SEC has warned that investors who focus on the date in a fund's name may not realize how much risk the fund still carries at and after that date, or that funds with the same date can hold very different mixes (INV-050).

The defaults are the Harpers: a personal glide path of 90% stocks 20 years before retirement, 50% at retirement at 62, and 40% ten years into retirement. At 57 the plan calls for 60%, which is their target in INV-055. After a strong year they hold 66%, a 6-point gap worth about $66,000 on $1.1 million. The plan also says the target itself falls about 2 points a year from here — to 58% next year — so part of each annual review is simply moving the target down on schedule.

Going furtherWhen to change the glide path itself

The glide path is part of the plan, so it changes only when the change test says so. A new retirement date moves the whole curve. A pension or annuity that covers most spending raises risk capacity and can justify a higher line; a large inheritance may lower risk need. Some planners favor a "rising equity" path in early retirement — holding fewer stocks at the retirement date and increasing them gradually — as a defense against a bad sequence of early returns (INV-077). Whatever the shape, set it in calm times and change it only for reasons about you.

Practitioner depthThe glide path is linear in this tool; yours need not be

The planner draws straight lines between three points. Published target-date glide paths often stay flat and high for decades, then decline more steeply in the 10 to 15 years before the target date. The shape matters less than two things: that the decline is gradual (no single year cuts stocks by 20 points) and that each year's target is known in advance. A yearly step of 1 to 3 points, combined with a rebalancing band of about 5 points, means most years the step and the rebalance happen together in one set of trades.

When the rules changed

Law changes that should trigger a review

Case study 1The SECURE (Setting Every Community Up for Retirement Enhancement) Act and inherited retirement accounts (2019)
What happened
For IRA owners and defined contribution plan participants who die after December 31, 2019, the SECURE Act requires the entire account to be distributed within ten years, with exceptions for a surviving spouse, a child who has not reached the age of majority, a disabled or chronically ill person, and a person not more than ten years younger than the owner. Before the change, many non-spouse beneficiaries could stretch withdrawals over their own life expectancy.
What it teaches
A law passed years after you wrote your plan can change how much your heirs keep. Ruth's IRA will pass to Anna, who is not an exempt beneficiary; Anna will have ten years to empty it, likely in her peak earning years. That makes Ruth's Roth conversion analysis (INV-038) and beneficiary choices (INV-091) worth revisiting — a textbook example of a fact that passes the change test.
Source
Internal Revenue Service, Retirement plan and IRA required minimum distributions FAQs.
Case study 2The 2025 senior deduction (Public Law 119-21)
What happened
The One, Big, Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025, created an additional deduction of $6,000 per person for taxpayers 65 and older for 2025 through 2028. It phases out for modified adjusted gross income over $75,000 ($150,000 for joint filers); married couples must file jointly to claim it; and it is available whether or not the taxpayer itemizes.
What it teaches
For Ruth, a temporary deduction interacts with decisions she controls: every extra dollar of IRA withdrawal or Roth conversion raises her income toward the phase-out. Her annual review for 2026 should re-run the size of any conversion with the deduction in mind. The deduction is scheduled to end after 2028, so the review must also note when the rule stops.
Source
Internal Revenue Service, One, Big, Beautiful Bill Act: Tax deductions for working Americans and seniors.
Case study 32008: a market event that did not pass the test
What happened
In 2008 the S&P 500 returned −36.6%. Many investors changed their plans, moving to cash. In 2009 the index returned +25.9% and in 2010 +14.8%.
What it teaches
Nothing about a household's goals or horizon changed in 2008; prices did. Figure 58.5 shows the cost of treating a market event as a life event. The investors who needed to lower risk were those who discovered they could not tolerate the losses — and they did better lowering it gradually, by rule, after the recovery.
Source
Damodaran, A. (2026), Historical returns on stocks, bonds and bills: 1928–2025, NYU Stern.

Decisions

Change, or stay the course?

Scenario 1 · The Harpers want to retire two years early

At their 2026 review Tom says he would like to retire at 60 rather than 62. What should the review do?

Wait on marketsThe decision is about their life, not the market. Waiting leaves the allocation set for a later date.
Re-planRight. A new retirement date passes both questions of the change test. In the planner, moving retirement from 62 to 60 lowers the target at 57 from 60% to about 56% stocks with the same three points; two fewer years of saving and two more of spending also matter (INV-075, INV-079).
All bonds nowOvercorrects. They may need their money for 30 years or more; a portfolio with too little growth risks running short late in life (INV-075).
The principle: a new date for a goal is a legitimate reason to change the plan — by re-planning, not by reflex.
Scenario 2 · The Riveras after a bad year

Stocks fell 20% this year and the Riveras' 401(k)s are down by about $10,000. Elena suggests moving to a 50/50 mix "until things settle." Their goals, jobs and horizon have not changed. What should they do?

Move to 50/50Sells stocks after the fall and usually buys back after the recovery: the pattern Morningstar measures as the investor return gap.
Keep the planRight. Nothing passes the change test. Their monthly contributions are now buying stocks cheaper. A calm, written adjustment later is fine if they learned something about their tolerance.
The principle: markets are handled by rules you already have; the plan changes for reasons about you.
Scenario 3 · Monica learns Eli's outlook

Just before her 2026 review, Eli's care team tells Monica that he will likely need support for the rest of his life. Her 401(k) and Roth IRA name Eli as sole beneficiary, and her only life insurance is a group policy worth one year's salary. What belongs on the review list?

Just the allocationMisses the urgent items. Money left to Eli directly could cost him Supplemental Security Income (SSI) and Medicaid, and without a will a court would choose his guardian (INV-084, INV-102).
The full listRight. This is a change about her life, so it passes the change test, but what it changes is the estate plan, not the mix. Her retirement date, income and tolerance for losses are the same, so the glide path stays where it is (INV-087).
The principle: each life event comes with a checklist; use it, and change only what the event actually touched.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A glide path runs in a straight line from 90% stocks at age 42 to 50% at age 62. What stock share does it call for at 57, in percent?

Fifteen of the 20 years have passed: 90 − (90 − 50) × 15/20 = 90 − 30 = 60%.
Exercise 2

The Harpers hold 66% stocks and their glide path calls for 60% on a $1,100,000 portfolio. How many dollars should move from stocks to bonds?

(0.66 − 0.60) × 1,100,000 = $66,000, best done inside their 401(k)s.
Exercise 3

A 35-year-old colleague of Marcus Rivera contributed $23,500 to her 401(k) in 2025 (the 2025 limit) and wants to contribute the maximum for 2026. By how many dollars must her 2026 contributions rise?

The 2026 limit is $24,500 (IRS, IR-2025-111): 24,500 − 23,500 = $1,000, about $83 more a month.
Exercise 4

From Figure 58.5: staying at 60/40 from 2008 ended 2013 at $148,121; stepping out to T-bills for 2009–2010 ended at $119,072. What did stepping out cost, in dollars?

148,121 − 119,072 = $29,049, on a portfolio that started at $100,000.
