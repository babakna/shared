Accessing Money Before 59½

Most retirement savings sit behind a wall: withdraw before age 59½ and you usually owe a 10% additional tax on top of income tax. The wall has doors. This module explains each one — the Rule of 55, 72(t) payments, Roth contributions and conversion ladders, taxable accounts — and the other wall early retirees hit: health insurance before Medicare at 65.

The question this module answersIf I stop working before 59½, how do I pay for the years in between without penalties or a health-insurance shock?

Start here

The wall, and why most people never notice it

If you retire at 62 or later, you may never meet this problem: by then you are past 59½ and can withdraw from IRAs and 401(k)s with only ordinary income tax. But anyone who stops full-time work earlier — by choice, by buyout or by layoff — discovers that most of their savings carry a 10% early-withdrawal tax for a few more years.

The tax comes from section 72(t) of the Internal Revenue Code. It adds 10% of the taxable amount withdrawn before 59½ from 401(k)s, 403(b)s, IRAs and similar accounts, unless an exception applies. The IRS lists about 30 exceptions; four matter most for planned early retirement, and Figure 74.1 places them on a timeline alongside the two health-insurance milestones.

What this module covers

  1. The Rule of 55: penalty-free access to a workplace plan when you leave that employer.
  2. 72(t) payments: the three IRS methods, the 2026 interest-rate cap, and the lock-in rules, with a calculator.
  3. Roth access: contributions anytime, and the conversion ladder with its five-year clocks.
  4. Taxable bridges: brokerage accounts, the 0% capital gains rate, and a bridge-years planner.
  5. Health insurance before 65: COBRA continuation (Consolidated Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act), marketplace coverage and the 2026 premium tax credit rules, with a calculator.

Door 1

The Rule of 55

The simplest door belongs to workplace plans. If you leave your job in or after the calendar year you turn 55, withdrawals from that employer's plan are exempt from the 10% additional tax. It does not matter whether you quit, retire or are laid off.

Separation from servicethe trigger for the Rule of 55
What it is
Ending your employment with the employer that sponsors the plan. Under the IRS exception, the separation must happen "during or after the year the employee reaches age 55."
Used for
Unlocking penalty-free withdrawals from that employer's 401(k), 403(b) or similar plan (Internal Revenue Code section 72(t)(2)(A)(v)).
What it means for you
Someone who turns 55 in December can leave in January of that year, at 54, and still qualify. Someone who leaves at 53 cannot use it for that plan, even after turning 55.

Public safety employees get an earlier door. The IRS applies the same exception at age 50 for public safety employees of a state or local government in a governmental plan, and the category now also includes specified federal law enforcement officers, corrections officers, customs and border protection officers, federal and private-sector firefighters, and air traffic controllers. A related rule is even simpler: distributions from a governmental 457(b) plan are not subject to the 10% additional tax at all, except for amounts that were rolled in from another type of plan or IRA.

Going furtherPractical points before you rely on it
  • The plan decides the logistics. The tax exception allows penalty-free withdrawals; your plan's rules decide whether you can take partial withdrawals whenever you like or must take the whole balance at once. Read the summary plan description before you leave.
  • Consolidate before, not after. If you have old 401(k)s from previous employers, many plans accept roll-ins while you still work there. Money rolled into your current plan before you separate is then covered by the rule when you leave.
Practitioner depthWhich plan, which employer

The exception follows the employer whose service you leave. Leaving Employer B at 56 covers Employer B's plan; a 401(k) left behind at Employer A when you departed at 45 is not covered, even though you are now past 55, because you separated from A before the year you turned 55. For dual-income couples, each spouse's eligibility is separate: if Tom Harper leaves at 58 and Rachel keeps working, only Tom's plan is unlocked.

Door 2

72(t): substantially equal periodic payments

The second door works at any age and for IRAs as well as plans. If you take a series of "substantially equal periodic payments" calculated by one of three IRS methods, the 10% additional tax does not apply. In exchange, you must keep taking exactly that series until the later of five years after the first payment or age 59½.

72(t) distributionsalso called SEPP
What it is
A schedule of withdrawals from one IRA or plan, sized by an IRS method based on your life expectancy, that avoids the 10% additional tax (Internal Revenue Code section 72(t)(2)(A)(iv)).
Used for
Creating penalty-free income from pre-tax savings before 59½ when the Rule of 55 does not apply.
What it means for you
It is a commitment. Changing the payments early (other than for death or disability) makes the 10% tax due on every payment taken so far, plus interest.

The three methods (IRS Notice 2022-6)

MethodHow the annual amount is setChanges each year?
Required minimum distribution (RMD) methodAccount balance ÷ life expectancy from the chosen table for your age that yearYes, recomputed every year; usually the smallest payment
Fixed amortization methodThe level payment that pays off the balance over your life expectancy at a permitted interest rateNo, fixed at the first year's amount
Fixed annuitization methodBalance ÷ an annuity factor built from IRS mortality rates and a permitted interest rateNo, fixed at the first year's amount
Applicable federal ratethe interest-rate cap
What it is
Interest rates the IRS publishes monthly by revenue ruling for short-, mid- and long-term loans. For 72(t), the rate you use may not exceed the greater of 5% or 120% of the federal mid-term rate for either of the two months before the month of your first payment.
Used for
Setting the fixed amortization and fixed annuitization payments: a higher rate produces a higher payment.
What it means for you
The 5% floor was added by Notice 2022-6, replacing a cap tied only to the federal rate. For September 2026, 120% of the federal mid-term rate was 5.40% (annual compounding, Rev. Rul. 2026-17), so a series whose first payment comes in October or November 2026 may use up to 5.40%.
Amortization: payment = B ÷ [(1 − (1 + i)−n) ÷ i]
Annuitization: payment = B ÷ Σt [pt ÷ (1 + i)t]

Here B is the balance, n the life expectancy from the table, i the interest rate, and pt the probability of being alive t years from now using the mortality rates in 26 CFR 1.401(a)(9)-9(e). The calculator below reproduces the IRS's own worked example to the dollar: at age 50 with $400,000 and a 4% rate, the RMD method gives $11,050, fixed amortization $21,102 and fixed annuitization $22,030.

Going furtherRules that trip people up
  • One account at a time. Each series is computed for one account; you cannot combine balances. You can split an IRA first so the series account holds exactly the amount needed for the payment you want, and keep the rest untouched and flexible.
  • No additions or extra withdrawals. Contributions to, transfers from or extra withdrawals from the series account count as modifications.
  • One permitted switch. You may switch once, from fixed amortization or annuitization to the RMD method, without it counting as a modification. You cannot switch back.
  • Plans require separation first. For 401(k)s and similar plans, you must have left that employer before the payments begin; IRAs have no such condition.
  • Running out is allowed. If following the method exhausts the account, the smaller final payment is not a modification.
Practitioner depthSizing the series and the recapture math

Planners typically decide the annual income needed first, then solve for the balance: at 5% and age 50, fixed amortization pays about 6.03% of the balance a year (1 ÷ 16.5804), so $30,000 a year needs an account of about $497,000. The lock-in lasts until the later of the fifth anniversary of the first payment or 59½: someone starting at 57 is locked in until 62, not 59½. If the series is broken, the recapture tax equals the 10% that would have applied to every prior payment, plus interest for the deferral period, and the current year's withdrawals are taxed at 10% as well (IRS Q&A 9).

Door 3

Roth contributions and the conversion ladder

Roth IRAs have their own rules for early withdrawals, and they are friendlier than most people assume. The key is the IRS's ordering rules: when you take money out of a Roth IRA before it is a qualified distribution, it is treated as coming out in a fixed order.

Roth ordering rulesIRS Publication 590-B
What it is
The order in which a nonqualified Roth IRA withdrawal is deemed to come out: first regular contributions, then conversion and rollover contributions (oldest first, taxable part first), and last the earnings.
Used for
Working out whether any part of an early Roth withdrawal is taxable or subject to the 10% additional tax.
What it means for you
The regular contributions you made are already taxed, so you can take them back at any age without tax or penalty. Only after all of them are out do conversions and then earnings come into play.

Conversions have five-year clocks. Converting pre-tax money to a Roth is taxable income in the year of conversion (INV-038). If you then withdraw the converted amount before 59½, the 10% additional tax applies to the taxable part of any conversion made within the previous five years. Each conversion has its own five-year period, starting on January 1 of the tax year of the conversion; the IRS's example is a conversion on February 25, 2025, whose clock starts January 1, 2025. That conversion can be withdrawn without the 10% tax from January 1, 2030.

Roth conversion laddera five-year pipeline
What it is
Converting a slice of pre-tax money to Roth every year after you stop working, then withdrawing each slice once its five-year clock has run.
Used for
Turning a large 401(k) or IRA into penalty-free early-retirement income, while paying tax on each slice in low-income years.
What it means for you
The first five years are the hard part: you need other money (taxable accounts, cash, Roth contributions) to live on until the first rung matures.

Converting in low-income years can cost little tax. For 2026, the standard deduction is $32,200 for married couples filing jointly and $16,100 for single filers. A retired couple with no other income could convert $32,200 in 2026 and owe no federal income tax on it; the 12% bracket for joint filers runs to $100,800 of taxable income, so conversions up to $133,000 of total income stay at or below 12% (state taxes differ, INV-041). But every converted dollar also raises the income used for marketplace premium credits, as the health-insurance tab shows.

Going furtherTwo different five-year rules

Do not confuse the conversion clocks with the five-year rule for qualified distributions. Earnings come out tax-free only once your first Roth IRA has been open five years (from January 1 of the year of your first contribution) and you are 59½, disabled, or using up to $10,000 for a first home. The conversion clocks decide only whether the 10% additional tax applies to converted principal before 59½. Each is counted separately.

Practitioner depthRoth contribution limits for 2026

For 2026, the IRA contribution limit is $7,500, plus a $1,100 catch-up at 50 or older. Direct Roth IRA contributions phase out between $153,000 and $168,000 of modified adjusted gross income for single filers and between $242,000 and $252,000 for joint filers. The Harpers, at $260,000, are above the joint range and would need the backdoor route (INV-032, INV-038) to add to their $60,000 of Roth IRAs.

Door 4, and the planner

Taxable accounts and the bridge-years planner

Ordinary brokerage accounts have no age rules at all. You can sell at 45 or 55 and owe only capital gains tax on the gain, not on the whole amount. For early retirees, that makes a taxable account the most flexible bridge.

Bridge yearsthe gap to plan for
What it is
The years between stopping work and reaching the ages when penalty-free withdrawals, Social Security and Medicare begin.
Used for
Deciding how much money must be reachable without penalty, and in which accounts, before 59½.
What it means for you
A household can be "FI on paper" with most of its money in a 401(k) and still be unable to pay the bills at 50 without the tools in this module.

The 0% capital gains rate. For 2026, long-term capital gains and qualified dividends are taxed at 0% up to $98,900 of taxable income for joint filers, $66,200 for heads of household and $49,450 for single filers. Because ordinary income fills the brackets first, a couple with little other income can sell appreciated investments and pay no federal tax on the gain, a technique called tax-gain harvesting (INV-037). Selling $50,000 of stock with a $20,000 cost basis creates $30,000 of gain, not $50,000.

The planner below puts the doors together. Enter what you have in each kind of account and it spends them in a sensible order each year from the age you stop to 59½: cash, then taxable money, then Roth contributions, then conversions that have passed their five-year clock, then any 72(t) payment, then the 401(k) under the Rule of 55 if you qualify. The defaults show a common problem: a 50-year-old with $700,000 in pre-tax accounts who runs short at 53 while waiting for the first conversion rung.

Going furtherOther doors worth knowing
  • Governmental 457(b) plans have no 10% additional tax on distributions (except on money rolled in from other plans or IRAs), which makes them unusually useful for public employees who retire early.
  • Health savings accounts pay for qualified medical expenses tax-free at any age (INV-033).
  • Other IRS exceptions include total and permanent disability, unreimbursed medical expenses above 7.5% of adjusted gross income, health insurance premiums paid from an IRA while unemployed, and one emergency personal expense distribution of up to $1,000 a year. None is designed as a retirement-income plan.
Practitioner depthBuild the bridge before you need it

The cheapest bridge is built while you still work: directing some saving to a taxable account once the match and HSA are captured, starting conversions in a lower-income year before retirement, and rolling old 401(k)s into your current plan so the Rule of 55 covers them. Babak's Retirement Planner models multi-phase spending with Roth conversions and account-by-account withdrawals, which is the next step once this planner has shown you the shape of the gap.

The second wall

Health insurance before Medicare

Medicare begins at 65 for most people. Until then, an early retiree needs another source of coverage, and for many it is the largest new cost of leaving work. There are four main options.

OptionHow it worksWatch for
A spouse's or part-time employer's planGroup coverage through a working spouse or a job with benefitsTies the retirement date to someone else's job
COBRAKeep your former employer's plan, usually for up to 18 months after leaving a job (employers with 20 or more employees)You may pay up to 102% of the plan's full cost. Losing job-based coverage opens a 60-day marketplace special enrollment period; voluntarily dropping COBRA later does not
Marketplace (Affordable Care Act, ACA) planIndividual coverage through HealthCare.gov or a state marketplace, with premium tax credits based on incomeFor 2026, no credit above 400% of the poverty line
MedicaidIn states that expanded Medicaid, adults with income up to about 138% of the poverty line generally qualifyRules vary by state; asset-rich, income-poor early retirees may qualify
Premium tax creditmarketplace subsidy
What it is
A federal tax credit, usually paid in advance each month to your insurer, that lowers the premium for a marketplace plan. It equals the premium of the benchmark plan (the second-lowest-cost silver plan in your area) minus an expected contribution, which is a percentage of your household income.
Used for
Making individual coverage affordable for people without job-based insurance, including early retirees.
What it means for you
Because it depends on your income for the year, including Roth conversions, capital gains and IRA withdrawals, an early retiree can raise or lower the credit by choosing where spending money comes from.

What changed in 2026

From 2021 through 2025, temporary "enhanced" credits made subsidies larger and removed the 400% income cap. They expired on December 31, 2025. The House passed a three-year extension on January 8, 2026, by 230 to 196, but as of this module's writing in September 2026 no extension had become law, and KFF's August 2026 analysis of 2027 premiums treats the enhanced credits as expired. For 2026, the original rules therefore apply:

  • Income range. Credits are available from 100% to 400% of the federal poverty line. For 2026 coverage, the poverty line used is the 2025 guideline of the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS): $15,650 for one person and $21,150 for two (48 contiguous states and DC). The 400% cutoffs are $62,600 for one and $84,600 for two.
  • Expected contribution. Under Rev. Proc. 2025-25, the 2026 applicable percentages run from 2.10% of income below 133% of the poverty line to 9.96% from 300% to 400%.
  • Full repayment. Under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025, there is no longer a cap on repaying excess advance credits. If your income ends the year higher than estimated, you repay the whole difference.
Going furtherWhat premiums look like

KFF reports that the average 2026 benchmark premium for a 40-year-old was $625 a month nationally; by state it was $532 in Arizona, $557 in Colorado, $638 in North Carolina, $513 in Ohio and $572 in Pennsylvania. Premiums rise with age: federal rules allow insurers to charge adults up to three times as much by age as they charge a 21-year-old, so a couple in their late fifties typically pays far more than twice the 40-year-old figure. Get real quotes for your ZIP code and ages at HealthCare.gov or your state marketplace before you set a retirement date.

Practitioner depthManaging marketplace income

Marketplace income is modified adjusted gross income: adjusted gross income plus tax-exempt interest, nontaxable Social Security benefits and excluded foreign income. Withdrawals of Roth contributions and spending from cash or from the cost basis of taxable investments do not add to it; Roth conversions, pre-tax withdrawals, realized gains, dividends and interest do. That creates a real trade-off with the conversion ladder: converting $40,000 might save tax decades later but eliminate a five-figure credit this year if it pushes income over 400%. Model both in the same year, and remember that with full repayment, an underestimate is costly.

Rules in action

Three official examples and changes

Case study 1The IRS's own 72(t) example: "Bob," age 50
The setup
Bob turns 50 in 2023 and has $400,000 in an IRA. He chooses a 4% interest rate (below the 5% cap) and the Single Life Table, which gives a 36.2-year life expectancy.
The result
RMD method: $400,000 ÷ 36.2 = $11,050 in the first year, recomputed yearly. Fixed amortization: $400,000 ÷ 18.9559 = $21,102 a year. Fixed annuitization: $400,000 ÷ 18.1568 = $22,030 a year. At 5.40%, the highest rate a series starting in October or November 2026 may use, the same balance would support about $25,382 (amortization) or $26,535 (annuitization).
What it teaches
The method and rate can double the payment from the same account. Choose the payment you need, then size the account to fit, rather than the other way round.
Source
IRS, Substantially equal periodic payments (FAQ, Q&A 7); IRS Notice 2022-6. The 5.40% figures are computed with the same method.
Case study 2The enhanced premium tax credit, 2021–2026
What happened
The American Rescue Plan Act of 2021 temporarily enlarged marketplace credits and removed the 400% income cap; the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 extended those rules through 2025. They expired at the end of 2025. The House voted 230–196 on January 8, 2026 to revive them for three years; the Senate had not passed an extension as of this writing.
What it teaches
Early-retirement plans that assumed subsidies above 400% of the poverty line lost them overnight. Health-insurance costs are a policy risk, not just a price; plan for the rules in force and treat any extension as a bonus.
Source
IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-25; Ballotpedia News (January 12, 2026); KFF, How much and why ACA Marketplace premiums are going up in 2027 (updated August 3, 2026).
Case study 3Full repayment of advance credits, from 2026 (illustration)
The setup
A couple estimates 2026 income of $80,000 and receives advance credits based on it. With an illustrative $1,800 monthly benchmark premium, the credit is $21,600 − 9.96% × $80,000 = $13,632 for the year.
The result
A late-year stock sale pushes their actual income to $86,000, above the $84,600 cutoff. Their allowed credit becomes zero, and because the One Big Beautiful Bill Act removed the repayment caps for tax years after 2025, they must repay the full $13,632 with their tax return.
What it teaches
Near the 400% line, a capital gain or conversion is not just taxed; it can cost the entire credit. Realize gains and conversions deliberately, and update your marketplace income estimate when plans change.
Source
IRS, One, Big, Beautiful Bill provisions (removal of limitations on repayment of excess advance premium tax credit); Rev. Proc. 2025-25. Figures illustrative.

Decisions

Open the right door

Scenario 1 · The Harpers

Suppose Tom (57, turning 58) accepts a buyout this year and Rachel (55) also stops work. They want about $110,000 a year until Medicare. Tom's first instinct is to roll his 401(k) into an IRA for more fund choices. What should they do?

Roll and 72(t)Rolling to an IRA gives up the Rule of 55, and a 72(t) series would lock them into fixed payments until 62 or later. They would be solving a problem they do not have.
Keep the plans, manage incomeRight. Both leave in or after the year they turn 55, so both plans are unlocked. Spending from the $220,000 taxable account's cost basis and from Roth contributions does not count as marketplace income, which can keep the credit (about $13,000 a year in Figure 74.7's illustration) while they wait for Medicare.
All from the 401(k)Penalty-free, but $110,000 of taxable withdrawals means $110,000 of marketplace income: no premium credit at all and higher income tax. The source of money matters as much as the amount.
The principle: do not roll over the account that holds your Rule of 55 door.
Scenario 2 · Monica

Suppose Monica's hospital outsources its billing office and her job ends at 58. She expects to need about six months of spending, roughly $31,200 at $5,200 a month, before a new job starts, and her $30,000 of cash is her only emergency money. A bank representative suggests rolling her $290,000 401(k) into an IRA and starting a 72(t) series. What fits?

Roll over and 72(t)A series started at 58 must continue until the later of five years or 59½, that is, to 63, even after she finds work; changing it early triggers the 10% tax on every payment taken, plus interest. The rollover also gives up the Rule of 55. It solves a problem of months with a five-year commitment.
Keep the plan; use the Rule of 55Right. She leaves in or after the year she turns 55, so withdrawals from that employer's 401(k) carry no 10% additional tax (income tax still applies). Her cash, her $40,000 taxable account and her Roth IRA contributions come first, and within 18 months she reaches 59½, when every door opens. Once she is working again, refilling the emergency fund comes first.
The principle: match the door to the date; a five-year commitment does not fit a gap of months, and the Rule of 55 depends on when you leave, not how old you are when you withdraw.
Scenario 3 · Jordan

Jordan (24) has only $3,000 of savings. Should she put new savings into a Roth IRA even though she might need some of it before retirement?

Locked until 59½Not true for contributions. Under the ordering rules, regular contributions come out first and are never taxed or penalized.
Yes, within limitsRight. For 2026 she can contribute up to $7,500. The flexibility is a safety valve, not an invitation: every dollar taken out loses decades of tax-free growth, and each year's contribution limit caps how much can go back in (INV-019, INV-032).
The principle: Roth contributions are the most flexible retirement dollars you can own; keep them invested unless there is no other choice.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

You are 52 and start a 72(t) series from a $500,000 IRA using the RMD method and the Single Life Table (life expectancy 34.3 at 52). What is the first year's payment, in dollars?

$500,000 ÷ 34.3 = $14,577. Next year it is recomputed with the new balance and the age-53 factor.
Exercise 2

You convert $40,000 to a Roth IRA in November 2026 at age 48. From January 1 of which year can you withdraw that converted amount without the 10% additional tax?

The five-year period begins January 1 of the tax year of the conversion, 2026, and ends after five years: available from January 1, 2031, when you are 52 or 53.
Exercise 3

A household of two has 2026 marketplace income of $60,000 and a benchmark premium of $1,500 a month. Using the 2026 table (8.44% at 250% of the poverty line rising in a straight line to 9.96% at 300%), what is the annual premium tax credit, in dollars?

$60,000 ÷ $21,150 = 283.69% of the poverty line. Applicable percentage = 8.44% + (283.69 − 250) ÷ 50 × 1.52% = 9.464%. Expected contribution = $60,000 × 9.464% = $5,678. Credit = $18,000 − $5,678 = $12,322.
Exercise 4

At 53, someone who left their employer at 50 withdraws $40,000 of pre-tax money from that old 401(k), with no exception applying. How much is the additional tax, in dollars, on top of income tax?

They separated before the year they turned 55, so the Rule of 55 does not apply. 10% × $40,000 = $4,000.
