Trusts

A trust is a set of instructions attached to money: who manages it, for whom, and on what terms. Trusts are sold as if every family needs one and dismissed as if only the rich do. Both are wrong. This module explains the main types, what each does and does not do, how trusts are taxed, and how to decide whether one is worth its cost for you.

The question this module answersWould a trust solve a real problem for my family, and which kind?

Start here

A trust is instructions attached to money

If you have ever left money with a relative "for the kids, for college," you have already imagined a trust. Someone holds the money, for someone else, under rules you set. A trust puts that arrangement in a legal document and makes the rules enforceable.

For a great many families the ordinary tools — a will, beneficiary designations and powers of attorney (INV-084, INV-085) — are enough, and a trust would add cost without solving a problem. A trust earns its place when there is a specific job to do: managing money for children or a disabled relative, keeping a house out of probate in a state where probate is costly, planning for incapacity, providing for a spouse while protecting children from a first marriage, or removing growth or life insurance from a taxable estate.

Trust
What it is
A legal arrangement in which a trustee holds property for the benefit of beneficiaries under terms set by the grantor. See trust.
Used for
Managing money for people who cannot or should not manage it, avoiding probate, planning for incapacity, and certain tax and asset-protection goals.
What it means for you
A trust only controls assets actually placed in it or payable to it. An elegant trust document holding nothing does nothing.

Drafting errors are costly and often irreversible for irrevocable trusts. Work with an attorney licensed in your state.

The most common trust

The revocable living trust, and funding it

Revocable living trust
What it is
A trust you create during life and can change or cancel at any time. You usually serve as your own trustee and beneficiary while alive, and name a successor trustee to take over at your incapacity or death. See revocable living trust.
Used for
Avoiding probate for assets titled to the trust, including real estate in several states; seamless management if you become incapacitated; privacy; and holding assets for heirs after death.
What it means for you
While you live, the IRS and your creditors treat the assets as yours. It saves no income tax, estate tax or Pennsylvania inheritance tax.

That last point surprises many buyers. Because you can revoke the trust, its assets are included in your gross estate for federal estate tax (26 U.S.C. § 2038 covers property you could "alter, amend, revoke, or terminate"). In Pennsylvania, "during the lifetime of the settlor, the property of a revocable trust is subject to claims of the settlor's creditors" (20 Pa.C.S. § 7745(1)). Pennsylvania also presumes a trust is revocable unless its terms expressly say it is irrevocable (§ 7752(a)).

Funding: the step that gets skipped

"Funding" means putting assets into the trust. A trust controls only what it owns or what is payable to it. A beautifully drafted trust whose owner never signed a new deed or retitled an account leaves everything in probate.

AssetHow it is fundedWatch for
Home and other real estateNew deed to "[you], trustee of the [name] trust"Record it; tell the homeowner's insurer and any title insurer

Cars, belongingsOften left out; handled by a pour-over will or small-estate procedureSome states offer TOD vehicle titles

A pour-over will catches anything left outside the trust and "pours" it in at death. It is a safety net, not a substitute for funding: property that reaches the trust through the will passes through probate first.

Going furtherJoint trusts for married couples

Couples can have one joint trust or two separate ones. Joint trusts are simpler to manage; separate trusts can make it easier to keep track of each spouse's property, which matters for bypass planning and for blended families. In a community property state such as Arizona, spouses' community property can be held in a joint revocable trust while keeping its community character, which preserves the double step-up in basis at the first death (INV-085). Pennsylvania's trust code has specific rules for revoking or amending a trust funded by more than one settlor (20 Pa.C.S. § 7752(b)).

Giving up control, on purpose

Irrevocable trusts, trust taxes and life insurance trusts

Irrevocable trust
What it is
A trust that the grantor generally cannot change or cancel after creating it. Transfers into it are usually completed gifts. See irrevocable trust.
Used for
Removing assets and their future growth from a taxable estate, holding life insurance outside the estate, protecting assets for beneficiaries, and some charitable and Medicaid planning.
What it means for you
You trade control and flexibility for a specific benefit. If the benefit is not real in your situation — for example, you are far below the $15,000,000 federal exclusion for 2026 — the trade may not be worth it.

How trusts are taxed on income

A revocable trust is ignored for income tax: you report its income on your own return. An irrevocable trust is often a separate taxpayer that files Form 1041 and pays tax on income it keeps, at rates that climb very fast. For 2026 a trust reaches the 37% top rate on taxable income above $16,000; a single person does not reach 37% until above $640,600 (IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32). Income distributed to beneficiaries is generally taxed to them instead, at their own rates.

Some irrevocable trusts are deliberately drafted as grantor trusts: the grantor keeps a power that makes them the owner for income tax, so the grantor pays the trust's income tax personally, while the assets stay outside the estate for estate tax. Paying the tax is, in effect, an extra tax-free gift to the beneficiaries. But there is a catch, covered in the case studies: in 2023 the IRS ruled that assets in such a trust do not get a step-up in basis at the grantor's death if they are not included in the gross estate (Rev. Rul. 2023-2).

The irrevocable life insurance trust (ILIT)

Life insurance proceeds are included in your gross estate if they are payable to your executor or if you held any "incidents of ownership" in the policy, such as the right to change the beneficiary (26 U.S.C. § 2042). An ILIT owns the policy and is its beneficiary, so the proceeds are outside your estate. Two rules matter: you cannot keep control, and if you transfer an existing policy to the trust and die within three years, the proceeds are pulled back into your estate (26 U.S.C. § 2035(a)). Having the trust buy a new policy from the start avoids the three-year problem.

Going furtherWho still needs an ILIT after 2026?

With a $15,000,000 basic exclusion per person for 2026, indexed for inflation from 2027, and portability for married couples (INV-088), very few families face federal estate tax. ILITs remain useful for people whose estates, including insurance, clearly exceed the exclusion; for residents of states with low estate-tax exemptions such as Massachusetts ($2,000,000) or Oregon ($1,000,000) in 2026 (INV-089); and for control, for example to keep a payout to young children managed by a trustee. For most of our households, naming a revocable or testamentary trust as the policy's beneficiary achieves the control goal more simply.

Practitioner depthDistributable net income

Trust income tax uses a concept called distributable net income (DNI) to decide how much income is taxed to the trust and how much to beneficiaries who receive distributions. Capital gains are usually kept in principal and taxed to the trust unless the document or state law allows them to be distributed. Trustees can use distributions to beneficiaries in lower brackets to reduce total tax, subject to the trust's terms. This is an area where an experienced tax preparer pays for themselves.

Protecting a beneficiary

Special needs trusts and spendthrift provisions

Some trusts exist to protect the beneficiary from something: losing means-tested public benefits, creditors, a divorce, or their own poor decisions.

Special needs trustalso "supplemental needs trust"
What it is
A trust for a person with a disability, drafted so that the trust's assets do not disqualify them from means-tested programs such as Supplemental Security Income and Medicaid. See special needs trust.
Used for
Paying for things those programs do not cover, such as therapies, equipment, education, travel and quality-of-life expenses, without the beneficiary owning the money.
What it means for you
Leaving money outright to a disabled child or naming them directly on a beneficiary form can cost them their benefits. The trust must be drafted correctly; INV-102 covers special-needs planning in depth.

Federal Medicaid law recognizes specific trust types. A trust holding the assets of a disabled individual under age 65, established for that person by the individual, a parent, grandparent, legal guardian or a court, qualifies if the state will receive whatever remains at the person's death, up to the total Medicaid paid for them (42 U.S.C. § 1396p(d)(4)(A)). A pooled trust run by a non-profit association, with a separate account for each disabled beneficiary, is a second type (§ 1396p(d)(4)(C)). Those "payback" rules apply to trusts funded with the disabled person's own money, such as an injury settlement or an inheritance received outright; a trust that parents fund with their own assets is drafted differently, and an attorney who specializes in special-needs planning should draft either kind.

Spendthrift provision
What it is
A clause that stops a beneficiary from selling or pledging their interest in a trust and stops most of their creditors from reaching it before it is distributed.
Used for
Protecting heirs who have debts, addictions, unstable marriages or simply little experience with money.
What it means for you
It protects a beneficiary's inheritance, not your own money. You generally cannot put your own assets in a trust for yourself and shelter them from your own creditors.

Pennsylvania's version of the Uniform Trust Code is typical. A spendthrift provision is valid only if it restrains both voluntary and involuntary transfer, and saying the interest is held in a "spendthrift trust" is enough (20 Pa.C.S. § 7742). It does not stop a beneficiary's child who has a support order from reaching the beneficiary's interest (§ 7743(b)). And a creditor of the settlor of an irrevocable trust can reach the maximum amount that could be distributed for the settlor's benefit (§ 7745(2)).

Going furtherDistribution standards

Trusts tell the trustee when to distribute. Common standards are "mandatory income" (all income paid out), "health, education, maintenance and support" (a standard with tax advantages when the trustee is also a beneficiary), and fully "discretionary" (the trustee decides). Staged distributions — one-third at 25, half the rest at 30, the balance at 35 — are common for children. The more discretion, the more protection, and the more the trustee's judgment matters.

Trusts at the first death

Credit shelter and QTIP trusts

A married couple can leave everything to each other free of federal estate tax, thanks to the unlimited marital deduction for property passing to a US-citizen spouse (26 U.S.C. § 2056(a); for a non-citizen spouse, a qualified domestic trust is needed, § 2056(d)). Two kinds of trust shape what happens after that.

Credit shelter trustalso "bypass trust," "B trust" or "family trust"
What it is
A trust funded at the first death with up to the first spouse's exclusion amount, for the benefit of the surviving spouse (and often children), designed so its assets are not taxed again at the survivor's death.
Used for
Using the first spouse's exclusion and sheltering all later growth from estate tax; protecting assets for children of a first marriage; creditor protection for the survivor.
What it means for you
Since 2011, portability lets a surviving spouse use the first spouse's unused exclusion without a trust. The trust still shelters growth; portability keeps a fresh cost basis. The model below weighs the two.
QTIP trustqualified terminable interest property
What it is
A trust that pays the surviving spouse all its income at least annually for life, where no one can direct the property to anyone else during the spouse's life (26 U.S.C. § 2056(b)(7)); the executor elects to treat it as qualifying for the marital deduction.
Used for
Supporting a surviving spouse while guaranteeing the remainder goes to the first spouse's chosen heirs, typically children from an earlier marriage.
What it means for you
No estate tax at the first death, but the QTIP assets are taxed in the survivor's estate. It is a control tool as much as a tax tool.

With the model's defaults — $30 million at the first death in 2026, split evenly, 5% growth, 15 years between deaths, a 2.5% inflation adjustment and a 23.8% tax on gains — portability alone leaves an estate tax of about $10.26 million at the second death. A $15 million bypass trust cuts the estate tax to about $3.78 million, but its assets, grown to about $31.18 million, get no new basis, costing heirs about $3.85 million in capital gains tax if they sell. The bypass trust comes out about $2.62 million ahead. Lower the growth rate or the assets and portability wins: the trust's advantage exists only when the estate is well above the exclusion and grows faster than the exclusion's inflation adjustment.

Practitioner depthWhy portability is not a complete substitute

The DSUE amount is figured once, on the first spouse's timely filed Form 706, as the lesser of the basic exclusion then in effect and the unused exclusion; it is not indexed afterward (IRS, Instructions for Form 706). It can be lost if the survivor remarries and the new spouse dies first, because only the "last deceased spouse's" DSUE can be used. The GST exemption is not portable. State estate taxes usually have no portability. Each of these can favor a trust for larger estates or for residents of states with their own estate tax.

Choosing people, choosing tools

Trustees, and when a trust is and is not worth it

A trust lasts only as well as its trustee. The trustee invests, keeps records, files tax returns, communicates with beneficiaries and exercises judgment about distributions, sometimes for decades.

Trustee
What it is
The person or institution that holds and manages trust property for the beneficiaries. A trustee is a fiduciary, bound by duties of loyalty, prudence and impartiality.
Used for
Running the trust: investing, distributing, reporting, filing Form 1041.
What it means for you
Pennsylvania requires a trustee to respond promptly to reasonable requests for information from the settlor of a trust or a beneficiary of an irrevocable trust (20 Pa.C.S. § 7780.3). Choose someone who will keep good records and communicate.

A trust is often worth it when…

  • Heirs are minors, have a disability, or should not receive money outright.
  • You own real estate in more than one state (avoiding ancillary probate).
  • You live in a state with costly probate, or value privacy.
  • You want a smooth handoff if you develop dementia, especially if you live alone.
  • You have a blended family and want to provide for a spouse while protecting children.
  • Your estate is near or above the federal or your state's exemption.

A trust is often not worth it when…

  • Almost everything already passes by beneficiary designation or joint title.
  • Your heirs are responsible adults and your state's probate is inexpensive.
  • You will not do the work of funding it.
  • You are buying it to save taxes that a revocable trust does not save.
Going furtherTestamentary trusts

A trust does not have to exist during your life. A testamentary trust is created by your will and comes into being after probate. It costs less to set up, and it is the usual way young parents like the Riveras hold money for children: the wills say that anything passing to a minor child goes to a trust with a named trustee. Beneficiary forms can name the testamentary trust. It does not avoid probate for the will's assets, and it offers no incapacity planning.

Real rulings and statutes

Where trusts help, and where they surprise

Case study 1Revenue Ruling 2023-2: no step-up for a trust outside the estate
What happened
The IRS considered an individual who created an irrevocable trust, funded it with an asset as a completed gift, and kept a power that made them the owner for income tax (a grantor trust), but whose trust assets were not includible in their gross estate. It ruled that the trust's assets receive no basis adjustment under § 1014 at the grantor's death.
What it teaches
Removing assets from the estate and keeping the step-up are usually alternatives, not a package. For families below the exclusion, keeping appreciated assets in the estate (outright or in a revocable trust) is often the better tax result (INV-090).
Source
Internal Revenue Service. (2023). Revenue Ruling 2023-2.
Case study 2The federal statute behind first-party special needs trusts
The law
42 U.S.C. § 1396p(d)(4)(A) recognizes a trust containing the assets of a disabled individual under 65, established for them by the individual, a parent, grandparent, guardian or court, if the state receives what remains at death up to the Medicaid it paid.
What it teaches
The difference between a young adult with a disability keeping or losing Medicaid after an inheritance can come down to whether a relative named the right trust on a form. Family members who might leave money to a disabled relative should coordinate with the family's plan.
Source
42 U.S.C. § 1396p(d)(4)(A) and (C), via Cornell Legal Information Institute.

The result
At the second death, the house and the account go through probate so the will can pour them into the trust — the exact outcome the trust was bought to avoid.

Decisions

Which trust, if any?

Scenario 1 · The Riveras

A trust for Sofia and Leo is the plan. At what age should the children receive the money outright?

At 18That is what happens without a trust. An 18-year-old receiving a life-insurance payout outright has no protection from pressure, poor choices or a bad relationship.
Staged, with a trusteeA common and sensible choice: the trustee pays for school, health and a first home, and the children gain control gradually. The exact ages are a family choice, not a rule.
The principle: a trust lets you decide when "adult" means ready.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers

A seminar suggests the Harpers set up bypass trusts in their wills "to save estate tax." Their estate is about $1.7 million, and North Carolina has no estate tax. What would you do?

Bypass trustsThey would save no federal estate tax at an estate far below $15,000,000, and assets in the bypass trust would lose the step-up at the second death.
Simplicity plus step-upRight for most couples at their level. A trust may still be useful for control, for example if they want Ethan's inheritance managed until he is older.
The principle: do not buy a solution to a tax you will not owe.
Scenario 3 · Monica

Monica, 58, wants her $290,000 401(k), $40,000 Roth IRA and life insurance to take care of Eli, 13, who has special needs and will likely need support for life. Options: name Eli directly; leave everything to a trusted relative who promises to use it for Eli; or name a third-party supplemental needs trust.

DirectlyA minor cannot manage it, and once the money is Eli's own it can make him ineligible for Supplemental Security Income and Medicaid until it is spent down, or force it into a first-party trust that must repay Medicaid at his death (42 U.S.C. § 1396p(d)(4)(A)).
A trusted relativeIt keeps the money out of Eli's name, but the relative owns it outright: it is exposed to their creditors and divorce, passes under their own will, and nothing obliges them to spend it on Eli.
A supplemental needs trustRight. A trust funded with Monica's own money needs no Medicaid payback, and when Eli cannot revoke it or direct its use, its principal is not his resource for SSI (SSA POMS SI 01120.200). The trustee pays for things directly rather than handing Eli cash, which SSI counts as income. INV-091 covers how the trust inherits the retirement accounts.
The principle: when an heir depends on means-tested benefits, the trust is not optional; the beneficiary forms must name it.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A non-grantor trust keeps $20,000 of taxable ordinary income in 2026. Using the 2026 trust schedule (37% above $16,000, with $3,851 of tax on the first $16,000), what is its federal income tax, in dollars?

$3,851 + 0.37 × ($20,000 − $16,000) = $3,851 + $1,480 = $5,331.
Exercise 2

A widow's taxable estate is already above the federal exclusion. A $5,000,000 life insurance policy is owned by an ILIT instead of by her. About how much federal estate tax does that avoid, in dollars?

Above the exclusion, each dollar is taxed at 40%: 0.40 × $5,000,000 = $2,000,000. If her estate were below the exclusion even with the policy, the ILIT would save no federal tax.
Exercise 3

Stock bought for $1,000,000 is worth $3,000,000 at the owner's death. It sits in an irrevocable grantor trust that is not included in the estate, so there is no step-up. If the heirs sell it immediately and pay 23.8% on the gain, how much tax do they pay, in dollars?

Gain = $3,000,000 − $1,000,000 = $2,000,000; × 0.238 = $476,000. Had it been in the estate, the basis would have stepped up to $3,000,000 and the gain would be zero (Rev. Rul. 2023-2).
Exercise 4

If you transfer an existing life insurance policy to an ILIT, within how many years of the transfer would your death pull the proceeds back into your estate?

3 years, under 26 U.S.C. § 2035(a). A policy the trust buys new is not subject to this rule.
