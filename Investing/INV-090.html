Step-Up in Basis and Inherited Assets

When someone dies, the tax cost of most of what they owned is reset to its value on the date of death. Decades of untaxed gains simply disappear. A gift made during life gets no such reset. This one rule shapes when families should sell, give or hold, and it treats a stock, a house and an IRA completely differently.

The question this module answersShould appreciated assets be sold, given away now, or held until death — and what will my heirs owe when they inherit?

Start here

The same shares, three very different tax bills

Most people know that selling an investment for more than you paid triggers a tax on the profit. Fewer know that if you hold that investment until you die, your heirs can usually sell it the next week and owe nothing on the decades of growth.

The rule is written plainly into section 1014 of the Internal Revenue Code, and it has limits. A gift made while you are alive does not get it. Retirement accounts do not get it. And for married couples, how the asset was titled decides whether half or all of it resets.

Consider the Harpers, who hold $220,000 of technology stocks in a taxable brokerage account. Suppose, for illustration, that they paid about $70,000 for them over the years. Figure 90.1 compares three things they could do with the shares.

This module explains each of those numbers. It builds on INV-035 (cost basis) and INV-088 (the federal estate and gift tax).

The core rule

What resets when someone dies

Every investment you own has a cost basis: roughly, what you paid for it, adjusted for things like reinvested dividends and, for property, improvements and depreciation. When you sell, your taxable gain is the sale price minus the basis. Basis is therefore the part of your money that has already been taxed.

Step-up in basisIRC section 1014
What it is
For most property acquired from someone who died, the heir's basis is the property's fair market value on the date of death (or on the alternate valuation date, if the executor elects it). If the asset rose in value, the basis steps up; if it fell, it steps down.
Used for
Measuring the heir's gain or loss when they later sell. Only growth after the date of death is taxable to the heir.
What it means for you
An appreciated stock, fund or piece of real estate that you hold until death passes to your heirs with its lifetime gain wiped clean. A loser passes with its loss wiped clean too, so losses are usually worth harvesting while you are alive (INV-037).

Three details that matter in practice

  • Every inherited gain is long-term. IRS Publication 559 states that property acquired from a decedent is treated as held for more than one year, however long anyone actually held it. An heir who sells a month later still gets the lower long-term capital gain rates: 0%, 15% or 20% for 2026, with the 0% rate applying to taxable income up to $49,450 for single filers and $98,900 for married couples filing jointly (Rev. Proc. 2025-32).
  • Value is fair market value. For listed stocks and funds, that is the price on the date of death, which brokers usually record for you. For a house, a business or art, it means an appraisal. Keep it: it is the heir's basis for as long as they own the asset.
  • Estate tax is a separate question. The step-up applies whether or not any estate tax is owed. For 2026 the federal basic exclusion is $15,000,000 per person, so the great majority of estates owe no federal estate tax and still get the full step-up.
Going furtherThe step-down: why losers should be sold first

Section 1014 sets the heir's basis at fair market value whether that is higher or lower than what the owner paid. A stock bought for $50,000 and worth $20,000 at death gives the heir a $20,000 basis, and the $30,000 loss is gone for everyone. Sold during life, that loss could have offset gains and up to $3,000 a year of other income (IRC 1211(b)), with the rest carried forward.

Practitioner depthConsistency reporting and the one-year boomerang rule

Basis consistency. When an estate must file Form 706, the executor generally sends each beneficiary a Schedule A of Form 8971 reporting the estate-tax value of what they received, and under IRC 1014(f) the heir's basis cannot exceed that value. Heirs should not quietly use a higher number later.

The one-year rule. IRC 1014(e) blocks a tempting trick: give appreciated property to an elderly relative, let them die, and inherit it back with a fresh basis. If the decedent received the appreciated property as a gift within one year of death and it passes back to the donor or the donor's spouse, the basis is the decedent's old basis, not the date-of-death value.

Income in respect of a decedent. IRC 1014(c) excludes any right to receive income the decedent had earned but not yet been taxed on: traditional IRA and 401(k) balances, unpaid wages, and the untaxed interest on U.S. savings bonds, among others. Those carry their income tax with them (see the Exceptions tab).

Carryover basis

A gift keeps the giver's basis

If the Harpers give Ethan their shares today, Ethan does not get a fresh start. He steps into their shoes, and the $150,000 of built-in gain comes with the gift.

Carryover basisIRC section 1015
What it is
The rule that a person who receives property as a gift takes the donor's adjusted basis. The donor's holding period carries over too (IRC 1223(2)).
Used for
Measuring the recipient's gain when they sell a gifted asset.
What it means for you
Giving appreciated shares moves the future tax bill to the recipient; it does not erase it. It helps only if the recipient's tax rate is lower than yours — and for students and children the kiddie tax often closes that gap.

The loss exception. If the gift is worth less than the donor's basis on the day it is given, the recipient uses two bases. For measuring a gain, they use the donor's basis; for measuring a loss, they use the value on the gift date. IRS Publication 551 gives an example: land with a $10,000 basis given when worth $8,000. Sold for $12,000, the gain is $2,000. Sold for $7,000, the loss is $1,000. Sold for anything from $8,000 to $10,000, there is neither gain nor loss. Under this carryover basis rule, the practical lesson is that a donor should sell a losing investment, take the loss, and give cash.

With the calculator's defaults, a $100,000 holding with a $20,000 basis grows 6% a year for 15 years to about $239,700. Given away today, the heir who sells then owes about $32,900 at a 15% rate; inherited, nothing. Switch the estate toggle to "Yes" to see the trade-off that only very large estates face: holding exposes the growth to a 40% estate tax, while a gift freezes the taxable value at today's price but gives up the step-up.

Going furtherThe kiddie tax closes the family loophole

Parents once shifted gains to children in low brackets. The kiddie tax now taxes a child's unearned income above a threshold ($2,700 in the IRS's current guidance) at the parents' rates. It applies to children under 18, to 18-year-olds whose earned income is not more than half their support, and to full-time students aged 19 through 23 in the same position (IRS Topic 553). Ethan, 20 and in college, is squarely inside it. Once a child is past those ages and has little income, gifting shares they will sell can use the 0% long-term rate — but the gain must fit under $49,450 of taxable income for a single filer in 2026, including the gain itself.

Titling decides

Half a step-up, or a double step-up

When one spouse dies, what happens to the survivor's basis depends on who legally owned the asset, not who paid for it. There are four common cases.

  1. Owned only by the spouse who died: the whole asset steps up when the survivor inherits it.
  2. Owned only by the surviving spouse: nothing was inherited, so nothing steps up.
  3. Owned jointly by the spouses as joint tenants with right of survivorship or as tenants by the entirety: the law treats each spouse as owning half, so half the value is included in the estate and only that half steps up. IRS Publication 551 calls this a "qualified joint interest."
  4. Community property: in the nine community-property states, if at least half of the community property is included in the deceased spouse's estate, the whole property, including the survivor's half, takes a new basis (IRC 1014(b)(6)). This is the "double step-up" of community property.
Community property9 states
What it is
A system of marital property law under which most property acquired during the marriage belongs equally to both spouses. The nine states are Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington and Wisconsin (IRS Publication 555).
Used for
Dividing property at divorce and death, and, for tax, determining the basis of both halves at the first death.
What it means for you
A couple in a community-property state can often sell appreciated assets after the first death with no capital gains tax at all. Couples elsewhere keep the survivor's original basis on their half.

At the defaults — the Harpers' $220,000 of shares with a $70,000 basis — a joint account gives the survivor a basis of $35,000 + $110,000 = $145,000, and a sale owes $14,100 at 18.8%. The same account held as community property would have a $220,000 basis and owe nothing. The Harpers live in North Carolina, which is not a community-property state; the Riveras live in Arizona, which is.

Practitioner depthMoving, separate property and elective regimes

Not everything a couple in a community-property state owns is community property. IRS Publication 555 lists what is generally separate property: property either spouse owned before the marriage; money earned while domiciled in a non-community-property state; gifts and inheritances received by one spouse; property bought with separate funds; and property converted to separate property by a valid agreement. Separate property of the spouse who dies steps up; separate property of the survivor does not. Publication 555 also notes that Alaska, Tennessee and South Dakota let couples elect community-property treatment, and that it does not address the federal tax treatment of those elections. Retitling assets to chase the step-up needs a lawyer in your state, because it also changes who owns what at divorce.

Real estate

The house: basis, the home-sale exclusion and heirs who sell

A family home is often the most appreciated asset a household owns, and it gets the same step-up as a stock. Three situations come up again and again.

1. The surviving spouse

The survivor's basis follows the titling rules on the previous tab. On top of that, a surviving spouse can use the home sale exclusion of up to $500,000 of gain, instead of the single filer's $250,000, if the home is sold within two years of the spouse's death, the survivor has not remarried, and the couple would have qualified just before the death (IRS Publication 523). The survivor may also count the late spouse's years of ownership and use toward the two-out-of-five-year test.

2. Children who inherit and sell

Heirs take the home at its date-of-death value. If they sell it promptly for about that price, there is little or no gain; selling costs may even produce a small loss. The home-sale exclusion does not help them unless they live there themselves long enough to qualify, but with a fresh basis they rarely need it.

3. Children who keep it and rent it

If heirs rent out an inherited house, depreciation is based on the building's stepped-up basis (or its value when first rented, if lower; IRS Publication 551), not on the land. When they sell, gain above that basis, plus depreciation taken, is taxable (INV-068).

⚠️

Giving a house away to "protect" it has other costs. Besides losing the step-up, a lifetime gift of a home can affect eligibility for Medicaid long-term care coverage and leaves the parent without legal ownership of where they live. INV-089 (state taxes), INV-097 (caring for aging parents) and an elder-law attorney are the right next steps before any such transfer.

Going furtherGetting the date-of-death value right

For a home, the executor or heirs should obtain a written appraisal as of the date of death from a licensed appraiser, even when no estate tax return is due. IRS Publication 551 notes that when no Form 706 is required, an appraised value for state inheritance or transmission tax purposes can establish the basis. A realtor's opinion or an online estimate is weaker evidence if the IRS later asks. Pennsylvania, where Ruth lives, has its own inheritance tax, 4.5% on transfers to children and other lineal heirs according to the state Department of Revenue (INV-089), which makes a formal valuation part of settling the estate anyway.

Six months, and what never steps up

The alternate valuation date and the assets left out

Alternate valuation dateIRC section 2032
What it is
An executor's election to value the entire estate six months after death instead of on the date of death. Anything sold or distributed within those six months is valued on the date it left the estate.
Used for
Reducing estate tax when values fall soon after a death, as in a market crash.
What it means for you
It is allowed only if it lowers both the gross estate and the estate (and generation-skipping) tax, so it matters only for estates that owe estate tax. It also lowers every heir's basis.

The election is all-or-nothing: it cannot be applied to only some assets, it is made on a Form 706 filed no more than a year late, and once made it is irrevocable (IRC 2032(d); Form 706 instructions). Because the lower value becomes the heirs' basis, it trades a 40% estate tax saving for a smaller future capital gains cost.

Practitioner depthThe deduction heirs often miss

When an estate did pay federal estate tax and an heir later reports income in respect of a decedent, such as IRA withdrawals, the heir may deduct the estate tax attributable to that income, in the same year the income is reported (the "estate tax deduction" in Publication 559, under IRC 691(c)). Individuals can claim it only as an itemized deduction on Schedule A, and it is easy to overlook years later. It exists only for taxable estates.

Real law, real numbers

Two stories about basis at death

Case study 12010: the year the step-up was optional
What happened
For people who died in 2010, the law let executors make a "Section 1022 election": no estate tax at all, but heirs lost the ordinary step-up. Under the election, an heir's basis was generally the lesser of the decedent's adjusted basis or the value at death, and the executor could allocate up to $1.3 million of extra basis to chosen assets, plus up to $3 million more to property passing to a surviving spouse, reported on Form 8939. Without the election, the estate tax and the normal step-up applied.
What it teaches
Executors had to weigh an estate tax against capital gains their heirs would owe later. Only estates large enough to owe estate tax had reason to give up the step-up; for everyone else it was the more valuable rule. It also shows that the step-up exists only as long as Congress keeps it.
Source
Internal Revenue Service (2011). Instructions for Form 8939: Allocation of increase in basis for property acquired from a decedent; Notice 2011-66; Rev. Proc. 2011-41.
Case study 2The Harpers' tech stocks over 25 years (illustration)
The setup
$220,000 of shares with an assumed $70,000 basis, held jointly in North Carolina and growing 6% a year for 25 years, to about $944,200.
The result
Sold during their lives, the $874,200 gain would owe about $164,400 at 18.8%. If Tom dies first, Rachel's basis becomes her half of the old basis plus half the value at his death; at her death, Ethan's basis resets to the full value and a prompt sale owes almost nothing.
What it teaches
The step-up is a reason not to sell for no reason, but it is not a reason to stay concentrated. Twenty-five technology stocks is a sector bet (INV-004). Selling the highest-basis lots, giving low-basis shares to charity, and holding the lowest-basis lots are ways to diversify without paying the full bill.

Decisions

Sell, give or hold?

Scenario 1 · The Harpers

Tom and Rachel, 57 and 55, want to cut their 25-stock technology portfolio in half. A friend says, "Never sell appreciated stock — your son gets the step-up." What should they do?

Hold everythingThey may live 30 or more years. The step-up is worth nothing if a sector collapse halves the account first. A tax bill of up to 18.8% of the gain is a known cost; concentration is an open-ended one.
Diversify with tax careRight. Lot selection (INV-035), tax-loss harvesting (INV-037) and charitable gifts of low-basis shares (INV-039) can cut the concentration substantially while paying tax only on the smallest gains.
Give to EthanAt 20 and in college, Ethan is subject to the kiddie tax, so most of the gain is taxed at his parents' rates anyway (Figure 90.1). And the shares become his, whatever he decides to do with them.
The principle: the step-up is a reason not to sell needlessly, never a reason to keep a risk you would not otherwise take.
Scenario 2 · Ruth and her house

Ruth, 68, is thinking of deeding her $380,000 house to Anna now "so it's settled." Anna has her own home and would sell Ruth's house eventually. What is the tax effect?

Saves taxProbate is a process cost (INV-086), not a tax. In the illustration of Figure 90.5, a gift would cost Anna about $22,900 of federal tax on a sale at today's price, and more as the house appreciates.
Likely costs taxRight. A revocable living trust (INV-087), or a transfer-on-death deed in states that allow one (INV-085), can avoid probate while Ruth keeps ownership until death, so the step-up survives. She should see an estate or elder-law attorney before transferring her home.
The principle: avoid probate with tools that keep ownership until death; giving away appreciated property during life gives away its step-up.

What the step-up means for each household

Nothing to plan yet, one habit to start. Denise's $3,000 is cash. When she starts a taxable account, keeping records of what she paid is the first basis habit, and her 401(k) beneficiary form is the first estate document (INV-085).
Arizona is a community-property state. Their home, and any taxable investments bought with their wages, can get a full step-up at the first death. Their 401(k)s will not step up at all; beneficiary forms control those.
The house and the index funds step up; the 401(k) never will. Maya's $310,000 house and her $40,000 taxable index funds would get a new basis at her death, so Eli's trust could sell either with little gain. Her $290,000 pre-tax 401(k) carries its income tax to the trust instead (INV-091), which makes it the account most worth planning around.
The step-up versus concentration. Their $220,000 of tech stocks may carry a large gain. Diversify with lot selection, losses and charitable gifts rather than holding everything for Ethan's step-up. Their $820,000 pre-tax 401(k)s will never step up.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

Your father bought shares for $30,000. They were worth $90,000 when he died, and you sell them for $92,000. What is your taxable gain, in dollars?

Your basis is the $90,000 value at death, so the gain is $92,000 − $90,000 = $2,000, taxed at long-term rates however soon you sell.
Exercise 2

Same shares, but your father gave them to you while alive, when they were worth $90,000. You sell for $92,000. What is your taxable gain?

A gift carries the donor's $30,000 basis: $92,000 − $30,000 = $62,000.
Exercise 3

A married couple in a non-community-property state paid $200,000 for a home they own as joint tenants. It is worth $500,000 when one spouse dies. What is the survivor's new basis?

The survivor keeps half the original basis, $100,000, and the inherited half steps up to half of $500,000, $250,000: $350,000. In a community-property state it would be $500,000.
Exercise 4

An estate is worth $17.0 million at death and $16.2 million six months later. With a $15 million exemption and a 40% rate, how much estate tax does the alternate valuation date save?

Tax at death: 40% × $2.0M = $800,000. At six months: 40% × $1.2M = $480,000. Saving: $320,000, at the cost of $800,000 less basis for the heirs.
