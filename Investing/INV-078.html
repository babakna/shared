Social Security

For most retirees, Social Security is the largest and most valuable asset they own: an inflation-adjusted income for life, with benefits for spouses and survivors, that no private product can match at the price. This module explains how the benefit is calculated, what claiming at 62, 67 or 70 really costs and buys, and how the 2025 Fairness Act and the 2026 Trustees Report affect your plan.

The question this module answersHow is my benefit figured, and when should I (and my spouse) claim it?

Start here

The most valuable retirement asset most people own

You paid for Social Security with every paycheck, so it is natural to want it back as soon as possible. Many people claim at 62 for exactly that reason, and for some it is the right choice.

But the question "when do I get my money back?" treats Social Security like an account, and it is not one. It is insurance: a monthly income that rises with inflation every year, lasts for the rest of your life, and continues for a surviving spouse. Every month you wait between 62 and 70 buys a permanently larger check, through delayed retirement credits after your full retirement age. Deciding when to claim is really deciding how much longevity and inflation insurance to buy. Figure 78.1 traces how the check is built.

The formula

Credits, AIME and the primary insurance amount

Eligibility. You earn Social Security "credits" (legally, quarters of coverage) by working and paying Social Security tax. In 2026, you earn one credit for each $1,890 of earnings, up to four a year. Anyone born in 1929 or later needs 40 credits, about ten years of work, to qualify for a retirement benefit. Earnings above the taxable maximum ($184,500 in 2026) are neither taxed nor counted.

Average indexed monthly earningsAIME
What it is
Your average monthly earnings over your 35 highest-earning years, after each earlier year has been scaled up by the growth in national average wages. Years with no earnings count as zeros if you have fewer than 35.
Used for
The input to the benefit formula. Indexing means a $20,000 salary in 1990 counts as the equivalent share of the average wage today.
What it means for you
Working a 36th year at a higher real salary replaces a low year in the average. Stopping work at 55 with only 30 years adds five zeros.

The Social Security Administration (SSA) indexes each year's earnings to the national average wage index for the year you turn 60. For someone first eligible (turning 62) in 2026, that is the 2024 index of 69,846.57; earnings in and after 2024 count at face value. The indexed earnings for the 35 best years are added up, divided by 420 months, and rounded down to a whole dollar.

Primary insurance amountPIA
What it is
The monthly benefit you would receive if you claimed exactly at your full retirement age, before any cost-of-living adjustments that apply after you turn 62.
Used for
The base for your own benefit at every claiming age, and for your spouse's, survivors' and children's benefits.
What it means for you
The statement in your my Social Security account at ssa.gov estimates it. Every claiming decision is a percentage of it.
2026 PIA = 90% × first $1,286 of AIME + 32% × AIME from $1,286 to $7,749 + 15% × AIME above $7,749 (rounded down to the dime)

The two dollar amounts in the formula are called bend points. They rise each year with average wages; for 2026 SSA derived them from the 1979 values of $180 and $1,085, times 69,846.57 ÷ 9,779.44. They apply to people who turn 62 in 2026; your bend points are fixed by the year you turn 62. SSA's own example: a person with maximum-taxable earnings every year since 22 who turns 62 in 2026 has an AIME of $14,358 and a PIA of $4,216.90.

Cost-of-living adjustments. After you turn 62, your PIA and every benefit based on it rise each year with inflation, as measured by the Consumer Price Index. SSA announced a 2.8% adjustment on October 24, 2025, and will announce the next in October 2026. This inflation protection, for life, is the feature that is most expensive to buy privately.

Practitioner depthWhy the formula is progressive

For an AIME of $3,000, the 2026 formula gives a PIA of $1,705.80, 57% of AIME. For $6,000 it gives $2,665.80 (44%); for $14,358, $4,216.90 (29%). Those declining percentages are the same pattern as SSA's published replacement rates (78%, 42% and 28% for very low, medium and high earners at 67; INV-075). It also explains why lower earners gain relatively more from Social Security and why the benefit of an extra working year shrinks at higher incomes: extra AIME above $7,749 adds only 15 cents of PIA per dollar.

The biggest decision

What claiming early costs, and what waiting buys

Full retirement ageFRA; also called normal retirement age
What it is
The age at which your benefit equals your PIA. It is 66 for people born 1943–1954, rises by two months per birth year for 1955–1959 (66 and 2 months up to 66 and 10 months), and is 67 for everyone born in 1960 or later. People born on January 1 use the previous year.
Used for
The reference point for reductions and delayed retirement credits.
What it means for you
Five of the six households have members born in 1960 or later and therefore an FRA of 67; Ruth, born in 1958, had 66 and 8 months.

Claiming early. You can start at 62. For each month before FRA, the benefit is reduced by 5/9 of 1% for the first 36 months and 5/12 of 1% for each month beyond that. With an FRA of 67, claiming at 62 (60 months early) reduces the benefit by 30%, to 70% of PIA, permanently.

Delaying. For each month you wait past FRA, the benefit rises by 2/3 of 1%, which is 8% a year for anyone born in 1943 or later. The increase stops at 70; there is no reason to wait longer. Waiting from 67 to 70 raises the benefit to 124% of PIA, and that is 77% more than the benefit at 62.

Break-even age
What it is
The age at which the total received from claiming later catches up with the total from claiming earlier, ignoring interest, taxes and inflation adjustments.
Used for
A rough sense of how long you must live for waiting to pay off.
What it means for you
For an FRA of 67, waiting from 62 to 70 breaks even at about 80.4; from 62 to 67 at about 78.7; from 67 to 70 at 82.5. A 62-year-old man has about a 60% chance of reaching 80.4 in the SSA table, a woman about 71%.
Break-even age A: Bearly × (A − aearly) = Blate × (A − alate)  ⇒  0.70(A − 62) = 1.24(A − 70)  ⇒  A = 80.4

The defaults are the Harpers: Tom, born 1969, with an illustrative PIA of $3,800, and Rachel, born 1971, with her own PIA of $2,500. If Tom claims at 67, he receives $3,800 a month in today's dollars; at 62, $2,660; at 70, $4,712. The calculator follows the same rules as Babak's Social Security Family Planner, which adds child benefits, the family maximum, taxes, portfolio effects and Monte Carlo sensitivity for a full household plan.

Going furtherBreak-even is the wrong question for most couples

Break-even compares totals, but the real risk in retirement is not dying early with money unspent; it is living long with too little. Delaying is a purchase of insurance against a long life, and like any insurance it "loses" if the bad event (here, a long life) never happens. For a married higher earner the case for waiting is stronger still, because the larger check continues for whichever spouse lives longer (next tab). The case for claiming early is strongest for single people in poor health, and for anyone who genuinely needs the income and has no other source.

Practitioner depthTiming details that change the numbers

You must be 62 for the entire month to receive a benefit for it. If you start benefits before 70, some delayed credits earned in that calendar year are not applied until the following January. If you have already reached FRA, you can ask for up to six months of retroactive benefits, but not for any month before FRA. And regardless of claiming age, SSA advises contacting it about three months before your 65th birthday to sign up for Medicare, to avoid higher Part B and Part D premiums (INV-079).

Family benefits

Spousal, survivor, divorced-spouse and children's benefits

Spousal benefit
What it is
A benefit of up to 50% of your spouse's PIA, available from 62 once your spouse has filed for their own benefit (see spousal benefit in the glossary). If you also qualify on your own record, SSA pays your own benefit first and adds the difference.
Used for
Supporting a spouse with low or no earnings. SSA's example: a person with a $1,250 own benefit and a $1,400 spousal benefit at FRA gets $1,250 plus a $150 top-up.
What it means for you
Claimed before FRA, it is reduced: with FRA 67, a spouse gets 32.5% of the worker's PIA at 62. It does not grow past FRA, so a spouse gains nothing by waiting beyond FRA to claim a spousal benefit.

Deemed filing. If you were born on or after January 2, 1954, and are eligible for both your own and a spouse's benefit, applying for one is treated as applying for both. You cannot take a spousal benefit first and let your own grow.

Survivor benefitwidow(er)'s benefit
What it is
A monthly benefit for a surviving spouse based on the deceased worker's record: 100% of the worker's benefit at the survivor's full retirement age, or as early as 60 at a reduction (71.5% at 60).
Used for
Replacing the larger of two checks when the first spouse dies. The survivor keeps the higher of the two benefits, not both.
What it means for you
For a married higher earner, the claiming age sets not just your own check but the one your spouse may live on for decades.
  • Survivor full retirement age is set separately in the Code of Federal Regulations (20 CFR 404.409(b)): 66 for survivors born 1945–1956, rising two months per year to 67 for those born 1962 or later. Ruth, born 1958, reached it at 66 and 4 months.
  • If the worker claimed early, the survivor benefit is limited to the larger of what the worker was receiving or 82.5% of the worker's PIA (SSA's Program Operations Manual System, POMS RS 00615.320). If the worker claimed at or after FRA, the survivor gets the worker's full amount, including delayed credits.
  • Switching. A survivor who is also eligible on their own record can take one benefit and switch later; SSA's example is starting survivor benefits and changing to your own retirement benefit at 70 if it is higher then. Deemed filing does not apply to survivor benefits.
  • Remarriage before 60 usually ends survivor eligibility; remarriage after 60 does not.
  • Children of a deceased worker generally get 75% of the worker's benefit, subject to a family maximum of 150–180% of the worker's benefit. A one-time $255 death payment may go to a spouse or minor child.

Divorced spouses. If a marriage lasted at least 10 years, an ex-spouse who is 62 or older and unmarried can collect on the worker's record, and the benefit does not reduce what the worker or a current spouse gets. If the couple has been divorced for at least two continuous years and both are at least 62, the ex-spouse can collect even if the worker has not filed. A surviving divorced spouse can collect from 60 under the same 10-year rule.

Going furtherThe Harpers' survivor question

Assuming independent lifetimes, the SSA table gives Rachel (55) about a 64% chance of outliving Tom (57). If Tom claims at 62, the survivor benefit she would inherit is the larger of his $2,660 and 82.5% of his PIA, $3,135. If he claims at 70, it is $4,712: $1,577 a month more, in today's dollars and indexed to inflation, for however long she lives after him. That is why the usual advice is for the higher earner in a couple to delay, even when break-even arithmetic looks unexciting.

Children, childhood disability benefits and the family maximum

A worker's record can also pay the worker's children. Once a parent starts retirement benefits, an unmarried child can be paid on the parent's record if the child is under 18, is 18 or 19 and a full-time student in grade 12 or below (benefits usually continue until graduation or two months after the 19th birthday, whichever comes first), or is 18 or older with a disability that began before 22 (SSA, Benefits for Children, 2026).

Child's benefit
What it is
A monthly benefit for an eligible child of a worker who is receiving retirement or disability benefits, or who has died. By law it equals one-half of the parent's PIA while the parent is alive and three-fourths of the PIA after the parent's death (Social Security Act §202(d)(2)).
Used for
Supporting children while a parent is retired, disabled or gone. A child can be paid only once a living parent has claimed; after a parent's death, no claim by the parent is needed.
What it means for you
Because it is a share of the PIA, the parent's claiming age does not change it: claiming at 62 does not shrink it, and waiting to 70 does not raise it. The parent's claiming age only decides when it can start.
Childhood disability benefitCDB; SSA also says disabled adult child (DAC) benefit
What it is
A child's benefit paid past 18 to an adult whose disability began before 22, on the record of a parent who receives retirement or disability benefits or has died. SSA decides disability using the adult rules. The adult does not need to have worked.
Used for
Lifetime income for an adult disabled since childhood. It continues for as long as the disability lasts; marriage may end it. After two years of these benefits, the adult generally qualifies for Medicare.
What it means for you
For a parent of a child with a lifelong disability, the parent's own record can become the child's income for life: 50% of the PIA while the parent lives and 75% after.
Family maximum
What it is
The most that can be paid each month on one worker's record, between 150% and 180% of the worker's PIA.
Used for
When benefits for the family would exceed it, every family member's benefit is cut proportionally; the worker's own retirement benefit, and a divorced spouse's benefit, are never cut for it (SSA Handbook §731.1).
What it means for you
With one child it rarely binds. It matters for a worker with several children, or a spouse and children, all drawing on one record.
2026 family maximum = 150% × first $1,643 of PIA + 272% × PIA from $1,643 to $2,371 + 134% × PIA from $2,371 to $3,093 + 175% × PIA above $3,093 (rounded down to the dime)

These bend points apply to workers who turn 62, or die before 62, in 2026; like the PIA bend points, they rise each year with average wages. Family members share whatever the maximum leaves after the worker's PIA.

👥

A large age gap plus a disabled child changes the claiming question. A spouse can qualify at any age while caring for the worker's entitled child who is under 16 or disabled. The unreduced child-in-care spouse amount can be up to 50% of the worker's PIA, but the spouse and child's payments share the family maximum and may be reduced. The caregiver benefit can continue after the child turns 16 when the child remains entitled because of disability and the spouse exercises parental control and responsibility. Eligibility is fact-specific; SSA, not a calculator, makes the determination.

Practitioner depthHow Eli's benefit on Monica's record meets SSI and Medicaid

Eli is expected to qualify at 18 for Supplemental Security Income (SSI), the needs-based program, which in 2026 pays at most $994 a month and generally requires countable resources of $2,000 or less. SSI treats Social Security benefits as income: after the first $20 a month, each dollar reduces the SSI payment by a dollar. Once Monica claims, Eli's $1,150 childhood disability benefit leaves $1,130 of countable income, more than $994, so his SSI stops. His income rises only by about $156 a month ($1,150 against $994), but he does not lose Medicaid: section 1634(c) of the Social Security Act requires states to keep treating a childhood disability beneficiary who loses SSI this way as an SSI recipient for Medicaid, as long as he would otherwise still qualify for SSI (POMS SI 01715.015). That condition includes the resource limit, which is why money meant for Eli should go to a supplemental needs trust or an ABLE account, never to him directly (INV-084 onward). A child entitled on two parents' records is paid on only one (POMS RS 00615.770), so benefits on his father's record would not add to these.

Two rules that surprise people

The earnings test and income tax on benefits

Earnings testretirement earnings test
What it is
If you claim before full retirement age and keep working, SSA withholds benefits when your earnings exceed a yearly limit. In 2026 it withholds $1 for every $2 earned above $24,480; in the year you reach FRA, $1 for every $3 above $65,160, counting only earnings before the month you reach FRA.
Used for
Discouraging people from claiming early while still working full time.
What it means for you
Withheld benefits are not lost: at FRA your benefit is recalculated upward to credit the months withheld. From the month you reach FRA on, there is no limit.

SSA's 2026 example of the earnings test: someone who claims at 62 in January 2026 with a $600 monthly benefit and earns $26,080 is $1,600 over the limit, so SSA withholds $800, taking the January and February checks and paying $600 a month from March. A special monthly rule applies in the first year of retirement: you can get a full check for any month your earnings are below a monthly limit, whatever your yearly total.

Income tax on benefits

Up to 85% of Social Security benefits can be subject to federal income tax, depending on your "combined income": adjusted gross income, plus tax-exempt interest, plus half of your benefits. SSA estimates that about 40% of people who get Social Security pay income tax on it. The combined income test decides how much.

None of the benefit taxableup to $25,000up to $32,000

SSA, Retirement Benefits (2026); Internal Revenue Service (IRS) Publication 915. Married filing separately and living together at any time in the year: up to 85% is taxable. The thresholds are set in law and are not indexed for inflation, so more retirees cross them each year (Trustees Report, 2026).

The default is Ruth: $34,800 of benefits and $30,000 of individual retirement account (IRA) withdrawals give combined income of $47,400, which makes $15,890 of her benefits taxable, 45.7% of them. The taxable part is added to her other income and taxed at ordinary rates. You can have SSA withhold 7%, 10%, 12% or 22% of each payment for federal tax.

💡

The 2025–2028 senior deduction. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act added a deduction of $6,000 per person aged 65 or older (both spouses can qualify, $12,000 total) for tax years 2025 through 2028, in addition to the existing extra standard deduction for seniors. It phases out for modified adjusted gross income above $75,000 ($150,000 for joint filers). It does not change how much of your Social Security is taxable; it lowers the income that is taxed (IRS, 2025).

Recent law and the long-range outlook

The 2025 Fairness Act and the 2026 Trustees Report

Windfall Elimination Provision and Government Pension OffsetWEP and GPO
What it is
Two rules, now repealed, that reduced Social Security benefits for people who also received a pension from work not covered by Social Security: WEP cut their own benefit, and GPO cut or eliminated spousal and survivor benefits.
Used for
They applied to many teachers, firefighters and police officers in some states, federal workers under the Civil Service Retirement System, and people with foreign social security pensions.
What it means for you
If you have a non-covered pension, your Social Security is no longer reduced for it, for benefits payable from January 2024 onward.

The Social Security Fairness Act was signed into law on January 5, 2025. It ended WEP and GPO, which had reduced or eliminated benefits for over 2.8 million people; December 2023 was the last month they applied. SSA began adjusting payments on February 25, 2025, and reported that by July 7, 2025, it had sent over 3.1 million retroactive payments totaling $17 billion. Not every public worker gained: SSA notes that about 72% of state and local public employees work in covered jobs and were never affected. (One SSA pamphlet, the January 2026 Retirement Benefits booklet, gives the signing date as January 2024; SSA's dedicated Fairness Act page and its survivors booklet give January 5, 2025, which this module uses.)

The trust fund outlook

Social Security trust funds
What it is
The Old-Age and Survivors Insurance (OASI) and Disability Insurance (DI) trust funds, which hold reserves built up when payroll-tax income exceeded costs, invested in special Treasury securities.
Used for
Paying benefits when income falls short. The trustees report each year on how long reserves will last.
What it means for you
"Depletion" does not mean zero benefits. Payroll taxes keep coming in, and they would cover most scheduled benefits even if Congress did nothing.

The 2026 report says the long-range outlook worsened mainly because the trustees lowered their assumed fertility rate from 1.90 to 1.75 children per woman, lowered immigration assumptions, and because the 2025 tax law's lower rates and new senior deduction reduce the income tax on benefits that flows back to the trust funds. In 2025, OASI's costs of $1,448.8 billion exceeded its income by $200.0 billion, and its reserves fell to $2,338.3 billion. The combined actuarial deficit is 4.42% of taxable payroll over 75 years.

⚠️

How to plan around it. Congress has always acted before past shortfalls, but the form of any fix (higher taxes, lower benefits for some, or both) is unknown. A prudent plan tests the effect of receiving about 80% of scheduled benefits from the mid-2030s, especially for people now under 60. Retirees already collecting have usually been protected in past reforms, though nothing in law guarantees that.

Rules in action

Three real rules, three real effects

Case study 1The Social Security Fairness Act, 2025
What happened
Signed January 5, 2025, the Act repealed WEP and GPO for benefits payable after December 2023. SSA sent over 3.1 million payments totaling $17 billion by July 7, 2025, and began paying the higher monthly amounts, for most people, in April 2025.
What it teaches
Benefit rules do change, sometimes in beneficiaries' favor. People with non-covered pensions who never applied because they expected WEP or GPO to wipe out their benefit may now need to file.
Source
Social Security Administration (2025). Social Security Fairness Act: Windfall Elimination Provision (WEP) and Government Pension Offset (GPO) update (updated July 21, 2025).
Case study 2SSA's own widow's-limit example
What happened
In SSA's operations manual, a worker aged 64 with a PIA of $374.90 was receiving a reduced benefit of $350 and died; the spouse, 66, filed for widow's benefits. Because 82.5% of the PIA was $309.20, lower than the $350 the worker had been receiving, the widow's benefit was set at $350.
What it teaches
A survivor inherits the larger of the worker's actual reduced benefit or 82.5% of PIA. Claiming at 62 therefore cuts the survivor's check by up to 17.5%, while delaying past FRA raises it by up to 24%.
Source
Social Security Administration. POMS RS 00615.320: Reduced WIB, deceased NH entitled to reduced RIB or reduced DIB prior to death (RIB LIM).
Case study 3The 2026 Trustees Report
What happened
The trustees projected OASI depletion in the fourth quarter of 2032, one quarter earlier than the previous year's report, with 78% of scheduled benefits payable afterward; the combined OASDI date stayed at the third quarter of 2034.
What it teaches
The shortfall is large but specific and measurable. For a household like the Harpers, receiving 80% of $76,000 means a $15,200 yearly reduction, which INV-075 turns into about $380,000 of extra savings target at a 4% withdrawal rate.
Source
Social Security and Medicare Boards of Trustees (2026). A summary of the 2026 annual reports.

Decisions

Three claiming decisions

Scenario 1 · The Harpers

Tom (PIA $3,800) plans to stop work at 62. Rachel (PIA $2,500) is two years younger. When should Tom claim?

Claim at 62This locks in $2,660 for Tom and a survivor floor of $3,135 for Rachel. It preserves the portfolio early but leaves the household more exposed to a long life and to a poor sequence of returns later (INV-077).
Claim at 70For a married higher earner in good health, usually the strongest choice. Rachel has about a 64% chance of outliving Tom, and would inherit $4,712 instead of $3,135 for the rest of her life. The cost is about $2,660 × 12 × 8 = $255,000 of forgone early checks, drawn instead from their $1.69 million portfolio.
Claim at 67A reasonable middle path that captures most of the survivor protection ($3,800). Many couples pair it with Rachel claiming her own benefit earlier, since her smaller benefit will be replaced if she is widowed.
The principle: in a couple, the higher earner's claim is a joint-life decision; the lower earner's claim mostly matters while both are alive.
Scenario 2 · Ruth

A seminar tells Ruth, now 68, that she should "switch to her own benefit at 70 to get delayed credits." Should she?

Always switchThe switch is real (SSA allows a survivor to start one benefit and change later), but it only helps if the second benefit is bigger. SSA pays the higher of the two, not both.
Check the numbersRight. With an own PIA of $1,750, her benefit at 70 would be about $2,217, less than her $2,900 survivor benefit, so she should stay put. The strategy works for widows whose own record is strong; Ruth's is not.
The principle: a survivor gets the larger of two benefits, so a switch is only worth planning when the numbers show the second is larger.
Scenario 3 · Monica and Eli

Monica (PIA $2,300, full retirement age 67 in 2035) plans to work at $92,000 until 67. Eli will be 17 when she turns 62, and, because his disability began in childhood, he can be paid on her record for life once she claims. When should she claim?

At 62, while workingIt buys almost nothing. Her benefit would be cut to $1,610 for life, and the earnings test counts her wages against the whole family's benefits. At 2026 limits, $92,000 is $67,520 over the $24,480 threshold, so SSA would withhold $33,760 a year, more than the $33,120 that she and Eli would be owed ($2,760 a month). At 67 SSA would credit her withheld months and largely undo her own reduction, so for her the early claim is close to a wash; but Eli's benefit was never reduced for age, so there is nothing to credit back for his withheld months.
At 67, when she retiresA sound default. The household receives $3,450 a month; her portfolio has to supply about $38,300 a year (INV-075). Eli's benefit ends his SSI but keeps his Medicaid (Spouses and survivors tab), and his Medicare clock starts, with coverage after two years. Waiting 67 to 70 breaks even for her own check at 82.5; a 67-year-old woman has about a 67% chance of reaching it.
At 70, bridging from savingsWorth it if she is healthy and can bridge without running down the cushion she needs for sequence risk (INV-077). She gives up 36 months of her $2,300 ($82,800) for $552 a month more for life, an 8.0% inflation-adjusted payout; INV-082 compares that with what an income annuity pays. Eli gives up 36 months of $1,150 ($41,400), but because his benefit would mostly have replaced SSI, the household's net loss on his side is about $156 a month ($5,616). Counting that, break-even is about 83.3 (a 64% chance); if Eli had no SSI, it would be 88.8 (41%). His $1,725 after her death is the same either way.
The principle: a child's benefit is set by the parent's PIA, not the claiming age; so claim no earlier than you stop working, and decide between full retirement age and 70 on your own health and savings, counting only what the child would really lose.
Scenario 4 · David, Paula and Lena Patel

David is 62 with an illustrative PIA of $3,500. Paula is 48 and caring for Lena, 14, whose disability began before 22. David is considering retirement now. Is this only a 62-versus-70 break-even decision?

David aloneIncomplete. His choice can start Lena's child's benefit, may make Paula eligible for a child-in-care spouse benefit, and sets Paula's possible survivor benefit. The household must compare the whole family's cash flow over both parents' lives.
Model the family recordBest process. Lena's scheduled child's amount while David lives is up to 50% of his PIA, or $1,750 in this illustration. Paula's child-in-care spouse amount may also be up to $1,750, but the family maximum can reduce their combined auxiliary benefits. If David dies, Lena may qualify for 75% of his PIA and Paula's survivor options change. Their actual SSA records, earnings and eligibility control.
Assume two full half-PIA checksWrong. “Up to” is not a promise: both auxiliary payments share the family maximum, and SSA must decide whether Lena and Paula meet the requirements. David's early claim also reduces his own lifetime benefit and can reduce Paula's later survivor income.
The principle: with a large age gap and a disabled child, claiming is a family-benefit and survivor-planning decision, not one person's break-even calculation.

Six things people say about Social Security

Apply it

What this module means for each household

Build the record. Every year Jordan works adds to her 35-year average. She should create a my Social Security account now and check that each year's earnings are recorded correctly; errors are much harder to fix decades later.
Protection they already have. If Marcus or Elena died while Sofia and Leo are young, the children, and the surviving parent caring for them, could receive survivor benefits. That lowers, though it does not remove, the life insurance they need (INV-023).
Her record is Eli's too. Monica's PIA of $2,300 will pay Eli $1,150 a month once she claims and $1,725 after her death, for life, because his disability began before 22; the $4,251.50 family maximum never binds. Claiming at 62 while working would be withheld almost entirely, so her real choice is 67 or 70 (Scenario 3). She should check her earnings record at my Social Security now, and apply for Eli's benefit when she files.
Coordinate two claims. Tom's claiming age decides Rachel's survivor income. A common plan is Tom at 70 and Rachel at or before 67, bridged by the portfolio.
Taxes, not timing. Ruth's claiming decisions are behind her. Her remaining levers are tax: about 46% of her benefit is taxable at her current IRA withdrawals, and her required minimum distributions (RMDs) from 73 will push that toward 85% (INV-081).
Map the family benefit before David files. His claim can start Lena's benefit and may make Paula eligible for a child-in-care spouse benefit, all subject to the family maximum. Delaying can strengthen Paula's survivor income, while claiming earlier may deliver family benefits sooner. They need SSA estimates for every eligible person, not a one-person break-even chart.

Using the 2026 formula, what is the PIA for an AIME of $3,000, in dollars (to the dime)?

90% × $1,286 = $1,157.40. 32% × ($3,000 − $1,286) = 32% × $1,714 = $548.48. Total $1,705.88, rounded down to the dime: $1,705.80.
Exercise 2

Your PIA is $2,000 and your FRA is 67. What is your monthly benefit if you claim at exactly 64, in dollars?

64 is 36 months before 67. 36 × 5/9 of 1% = 20%. $2,000 × 0.80 = $1,600.
Exercise 3

Monica's PIA is $2,300. Using the 2026 family-maximum formula (150% of the first $1,643 of PIA, 272% from $1,643 to $2,371), what is the family maximum on her record, in dollars (to the dime)?

150% × $1,643 = $2,464.50. 272% × ($2,300 − $1,643) = 272% × $657 = $1,787.04. Total $4,251.54, rounded down to the dime: $4,251.50, 184.8% of her PIA. After her own $2,300, $1,951.50 is left for family members, more than Eli's $1,150 (or his $1,725 after her death).
Exercise 4

A single retiree has $24,000 of Social Security and $30,000 of other taxable income, with no adjustments. Using IRS Publication 915's worksheet, how much of the benefit is taxable, in dollars?

Combined income = $30,000 + $12,000 = $42,000. Above $25,000 by $17,000; above $34,000 by $8,000. Taxable = the smaller of half the benefit ($12,000) or half of $9,000 ($4,500), plus 85% of $8,000 ($6,800) = $11,300, capped at 85% of benefits ($20,400). Answer: $11,300.
