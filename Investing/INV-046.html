Value, Growth and Quality

Should you buy what is cheap, what is growing fast, or what is simply a very good business? Investors have argued about this for nearly a century. This module defines each style, follows Benjamin Graham and Warren Buffett, and tests the claims against 99 years of data — including value's 14-year drought.

The question this module answersDo cheap stocks, fast-growing stocks or high-quality stocks earn more over time — and could I stick with any of them through the bad years?

Start here

Bargain or best-in-class?

When you buy a used car you already weigh two things: how good the car is and how much it costs. A great car at an outrageous price can be a bad deal; a plain car at a very low price can be a good one. Stock investors face the same trade-off, and each investing "style" is a different answer to it.

Value investors buy companies that look cheap relative to their assets or earnings, accepting that many are cheap for a reason. Growth investors pay higher prices for companies whose sales and profits are rising fast. Quality investors look for companies that are highly profitable and financially sound, whatever the price label. Over the long history of US data, cheap stocks earned more than expensive ones — but not steadily, and not recently for a long stretch.

Definitions

What makes a stock "value" or "growth"

Value investing
What it is
Buying stocks that are priced low relative to a measure of what the business has or earns: book value, earnings, sales or cash flow.
Used for
Seeking a margin between price and value; in funds, tilting a portfolio toward the cheaper part of the market.
What it means for you
Value stocks are often in unloved industries or going through trouble. You will own companies the news is negative about, sometimes for years.
Growth investing
What it is
Buying companies expected to grow sales and earnings faster than average, usually at high prices relative to current earnings and book value.
Used for
Owning the companies expected to dominate future profits.
What it means for you
You are paying today for profits that have not happened yet. If the growth arrives but is merely good rather than spectacular, the stock can still disappoint.
Book-to-market ratio
What it is
A company's book value (assets minus liabilities, from its balance sheet) divided by its market value. It is the upside-down version of the price-to-book ratio: a book-to-market of 0.5 is a price-to-book of 2.
Used for
The academic definition of value: Eugene Fama and Kenneth French rank all US stocks by book-to-market each year and call the top 30% value and the bottom 30% growth.
What it means for you
It is one simple lens. Companies whose main assets are brands, software or research can look "expensive" on book value even when their earnings are strong.
Going furtherIndex providers measure style differently

Commercial "value" and "growth" indexes, such as S&P's, combine several measures and split each company's weight between the two sides when it is in the middle. Academic portfolios like Fama and French's use one ratio and leave the middle 40% out. So a "large value" index fund and the academic value portfolio can behave quite differently. When a study reports the "value premium," check which definition it uses.

The founders

From "cigar butts" to "wonderful companies"

Benjamin Graham, who wrote Security Analysis (1934, with David Dodd) and The Intelligent Investor (1949), is the father of value investing. His central ideas were that a share is part-ownership of a business with a value you can estimate, that the market's price often swings far above and below it, and that you should buy only when the price is well below your estimate — a margin of safety against your own errors. Warren Buffett, his most famous follower, wrote that he learned most of his investing ideas from The Intelligent Investor, bought in 1949, especially the chapters later editions number 8 and 20, and he quotes "Ben's adage: Price is what you pay, value is what you get."

Margin of safety
What it is
The gap between what you estimate a business is worth and the price you pay. Buying at $60 something you value at $100 gives a 40% margin.
Used for
Protecting against mistakes in your estimate and bad luck in the business.
What it means for you
The principle applies beyond stocks: to houses, to businesses, even to your retirement plan's return assumptions. Plan with room for being wrong.
Mr. Market
What it is
Graham's parable of a business partner who offers every day to buy your share or sell you his, at prices driven by his moods. You are free to ignore him, or to take advantage of him when he is foolish.
Used for
Reframing market swings as opportunities rather than instructions.
What it means for you
A falling price is information about Mr. Market's mood, not necessarily about the business. The discipline is to know your own estimate before he shouts.

Buffett's own shift came from mistakes. In his 1989 letter, reviewing the "mistakes of the first twenty-five years," he describes buying mediocre businesses because they were cheap and concludes: "It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price." That sentence is the bridge between value and what is now called quality investing.

Going furtherA famous Graham purchase

Buffett's 2013 letter adds that later editions of The Intelligent Investor describe an unnamed investment that was "a bonanza" for Graham: he bought it in 1948, and the company was GEICO, the insurer Berkshire Hathaway later owned outright. Even the father of deep value made his largest gain on a high-quality growing business.

Nearly a century of data

A real premium, and a 14-year drought

Using data from 1927 to 2025, the cheapest 30% of US stocks by book-to-market beat the most expensive 30% by about 2.9 percentage points a year (12.96% versus 10.09%). The academic "value factor," HML (high minus low book-to-market), averaged 4.2% a year. But look at when that happened.

The value factor peaked at the end of 2006. By the end of 2020 it had lost 69% from that peak, compounding each year's premium; 2020 alone was −46.9%, as value stocks fell 2.7% and growth stocks rose 36.6%. Value then led strongly in 2021 and 2022, but at the end of 2025 the factor was still 58% below its 2006 high. An investor who tilted toward value in 2007 waited through the worst stretch in the data, and had to believe the next 20 years would look more like the previous 80.

Practitioner depthCompounding a long-short factor

HML is a long-short return: the average of two value portfolios minus the average of two growth portfolios, rebalanced each June (Fama and French, 1993). No one can invest in it directly without shorting. Compounding its yearly returns, as in the drawdown figures above, shows how an investor who held a value tilt would have felt relative to the market, not their actual wealth. A long-only value fund earns roughly the market return plus some fraction of HML, minus its costs.

Two explanations

Reward for risk, or reward for patience?

Why would cheap stocks earn more? Two families of explanation compete, and they have different implications for whether the premium should continue.

Fama and French (1992) showed that two easily measured variables, size and book-to-market, captured the differences in average returns across US stocks that had been attributed to beta, leverage and earnings-to-price. Their three-factor model (1993) added value and size to the market as sources of return (INV-048). What the data also show is that the premium arrives in bursts. In the 1998–1999 technology boom growth stocks returned 36.2% and 27.1% while value returned 13.0% and 6.6%; then from 2000 to 2002 value gained 2.6% a year while growth lost 17.1% a year.

Going furtherHas the value premium been competed away?

It is possible. R. David McLean and Jeffrey Pontiff (2016) found that published return patterns lose about 58% of their returns after publication on average; value is among the most published of all. On the other hand, value's recent drought began more than a decade after the famous papers, and it was followed by strong years in 2021–2022. The honest position is that the premium's expected size is uncertain, probably smaller than its history, and that no one can know in advance when it will appear.

Buffett's insight, measured

Quality: profitable, efficient businesses

Quality investing
What it is
Favoring companies with high and stable profitability, strong balance sheets and efficient use of capital.
Used for
Avoiding "cheap for a reason" companies, and capturing the profitability premium researchers have measured.
What it means for you
"Quality" has no single definition; every fund and index measures it differently. Read how a quality fund defines it before you buy.

Robert Novy-Marx (2013) measured profitability as gross profits divided by assets and found it has "roughly the same power as book-to-market" in predicting differences in average returns: profitable firms earned significantly more than unprofitable ones, even though they tended to be larger and more expensive. Adding profitability to value — buying cheap companies that are also profitable — strengthened value strategies. Fama and French built the same idea into their five-factor model (2015) as RMW, "robust minus weak" operating profitability.

The profitability premium has been steadier than value: from 1964 to 2025 the RMW factor averaged 3.4% a year, was positive in 68% of years, and lost money in only 3 of 53 rolling 10-year periods, against 14 of 53 for value over the same years. It has had its own bad stretches: its worst year was 1999 (−28.9%), and in 2025 it lost 10.9%.

The other side

The case for growth, and the price of it

Growth investing gets a bad name in value circles, but the world's most valuable companies were once growth stocks, and from 2007 to 2020 growth stocks compounded at 12.8% a year against 5.3% for value. The problem is not growth itself; it is the price. A company growing 20% a year is a wonderful business and can still be a poor investment if its price already assumes 30%.

The P/E ratios in Figure 46.2 put numbers on the price. At a P/E of 36.74, the S&P 500 growth half offers an earnings yield of about 2.7% (1 ÷ 36.74); the value half, at 24.90, about 4.0% (1 ÷ 24.90). The growth half must grow its earnings substantially faster, for many years, to deliver the same return. It may; the point is that the price already assumes it will.

⚠️

Style funds and your allocation. Owning both a large-cap growth fund and a large-cap value fund in equal amounts approximately recreates the S&P 500, at a higher combined cost. If you want a tilt, choose one; if you do not, a total-market fund already holds both.

Real history

Three episodes

Case study 1Value's long drought, 2007–2020
What happened
From the start of 2007 to the end of 2020, US value stocks returned 5.3% a year and growth stocks 12.8%. $1 in value became about $2.06; $1 in growth about $5.38. The value factor fell 69% from its 2006 peak. Value then won 2021 (HML +25.6%) and 2022 (+25.7%).
What it teaches
A premium that exists over a century can vanish for longer than most investors' patience. Any tilt you choose must be one you can hold for 15 years of underperformance without abandoning it at the bottom.
Source
Computed from the Kenneth R. French Data Library: Portfolios Formed on BE/ME and Fama/French 3 Factors, annual returns.
Case study 2The technology bubble, 1998–2002
What happened
In 1998 and 1999 growth stocks returned 36.2% and 27.1%; value stocks 13.0% and 6.6%. Value investors looked out of touch. Then, from 2000 through 2002, value stocks gained 2.6% a year while growth stocks lost 17.1% a year, and the whole US market lost 11.6%, 11.2% and 21.1% in turn.
What it teaches
Value tends to lag most at the height of enthusiasm, and to recover when valuations snap back. The same pattern did not repeat on the same schedule in 2007–2020, which is why timing style rotations is so hard.
Source
Computed from the Kenneth R. French Data Library.
Case study 3Buffett's lesson from a textile mill
What happened
In his 1989 letter, reviewing the "mistakes of the first twenty-five years," Buffett describes buying control of Berkshire Hathaway, then a textile maker, because it was cheap, and a department store bought on similar reasoning. Cheap purchases of poor businesses, he concluded, tend to disappoint: the discount is used up by the business's weak economics.
What it teaches
"It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price." Price matters, but so does what you are buying: the insight behind combining value with quality.
Source
Berkshire Hathaway Inc. (1990). 1989 letter to shareholders.

Decisions

Tilt, or hold the market?

Scenario 1 · Denise

Denise reads that value stocks beat growth by nearly 3 points a year since 1927. Her 401(k) offers a total-market index fund (0.02%) and a large-cap value index fund (0.05%). She is 24. Should she put everything in value?

All valueThe long-run premium is real in the data but uncertain in the future, and a large-cap value fund captures only part of it. From 2007 to 2020 an all-value investor trailed by more than 7 points a year.
Core plus modest tiltRight. The market is a sound default, and a small tilt is a choice she can revisit. The decision that matters most at 24 is the contribution rate, not the style.
All growthChasing the style that led the last decade is the classic mistake: growth then faced 2000–2002 after leading in 1998–1999.
The principle: size any tilt to your ability to stay with it when it is losing.
Scenario 2 · The Riveras

Among their six 401(k) funds the Riveras hold a large-cap growth fund and a large-cap value fund, in about equal amounts, plus an S&P 500 fund. What does that combination amount to?

Style-balancedBalancing growth and value just recreates the market's own mix. Unless the two funds are much cheaper than an index fund, the combination adds cost and nothing else.
The market, repeatedRight. A total-market fund plus international and bond funds (INV-044) covers the same ground and adds what they are missing.
The principle: growth plus value in market proportions is the market.

Six claims about investing styles

Apply it

What style means for each household

The market first. A total-market or target-date fund owns every style in proportion. Any tilt is optional and should be small enough to hold for decades.
Drop the offsetting funds. Their growth and value funds cancel into the market. One total-market fund does the same job more cheaply.
Nine years out, no big style bets. Maya's broad index funds already own value and growth in market proportions. A growth-heavy year like 2022 (−25.8%) would hurt most just before she retires at about 67, so any tilt should be small, and a plain total-market fund is a sound default.
An unintended growth bet. Twenty-five technology stocks are a heavy growth and sector tilt. Unwinding it tax-efficiently (INV-045) moves them toward the market's balance of styles.
Quality over yield. If Ruth holds individual stocks, profitable, financially strong companies are a sounder choice than the cheapest or highest-yielding ones (INV-047).

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A fund's P/E ratio is 36.74. What is its earnings yield, in percent?

Earnings yield = E ÷ P = 1 ÷ 36.74 = 0.0272 = 2.72%.
Exercise 2

The value factor fell 69.3% from its 2006 peak to 2020. What percentage gain would it need to get back to the peak?

After a 69.3% loss, 0.307 of the peak remains. Recovery = 1 ÷ 0.307 − 1 = 2.26, a 226% gain.
Exercise 3

You estimate a business is worth $100 a share and buy it at $60. What is your margin of safety, as a percent of your estimate?

(100 − 60) ÷ 100 = 40%. Your estimate could be 40% too high and you would still not have overpaid.
Exercise 4

From 1927 to 2025, value stocks compounded at 12.96% a year and growth stocks at 10.09%. By how many percentage points a year did value lead?

12.96 − 10.09 = 2.87 points a year. Compounded over 99 years that turned $1 into about $174,000 versus $13,600.
