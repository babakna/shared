Self-Employment

Working for yourself means doing the jobs an employer used to do for you: withholding tax, paying both halves of Social Security and Medicare, providing a retirement plan and buying health insurance. It also opens some of the most generous tax breaks in the code. This module covers each, with 2026 figures.

The question this module answersI am self-employed, or about to be. What do I owe, when do I pay it, and how do I save for retirement on income that changes every month?

Start here

Your own boss, and your own payroll department

As an employee you see a gross salary and a smaller net paycheck. Your employer has already withheld income tax, paid half of your Social Security and Medicare tax, offered a 401(k) and probably subsidized health insurance. When you work for yourself, none of that happens unless you do it.

That is the whole shift in one sentence. It explains why new freelancers are so often surprised by their first April tax bill, and why a self-employed person earning the same as an employee needs to set aside noticeably more. The good news is that the same system gives the self-employed some of the highest retirement contribution limits available to anyone.

Both halves of payroll tax

Self-employment tax: 15.3%, on 92.35% of profit

Self-employment taxSE tax
What it is
The Social Security and Medicare tax for people who work for themselves: 12.4% for Social Security plus 2.9% for Medicare, applied to 92.35% of net earnings from self-employment. It is owed if those net earnings are $400 or more.
Used for
Funding the same Social Security and Medicare benefits employees earn; your Schedule SE is how the Social Security Administration (SSA) credits your earnings record.
What it means for you
It is owed on top of income tax, even in a year when your income tax is zero, and even if you already receive Social Security. Half of it is deductible when figuring adjusted gross income.
SE tax = 12.4% × min(0.9235 × profit, wage base remaining) + 2.9% × 0.9235 × profit

Three details of self-employment tax from IRS Tax Topic 554 matter most. First, only 92.35% of net profit is taxed; this mirrors the fact that an employer's half of payroll tax is not wages to an employee. Second, the Social Security part stops at a yearly maximum: for 2026 the SSA's taxable maximum is $184,500, shared with any W-2 wages you also earn. The 2.9% Medicare part has no cap. Third, an Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9% applies to self-employment income above $200,000 for single filers and $250,000 for joint filers ($125,000 married filing separately).

Worked example. Suppose Elena leaves her $60,000 salaried job to freelance (an illustration used throughout this module), and her design business nets $90,000 in 2026. Net earnings are $90,000 × 0.9235 = $83,115. Social Security: 12.4% × $83,115 = $10,306.26. Medicare: 2.9% × $83,115 = $2,410.34. Self-employment tax: $12,716.60, of which half, $6,358.30, is deductible. Income tax is owed separately.

Going furtherSE tax builds your Social Security record

Self-employment tax is not only a cost. The SSA uses the information from Schedule SE to compute your future benefits, so the earnings you report are the earnings you are credited with (IRS Tax Topic 554). In a year with a loss or very small profit, Tax Topic 554 notes that one of two optional methods on Schedule SE may let you report more net earnings, which can give you credit toward Social Security coverage or raise certain credits. Under-reporting income to save SE tax also shrinks the benefit you will draw later.

Pay as you go

Quarterly estimated taxes and the safe harbors

The federal income tax is pay-as-you-go: tax is due as income is earned, through withholding or estimated payments (IRS Tax Topic 306). With no employer withholding, a self-employed person generally pays four installments a year using Form 1040-ES. Paying too little, too late, can bring an underpayment penalty even if you pay everything by April.

Estimated tax safe harbor
What it is
The payment level that protects you from the underpayment penalty. You are generally safe if withholding plus timely estimates cover the smaller of 90% of this year's tax or 100% of last year's tax — 110% if last year's adjusted gross income (AGI) was more than $150,000 ($75,000 if married filing separately) — or if you owe less than $1,000 after withholding.
Used for
Setting quarterly payments without knowing this year's income in advance.
What it means for you
Last year's tax is a known number. Paying 100% (or 110%) of it in four equal parts removes the penalty risk even if this year turns out much better; the rest is simply due in April.
Payment for 2026 incomeDue date
1st installmentApril 15, 2026
2nd installmentJune 15, 2026
3rd installmentSeptember 15, 2026
4th installmentJanuary 15, 2027 (not needed if you file by February 1, 2027 and pay in full)

Source: IRS, Form 1040-ES (2026).

With the defaults — $14,000 of tax last year, AGI under $150,000 and $20,000 expected this year — 100% of last year ($14,000) is smaller than 90% of this year ($18,000), so the target is $14,000: $3,500 by each due date, and $6,000 more with the return in April 2027, penalty-free.

Going furtherUneven income and a side job

If most of your income arrives late in the year, the annualized installment method on Form 2210 can lower the early payments to match when income was actually earned (IRS Tax Topic 306). If you also have a W-2 job, Form 1040-ES notes that you can instead ask your employer to withhold more; withholding is considered paid one-fourth on each due date unless you show otherwise (Instructions for Form 2210), which makes a late-year catch-up easy.

Practitioner depthA quick set-aside rule, and its limits

Many freelancers move a fixed share of every payment, often 25–30%, into a separate tax account. That is a rule of thumb, not a calculation: at $90,000 of profit, self-employment tax alone is about 14% of profit, and federal plus state income tax can add 10–20% more depending on the household. Recompute the percentage each year from your actual return.

After P.L. 119-21

The qualified business income deduction is now permanent

Qualified business income (QBI) deductionsection 199A
What it is
A deduction of up to 20% of qualified business income from a sole proprietorship, partnership, S corporation, trust or estate, created by the 2017 tax law. It is taken whether you itemize or not, and is limited to 20% of taxable income minus net capital gain.
Used for
Lowering income tax on business profits; it does not reduce self-employment tax.
What it means for you
For most self-employed people below the income thresholds, it removes income tax from about a fifth of their profit, after subtracting half of SE tax, self-employed health insurance and retirement contributions.

The QBI deduction was scheduled to end after 2025. Section 70105 of Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025, rewrote the subsection that contained the expiration date, so the deduction continues with no end date. The same section widened the phase-in ranges and added a small minimum deduction:

Sources: P.L. 119-21, sec. 70105; IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32, sec. 4.26; IRS, Qualified business income deduction.

Above the threshold, two limits phase in across the range: the deduction for most businesses becomes limited by the W-2 wages they pay and their property, and "specified service trades or businesses" stop qualifying at all once taxable income is above the phase-in range (Instructions for Form 8995). The minimum deduction applies to a business in which you materially participate.

Worked example. Elena's $90,000 profit, less the $6,358 deductible half of SE tax and a $16,728 SEP contribution, leaves QBI of $66,913. Twenty percent is $13,383. The Riveras' taxable income before the deduction (Marcus's $85,000 plus Elena's profit, less those adjustments and the $32,200 standard deduction for 2026) is about $119,700, and 20% of that is $23,943, so the full $13,383 is allowed. They are far below the $403,500 threshold, so no other limits apply.

Your own retirement plan

SEP IRA versus solo 401(k): the 2026 limits

With no employer plan, you set up your own. For a business with no employees other than a spouse, the two common choices are a SEP IRA and a solo 401(k). Both are funded with pre-tax dollars (a solo 401(k) can also accept Roth deferrals if the plan allows), and both allow far more than an IRA's $7,500 for 2026.

At Elena's $90,000 profit, the SEP maximum is $16,728 (20% of $83,641.70). The solo 401(k) allows the same employer share plus her own $24,500 deferral: $41,228. The employee deferral is why a solo 401(k) usually wins at modest incomes. At high profits both reach the $72,000 cap, and the SEP's simplicity can win.

Going furtherWhy "20%" and not 25%

A self-employed person's "compensation" is net earnings after subtracting the deductible half of SE tax and the contribution itself. A 25% plan rate applied to that circular base works out to 20% of the base before the contribution, which is why Publication 560's rate table for the self-employed lists 0.20 for a 25% plan. The calculator applies this directly.

Practitioner depthTwo jobs, one deferral limit

The $24,500 employee deferral limit applies to the person across all plans in the year. If Elena had deferred $10,000 at a job earlier in 2026, only $14,500 of deferral room would remain in her solo 401(k). The $72,000 total limit, by contrast, counts contributions to the plans you maintain (Publication 560), so her old employer's match in its own plan does not use up room in hers. Once the business has employees, Publication 560's participation rules apply to them too, and a solo 401(k) stops being "solo."

Buying your own

The self-employed health insurance deduction

Self-employed health insurance deduction
What it is
A deduction, figured on Form 7206 and taken on Schedule 1, for medical, dental, vision and qualified long-term care insurance premiums you pay for yourself, your spouse, your dependents and a child under 27 at year-end.
Used for
Putting the self-employed closer to employees, whose employer-paid premiums are tax-free.
What it means for you
It reduces income tax, not SE tax, and is limited by the business's net earnings. It is not allowed for any month you were eligible for an employer-subsidized plan, including through a spouse's job.

That last rule catches many couples. The instructions for Form 7206 exclude amounts for any month you were eligible to participate in a health plan subsidized by your employer or your spouse's employer, whether or not you enrolled. If Elena buys her own policy while Marcus's employer offers subsidized family coverage, her premiums do not qualify for the deduction.

Other benefits you now provide yourself

  • Disability insurance. The self-employed have no employer group policy; an individual disability policy protects the income that funds everything else (INV-023).
  • A health savings account, if you choose a qualifying high-deductible plan (INV-033).
  • An emergency fund sized for irregular income: larger than an employee's, and no state unemployment benefit behind it in most cases (INV-100).

Budgeting when pay is lumpy

Pay yourself a salary

The hardest part of self-employment for many people is not taxes; it is a checking account that swings from flush to empty. The fix is to separate the business's income from the household's pay, and put a buffer account in between.

Income bufferalso called a smoothing account
What it is
A separate account that receives all business income after the tax set-aside and pays the household a fixed monthly "salary."
Used for
Turning irregular receipts into a predictable paycheck, so the household budget can work like an employee's.
What it means for you
Start with two to three months of salary in the buffer. Set the salary at or below your average net income, and raise it only after the buffer has held for a year.
  1. Three accounts. Business checking (all income lands here), tax savings (the set-aside and quarterly payments come from here) and the buffer (pays the salary).
  2. A lean baseline budget. Plan the household on the salary, not on good months.
  3. Save good months, not every month. Extra retirement contributions and investing can happen once the buffer is full, which suits the SEP and solo 401(k), since employer contributions can be made after year-end.

Real law

Three changes that shape self-employed saving

Case study 1The QBI deduction's expiration is removed, 2025
What happened
The 2017 tax law created the section 199A deduction with an end date after 2025. P.L. 119-21, section 70105, replaced the subsection containing that end date with a new $400 minimum deduction for taxpayers with at least $1,000 of qualified business income from active businesses, and widened the phase-in ranges from $50,000 and $100,000 to $75,000 and $150,000, effective for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025.
What it teaches
Tax planning for the self-employed can now assume the deduction continues, rather than planning around a 2026 cliff. Laws that are "permanent" can still be changed by a later Congress; build plans that work under reasonable alternatives.
Source
Public Law 119-21, sec. 70105; IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32, sec. 3.12 and 4.26.
Case study 2A solo 401(k) can be opened after the year ends, 2023
What happened
For 2023 and later years, a sole proprietor with no employees can adopt a new 401(k) plan after the end of the tax year, provided it is adopted by the tax-filing deadline without extensions. Elective deferrals for that first year must be paid to the plan before that deadline.
What it teaches
A freelancer who discovers in March how good last year was can still open a solo 401(k) and make both deferrals and employer contributions for that year, instead of being limited to a SEP.
Source
Internal Revenue Service. Publication 560 (2025), Retirement plans for small business, "What's New" and "Set-up deadline."
Case study 3A bigger catch-up at ages 60 to 63, 2025
What happened
Starting in 2025, workers who turn 60, 61, 62 or 63 during the year may make a higher catch-up contribution to a 401(k): $11,250 for 2026, instead of the $8,000 available at 50 and older (IRS Notice 2025-67).
What it teaches
Someone who consults in their early 60s, as Tom Harper plans to, can contribute up to $72,000 plus $11,250 to a solo 401(k) on sufficient self-employment income. The window closes at 64.
Source
Internal Revenue Service. Notice 2025-67; news release, "401(k) limit increases to $24,500 for 2026."

Decisions

Three households go out on their own

Scenario 1 · The Riveras

Elena leaves her job to freelance and expects about $90,000 of profit. The family is on Marcus's subsidized employer health plan. She wants to save as much as possible for retirement and asks which plan to open, and whether she can deduct health premiums.

SEP and deduct premiumsThe SEP works but caps her at about $16,700. And the health deduction is not allowed for months she is eligible for a spouse's subsidized employer plan (Form 7206 instructions).
Solo 401(k), no health deductionRight. Up to about $41,200 of contributions, and she can adopt the plan as late as the filing deadline. Staying on Marcus's plan is likely cheaper anyway.
WaitShe can wait: both plans can be funded after year-end. But without a plan, the default is spending the money. Setting a monthly transfer now is easier.
The principle: at modest incomes the solo 401(k)'s employee deferral usually beats a SEP; check health-plan eligibility before claiming the deduction.
Scenario 2 · Jordan

Jordan starts weekend photography and expects $12,000 of profit this year on top of her $62,000 salary. She has never made an estimated payment. What should she do?

Wait until AprilSelf-employment tax alone is about $1,700 ($12,000 × 0.9235 × 15.3%), plus income tax at her rate. If she owes $1,000 or more beyond the safe harbors, a penalty may apply.
More withholdingOften the simplest route: withholding is treated as paid one-fourth on each due date (Instructions for Form 2210), so she can catch up late in the year, and it can cover both income and SE tax.
Quarterly paymentsAlso correct, and necessary if she had no paycheck. It requires remembering four dates.
The principle: side income needs its own tax plan; extra withholding and estimated payments both work.
Scenario 3 · The Harpers

Tom plans to retire at 62 and consult part-time, expecting about $80,000 of profit a year. The Harpers want to keep lowering their taxable income before RMDs begin. Which plan fits?

SEP IRAAbout $14,900 a year at $80,000 of profit, with no catch-up.
Solo 401(k)Up to about $50,600 in 2026 terms: $24,500 + $14,870 + $11,250. They could also make some deferrals Roth, if the plan allows, to diversify future taxes (INV-038).
The principle: self-employment in the early 60s can be a powerful saving window; match the plan to the age-based limits.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A sole proprietor has $60,000 of net profit in 2026 and no W-2 wages. What is the self-employment tax, in dollars?

Net earnings: $60,000 × 0.9235 = $55,410, below the $184,500 wage base. Tax: $55,410 × 0.153 = $8,477.73.
Exercise 2

Same business. What is the maximum 2026 SEP IRA contribution, in dollars?

Deductible half of SE tax: $4,238.86. Base: $60,000 − $4,238.86 = $55,761.14. SEP: 20% × $55,761.14 = $11,152.23. A solo 401(k) could add a $24,500 deferral, for $35,652.23.
Exercise 3

Last year's tax was $30,000 and last year's AGI was $210,000. This year's tax is expected to be $40,000, with no withholding. What quarterly estimated payment meets the safe harbor, in dollars?

AGI above $150,000, so the prior-year rule is 110%: $33,000. The current-year rule is 90% × $40,000 = $36,000. The smaller is $33,000; divided by 4 = $8,250 per quarter. The remaining $7,000 is due with the return.
Exercise 4

Elena's QBI is $90,000 minus $6,358.30 (half of SE tax) minus a $16,728.34 SEP contribution. Her household is below the threshold and 20% of taxable income is larger. What is her QBI deduction, in dollars?

QBI = $90,000 − $6,358.30 − $16,728.34 = $66,913.36. Deduction = 20% × $66,913.36 = $13,382.67.
