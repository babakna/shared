529s and Education Accounts

Saving for a child's education has its own tax-advantaged account, the 529 plan, and a few alternatives with different trade-offs. The 2025 tax law widened what a 529 can pay for, from private-school tutoring to professional credentials. This module covers the federal rules, the Arizona and Ohio state deductions that matter to two of our households, the new path from a 529 into a Roth individual retirement account (IRA), and how each account affects financial aid.

The question this module answersWhich account should hold the money for a child's education, and how much tax does it save?

Start here

College is a known, dated expense

Most families save for a child's education the way they save for anything else: a savings account, maybe some investments, and hope. That works, and money saved anywhere beats money not saved.

Education is unusual, though, because you know roughly when the bill arrives and roughly how large it will be, often a decade or more ahead. That is exactly the kind of goal where tax-free growth compounds into real money. The 529 plan, named for section 529 of the tax code, lets the earnings grow and come out free of federal tax if they pay for qualified education costs, and many states add a deduction for contributions.

Marcus and Elena Rivera have not started saving for Sofia (7) or Leo (4). Figure 34.1 shows what $350 a month for Sofia would become by the fall she turns 18.

The rules

Contributions, growth and withdrawals

Every state sponsors at least one 529 program, and you can generally use any state's plan. There are two kinds: savings plans, which invest your money in funds, often age-based portfolios that grow more conservative as the child nears college; and prepaid tuition plans, which sell future tuition at today's prices at participating schools. This module focuses on savings plans, which most families use.

Superfundingthe five-year gift election
What it is
A donor who contributes more than the annual gift exclusion to a 529 in one year can elect to treat the gift as made evenly over five years (26 U.S.C. §529(c)(2)(B)).
Used for
Front-loading a large sum, for example from a grandparent, so it grows tax-free longer, without using lifetime gift-tax exemption. At the 2026 exclusion, up to $95,000 per donor per beneficiary.
What it means for you
It is reported on a gift tax return. If the donor dies within the five years, part of the gift returns to the donor's estate.
Going furtherCoordinating with education tax credits

You cannot use the same dollars of tuition for both a tax-free 529 withdrawal and an education credit. The American opportunity tax credit is worth up to $2,500 a year per student for the first four years of college (100% of the first $2,000 of qualified expenses and 25% of the next $2,000), phased out between modified adjusted gross income (AGI) of $80,000 and $90,000 ($160,000–$180,000 joint). A common approach is to pay $4,000 of tuition from cash or loans to claim the credit and use the 529 for the rest (IRS Publication 970).

What it can pay for

Qualified expenses, including the 2025 changes

Qualified higher education expenses
What it is
Tuition and required fees, books, supplies and equipment at an eligible college, university or vocational school (almost every accredited school that takes federal student aid); computers and internet used mainly by the student; and room and board for students enrolled at least half-time, up to the school's cost-of-attendance allowance or actual on-campus charges.
Used for
Deciding which withdrawals are tax-free. The total withdrawn in a year is compared with that year's expenses, after subtracting tax-free aid such as scholarships.
What it means for you
Take withdrawals in the same calendar year as the expenses, and keep the bills. Travel, insurance and most transportation do not qualify.

What Public Law 119-21 changed

  • K–12 expenses widened. Before, a 529 could pay only K–12 tuition. For distributions after July 4, 2025, it can also pay for curriculum and curricular materials, books and other instructional materials, online educational materials, tutoring by a qualified tutor who is not a relative, fees for standardized achievement tests, Advanced Placement exams and college admission tests, dual-enrollment fees, and educational therapies for students with disabilities.
  • K–12 limit doubled. The cap on K–12 amounts rises from $10,000 to $20,000 per beneficiary per year for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025, so $20,000 for 2026.
  • Credentials added. For distributions after July 4, 2025, qualified expenses include tuition, fees, books and equipment for recognized postsecondary credential programs, testing fees required to obtain or maintain a credential, and required continuing education.

Sources: 26 U.S.C. §529 as amended and statutory notes on the 2025 amendments (Legal Information Institute); IRS Publication 970 (2025).

⚠️

State tax may not follow. The federal changes do not automatically change state law. Some states tax, or recapture deductions on, K–12 or other non-college withdrawals. Check your state's rules before using a 529 for anything other than college.

Arizona and Ohio

State tax breaks for contributions

There is no federal deduction for 529 contributions, but many states offer one. The rules differ in three ways that matter: how much you can deduct, whether you must use your own state's plan, and whether excess contributions carry forward.

The state deduction is a modest bonus: a few hundred dollars a year for most families. It is a good reason to prefer your own state's plan when its fees and investments are competitive; it is not a reason to accept a high-cost plan, because fees compound for years and the deduction does not (INV-007).

Practitioner depthWhy the tax gap looks small, and when it is not

Over 11 years at 6%, only about 28% of the final 529 balance is growth, so the federal tax avoided is modest: about $2,760 at 15%. The advantage grows with time: starting at birth, or letting a grandparent superfund early, makes most of the balance growth. It also grows with your tax rate. The larger risk is on the other side: money in a 529 that is never used for education faces tax plus the 10% additional tax on the earnings, which is why the 529-to-Roth rollover and the wider list of qualified expenses matter.

If the money is not needed

Rolling unused 529 money into a Roth IRA

529-to-Roth rolloverSECURE 2.0, distributions after 2023
What it is
A direct trustee-to-trustee transfer from a 529 to a Roth IRA for the same beneficiary, without tax or penalty, if the 529 has been maintained for more than 15 years.
Used for
Reducing the cost of over-saving, or of a child who wins a scholarship or skips college.
What it means for you
It turns leftover college money into the beneficiary's retirement savings, a few thousand dollars a year at a time.
  1. Account age: the 529 must have been maintained for the 15-year period ending on the date of the rollover.
  2. Recent money excluded: contributions made in the last 5 years, and their earnings, cannot be rolled over.
  3. Annual cap: each year's rollover is limited to the Roth IRA contribution limit, $7,500 for 2026 (or the beneficiary's taxable compensation for the year, if less), minus any other IRA contributions the beneficiary makes that year. The Roth IRA income limits do not apply (26 U.S.C. §408A(c)(3)(E)).
  4. Lifetime cap: $35,000 per beneficiary.

Sources: 26 U.S.C. §529(c)(3)(E); IRS Publication 590-A (2025); IRS Publication 970 (2025).

The 15-year clock is the reason to open a 529 early, even with a small amount: an account opened for Leo at 4 in 2026 would be eligible from 2041, when he is 19. If you later change the beneficiary, ask the plan how it treats the 15-year period before relying on a rollover.

Student Aid Index (SAI)
What it is
The number the FAFSA produces from family income and assets to determine eligibility for need-based federal aid; colleges use it, and sometimes their own formulas, to set aid packages.
Used for
Estimating how much a family is expected to contribute. For a dependent student in 2026–27, parents' net assets above an allowance are converted to income at 12% and then assessed, with other available income, at 22% to 47%; the student's own assets are counted at 20%.
What it means for you
A dollar in a parent-owned 529 raises the SAI by at most about 5.6 cents (12% × 47%); a dollar in the student's custodial account raises it by 20 cents. Retirement accounts are not counted as assets, though untaxed IRA distributions are added back to income.

For a child with a disability: the ABLE account

A 529 assumes the child will one day pay tuition. For a child with a significant disability that may not happen, and a second problem appears at 18: Supplemental Security Income (SSI), the needs-based federal benefit, is lost in any month the adult's countable resources exceed $2,000. Congress created a cousin of the 529 for exactly this case, in section 529A of the tax code.

ABLE accountsection 529A; Ohio's program is called STABLE
What it is
A state-run, tax-advantaged account owned by a person whose disability began before age 26, or before 46 for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025. Anyone can contribute; earnings grow tax-free, and withdrawals are tax-free when they pay qualified disability expenses: education, housing, transportation, employment training and support, assistive technology, personal support services, health, financial management, legal fees and funeral costs, among others. A person may have only one ABLE account at a time.
Used for
Saving for a disabled person's lifetime costs without losing SSI or Medicaid. For SSI, the first $100,000 in an ABLE account is not counted as a resource, and contributions from a parent are not income to the beneficiary.
What it means for you
For 2026, total contributions from everyone are capped at $20,000 per beneficiary (a beneficiary who works may add more from earnings). At the beneficiary's death, the state may claim what remains to repay Medicaid, which is why larger sums usually go to a third-party special needs trust instead (INV-102).

Going furtherTrump accounts are not education accounts

P.L. 119-21 created Trump accounts for children: a new kind of IRA that cannot be funded before July 4, 2026, accepts up to $5,000 a year from individuals, must invest in US stock index funds, and generally cannot be withdrawn before the year the child turns 18, after which it is treated like a traditional IRA. A $1,000 federal pilot contribution is available for children born after 2024 and before 2029, so not for Sofia, Leo or Eli. Because withdrawals are taxed like a traditional IRA's, it is a long-term savings vehicle rather than a replacement for a 529. Sources: IRS, "One, Big, Beautiful Bill provisions"; IRS Publication 970 (2025).

Real law

Three laws that built the 529

Case study 11996: section 529 is created
What happened
The Small Business Job Protection Act of 1996 (P.L. 104-188, August 20, 1996) added section 529, giving federal tax status to state-run tuition programs. It has been amended many times since, including in 1997, 2001, 2017, 2019, 2022 and 2025.
What it teaches
The 529 grew out of state experiments. That is why each state runs its own plans with its own fees, investments and state tax rules, and why comparing plans matters.
Source
26 U.S.C. §529 and statutory notes (Legal Information Institute).
Case study 22017 and 2025: from college to K–12 and beyond
What happened
P.L. 115-97 (December 22, 2017) allowed up to $10,000 a year of K–12 tuition for distributions after December 31, 2017. P.L. 119-21 (July 4, 2025) broadened K–12 expenses beyond tuition, doubled the cap to $20,000 from 2026, and added postsecondary credentials.
What it teaches
Congress has repeatedly widened what counts. For families like the Riveras, the $20,000 K–12 cap and the credential rules reduce the risk that money saved for a child who does not attend a four-year college goes unused.
Source
26 U.S.C. §529 statutory notes on the 2017 and 2025 amendments.
Case study 32022: the Roth escape hatch
What happened
The SECURE 2.0 Act (P.L. 117-328) allowed rollovers from long-term 529 accounts to the beneficiary's Roth IRA, for distributions after December 31, 2023, up to $35,000 per lifetime.
What it teaches
Over-saving in a 529 is now less costly, which makes early, steady saving easier to justify. The 15-year and 5-year rules reward opening the account early and punish last-minute deposits.
Source
26 U.S.C. §529(c)(3)(E) and notes; IRS Publication 590-A (2025).

Decisions

Three education-saving decisions

Scenario 1 · Maya

A coworker tells Maya to "start a 529" for Eli, 13. Eli has autism and an intellectual disability; he may never enroll in college, and he is expected to qualify for SSI at 18. Maya can set aside $250 a month for him. Where should it go?

A 529Not wrong, and it can pay for educational therapies, K–12 costs or a credential program. But if Eli never pays tuition, the earnings face tax plus 10% unless the money is rolled into an ABLE account, and that rollover uses up the same $20,000 yearly ABLE room.
STABLE accountRight. It pays tax-free for housing, transportation, therapies and support as well as education, the first $100,000 is ignored for SSI, and Maya's $3,000 a year earns an Ohio deduction worth about $83 (2.75%). Because the state can claim the balance for Medicaid at Eli's death, it suits spending money, not a lifetime inheritance (INV-102).
In Eli's nameThe worst choice. At 18, anything in his own name over $2,000 blocks SSI, and a custodial account becomes his outright at Ohio's age for ending the custodianship.
The principle: for a child who may rely on means-tested benefits, choose the account by how the benefit rules count it, not by its tax break alone.
Scenario 2 · The Riveras

The Riveras want to start saving for Sofia and Leo. Should they use Arizona's plan or another state's?

In-state onlyMany states require their own plan for a deduction, as Ohio does. Arizona's statute refers to plans "established pursuant to section 529" without limiting it to Arizona's, so the Riveras have a free choice.
Compare on costRight. At $100 a year per child, the deduction is small next to a difference of even 0.3% in yearly fees on a balance that may reach $60,000–$90,000.
The principle: know whether your state's deduction requires its own plan, then choose on cost.
Scenario 3 · The Harpers

Ethan, 20, is in college. The Harpers pay his costs from their taxable account. Would the American opportunity credit help?

$2,500Only for joint filers with MAGI below $160,000 in full, and none at $180,000 or more.
NoRight. Selling stock to pay tuition also realizes capital gains at 15% (INV-030). Paying from cash flow is often simplest.
The principle: check the income limits on education credits before planning around them.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A $20,000 non-qualified 529 withdrawal includes $6,000 of earnings. In the 22% bracket, what is the federal tax cost, in dollars?

Only the $6,000 of earnings: $1,320 of income tax + $600 of additional tax = $1,920.
Exercise 2

A single parent filing as head of household has a MAGI of $82,720. What American opportunity credit can the parent claim for a child's first year of college, in dollars, if $4,000 of qualified expenses is paid outside the 529?

Tentative credit $2,500, times (90,000 − 82,720) ÷ 10,000 = 0.728: $1,820.
Exercise 3

Using the five-year election, what is the most a grandparent can contribute to one grandchild's 529 in 2026 without using lifetime gift-tax exemption, in dollars?

The 2026 annual exclusion is $19,000; spread over five years: $95,000 (assuming no other gifts to that grandchild in those years).
Exercise 4

The Riveras contribute $4,200 each to 529s for Sofia and Leo in 2026. How much Arizona income tax does the subtraction save, in dollars?

The joint subtraction is capped at $4,000 per beneficiary: $8,000 × 2.5% = $200.
