Marriage and Divorce

A marriage is a relationship, and it is also a legal contract with financial terms. If you do not write those terms yourself, your state and the tax code write them for you. This module shows what changes when you marry, what the 2026 tax rules do to two incomes, and how retirement savings, support and Social Security are divided if a marriage ends.

The question this module answersWhat does marriage change about my money, and how do I protect both of us if it ends?

Start here

Two people, one set of rules

Most couples handle money the ordinary way: they talk about it, open a joint account for shared bills, and get on with life. That works. Nothing in this module says marriage needs a lawyer or a spreadsheet.

But the moment you marry, a set of legal defaults switches on whether you think about them or not. Your tax filing status changes. In nine states, including Arizona where the Riveras live, most of what either spouse earns becomes owned by both. Your spouse gains rights to your 401(k). You may each gain a claim on the other's Social Security record. And if the marriage ends, the same defaults decide who keeps what. Figure 95.1 lists the six areas that change.

Before and after the wedding

How couples combine money

There is no single right way to merge finances. What matters is that both people understand the arrangement and that it matches how the law will treat your money anyway. Couples usually land on one of three models.

Filing statuschosen each year, on your status on December 31
What it is
The category you file your federal tax return under: single, head of household, married filing jointly, married filing separately, or qualifying surviving spouse. It sets your brackets and your standard deduction.
Used for
Working out your tax. The IRS says your status depends on your marital status on the last day of the tax year: marry on December 31 and you are treated as married for the whole year; a final divorce decree by December 31 makes you unmarried for the whole year.
What it means for you
The wedding date or the date a divorce becomes final can change a whole year's tax. Married couples almost always compare joint and separate returns; for most, joint is cheaper.

A money conversation before the wedding

Planners and counselors often suggest covering the same ground before marriage: each person's income, debts and credit history; how bills will be split; savings goals; how much either can spend without asking; and what each expects about children, housing and supporting parents. Credit reports stay individual after marriage — there is no merged "couple's score" — but lenders look at both reports when you borrow together, so a partner's credit history matters for a joint mortgage.

💡

Update three documents within a month of the wedding: the beneficiaries on every retirement account and life insurance policy, your employer benefits (marriage usually lets you add a spouse to health coverage mid-year), and your will. INV-085 covers beneficiaries and titling in depth.

Going furtherYour spouse's automatic claim on your 401(k)

Federal tax law requires most 401(k)-type plans to pay a married participant's balance at death in full to the surviving spouse, unless the spouse consents in writing, in the manner the law requires, to a different beneficiary (26 U.S.C. 401(a)(11)(B)(iii) and 417(a)(2)). So if Maya marries and wants her sister to inherit part of her 401(k), her spouse must sign a consent — typically witnessed by a notary or plan representative. IRAs are different: an IRA owner can usually name anyone, although in community property states a spouse may have a claim on the part funded with community earnings.

The tax effect of saying "I do"

The marriage penalty and the marriage bonus

A married couple files one return on their combined income. Whether that costs more or less than two unmarried returns depends on how the joint brackets compare with two sets of single brackets — and on how evenly the two incomes are split.

Marriage penalty and marriage bonus
What it is
A penalty is extra tax a couple pays because they are married, compared with what the same two people would owe filing as unmarried individuals. A bonus is the reverse: tax saved by marrying.
Used for
Understanding why a wedding, a divorce or a remarriage changes your tax bill even though nobody's income changed.
What it means for you
For 2026, couples with one high earner usually get a bonus; couples with two similar incomes usually see no change; penalties appear mainly at very high incomes, for single parents who lose head-of-household status, and for retirees whose Social Security becomes more taxable.

Read the last column. For 2026, every joint bracket up to the 35% bracket is exactly twice the single bracket, and so is the standard deduction. That means two people with equal incomes pay the same tax married or not, unless their combined taxable income passes $768,700. Where the incomes are unequal, the joint return lets the lower earner's unused low brackets absorb the higher earner's income: a bonus.

Going furtherOther rules that treat couples differently

Brackets are not the only place filing status matters. Some thresholds are exactly double for couples and so are neutral: the child tax credit phase-out ($200,000 single, $400,000 joint), the new senior deduction phase-out ($75,000 and $150,000) and the 2026 Medicare premium surcharge thresholds ($109,000 and $218,000). Others are not doubled and create penalties: the 0.9% additional Medicare tax starts at $200,000 of wages for a single filer but only $250,000 for a couple, and the income levels at which Social Security benefits become taxable are $25,000 and $34,000 for a single filer but only $32,000 and $44,000 for a couple (26 U.S.C. 86). Those unindexed Social Security thresholds are why retirees who remarry often see their tax rise (see Ruth in Tab 8).

Practitioner depthWhen married filing separately makes sense

Married filing separately uses brackets that are half the joint brackets up to the 35% bracket, which for 2026 ends at $384,350 — so separate returns almost never lower a couple's combined bracket tax, and IRS Publication 501 notes that several credits and deductions are reduced or unavailable when you file separately. People still choose it to keep their tax liability separate from a spouse's (each is liable for the full tax on a joint return), or when one spouse has large deductions that depend on their own income, such as medical expenses above 7.5% of adjusted gross income. In the nine community property states, each spouse filing separately must generally report half of all community income plus all of their own separate income (IRS Publication 555), which removes much of the advantage.

Who owns what

Prenuptial agreements, community property and equitable distribution

Every state has a default answer to "who owns what we built?" — either community property or equitable distribution. It matters most at divorce and death. You can accept your state's answer or write your own with a premarital agreement.

Community property9 states
What it is
A system in which most property either spouse acquires during the marriage belongs to both of them equally, no matter whose name is on it or who earned it. IRS Publication 555 lists the nine community property states: Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington and Wisconsin.
Used for
Deciding ownership during the marriage, and dividing property at divorce or death. Arizona law says all property acquired by either spouse during the marriage is community property except property received by gift or inheritance, or acquired after divorce papers are served (A.R.S. 25-211).
What it means for you
In Phoenix, the Riveras each own half of the contributions and growth in both 401(k)s since their wedding, including Marcus's larger account.
Equitable distributionthe other 41 states
What it is
A system in which each spouse owns what is titled to them during the marriage, but at divorce a court divides "marital property" (roughly, what was acquired during the marriage) in a way it finds fair, which is not always 50/50.
Used for
Divorce settlements in states such as Ohio, North Carolina and Pennsylvania. North Carolina's statute starts from an equal division of marital property unless the court finds equal is not equitable, and then weighs factors such as each spouse's income and property (N.C.G.S. 50-20(c)).
What it means for you
Property you brought into the marriage, or inherited, generally stays separate in both systems — if you can trace it. Mixing it into joint accounts ("commingling") can make it marital.

Prenuptial agreements

Prenuptial (premarital) agreement
What it is
A prenuptial agreement is a written contract signed before the wedding that changes the state's default rules on property, debts and support. Arizona, for example, requires it to be in writing and signed by both parties, and it takes effect on marriage (A.R.S. 25-202).
Used for
Protecting a business, a family inheritance or assets for children from an earlier relationship; deciding in advance how property and debts would be divided; and, within limits, spousal support.
What it means for you
It only works if it is fair when signed. Arizona will not enforce one against a spouse who proves they did not sign voluntarily, or that it was unconscionable when signed and that, beforehand, they did not receive fair disclosure of the other's finances, did not waive that disclosure in writing, and could not reasonably have known those finances (A.R.S. 25-202(C)). Separate lawyers and full financial disclosure are the standard protection.
Practitioner depthHow ownership rules reach taxes and estates

Community property has a large tax benefit at the first death: under 26 U.S.C. 1014(b)(6), the surviving spouse's half of community property also receives a new cost basis at the other spouse's death, so both halves of a long-held stock portfolio or home can be sold with little capital gain. In common-law states, jointly owned property between spouses is treated as half owned by each (26 U.S.C. 2040(b)), so only the deceased spouse's half gets a new basis. INV-090 covers step-up in basis; INV-098 applies it to Ruth.

The biggest asset after the house

Splitting a 401(k) versus an IRA

For many couples, retirement accounts are worth more than anything except the home. They can be divided at divorce without tax — but the paperwork differs by account type, and getting it wrong can trigger tax and a 10% penalty.

Qualified domestic relations order (QDRO)
What it is
A court order, accepted by a retirement plan, that directs the plan to pay part of a participant's benefit to an alternate payee: a spouse, former spouse, child or other dependent (IRS; 26 U.S.C. 414(p)). Plans governed by federal law cannot otherwise pay benefits to anyone but the participant.
Used for
Dividing 401(k)s, 403(b)s, pensions and similar employer plans at divorce, or paying child support or alimony from them.
What it means for you
A spouse or former spouse who receives money under a QDRO pays tax as if they were the participant, can roll it tax-free into their own IRA, and — unusually — owes no 10% early-withdrawal penalty on money taken in cash directly from the plan, at any age (26 U.S.C. 72(t)(2)(C)).
Transfer incident to divorcefor IRAs
What it is
Moving all or part of an IRA to a spouse or former spouse under a divorce or separation instrument. It is not a taxable event; from the date of transfer, the money is treated as the receiving spouse's own IRA (26 U.S.C. 408(d)(6); IRS Publication 504). No QDRO is needed.
Used for
Dividing traditional and Roth IRAs. It is usually done as a direct trustee-to-trustee transfer, with the decree attached.
What it means for you
If the account owner instead withdraws the money and hands it over, the owner is taxed on the withdrawal (and may owe the 10% penalty) — an expensive mistake.

A dollar is not a dollar: divide after tax

Transfers of property between spouses, and between former spouses when related to the divorce, create no taxable gain or loss, and the receiver takes over the giver's cost basis (26 U.S.C. 1041). So assets that look equal on paper can be worth very different amounts once taxes are due. Figure 95.6 compares $100,000 in four forms for someone in the 22% bracket paying 15% on long-term capital gains.

Practitioner depthPensions, loans and timing

A QDRO for a defined benefit pension must describe the amount or percentage of each payment, or the method for working it out, and the number of payments or period covered (26 U.S.C. 414(p)). It can also give a former spouse survivor rights. Plans have their own review procedures and often publish model orders; drafting the QDRO at the same time as the decree, rather than afterward, avoids the risk that the participant dies, retires or cashes out first. Outstanding 401(k) loans reduce the balance available to divide, and the payout from a QDRO to a child is taxed to the participant, not the child (IRS, Retirement topics: QDRO).

After the decree

Alimony, child support and Social Security for divorced spouses

Alimonyalso called spousal support or separate maintenance
What it is
Payments to a spouse or former spouse under a divorce or separation instrument.
Used for
Supporting a lower-earning spouse for a period after divorce. State law and the court, or the couple's agreement, set the amount and length.
What it means for you
For agreements executed after December 31, 2018, alimony is not deductible by the payer and not taxable to the recipient. Agreements executed earlier keep the old treatment (deductible and taxable) unless they are modified and the modification expressly adopts the new rule (IRS Topic 452; IRS Publication 504).

Child support was never part of that change: it is not deductible by the payer and not taxable to the recipient, under any agreement. If a payer pays less than the total required for alimony and child support together, the IRS applies the payments to child support first (IRS Publication 504).

The 2018 change moved the tax on alimony from the recipient to the payer. Before it, a higher-earning payer in the 32% bracket could deduct payments that the recipient then reported in, say, the 12% bracket, so the couple as a unit saved tax and often shared the saving through a larger payment. Now the payer's after-tax dollars fund the support, which tends to shrink the amount a payer can afford.

Social Security on an ex-spouse's record

Divorced spouse's benefit
What it is
A monthly Social Security benefit based on a former spouse's work record. It is a version of the spousal benefit and pays up to half of the former spouse's full-retirement-age benefit.
Used for
Giving a lower-earning former spouse — often someone who spent years out of the paid workforce — a benefit tied to the marriage.
What it means for you
It does not reduce your former spouse's benefit or the benefits of their current family; payments to ex-spouses do not count toward the family maximum (Social Security Administration, or SSA).

The conditions, from SSA's operating manual (POMS RS 00202.005): you were married at least 10 years before the divorce became final; you are at least 62; you are not married; your own benefit is less than half of your former spouse's full-retirement-age benefit; and your former spouse is receiving benefits — or, if they are at least 62 and have not claimed, you have been divorced for at least two continuous years. SSA pays the higher of your own benefit or the divorced-spouse benefit, not both added together. Claimed before your own full retirement age, the spousal amount is reduced.

Divorced spouse's benefit at your full retirement age = up to 50% × former spouse's full-retirement-age benefit

A former spouse married 10 years can also receive survivor benefits when the ex dies — up to 100% of the ex's benefit at the survivor's full retirement age — and SSA says remarrying at 60 or later does not bar survivor benefits. INV-098 covers survivor benefits in detail.

Real law, real consequences

Three cases every couple should know

Case study 1Kennedy v. Plan Administrator for DuPont (2009)
What happened
William Kennedy named his wife, Liv, as beneficiary of his employer savings plan. Their divorce decree divested Liv of her interest in the plan, but William never filed a new beneficiary form. When he died, the plan paid Liv, following the form. His daughter, as executor, sued. The U.S. Supreme Court ruled unanimously that the plan administrator did its duty under federal law by paying according to the plan documents.
What it teaches
A divorce decree does not change a beneficiary form. For employer plans, the form on file wins. File new beneficiary designations the day the divorce is final.
Source
Kennedy v. Plan Administrator for DuPont Savings and Investment Plan, 555 U.S. 285 (2009), syllabus.
Case study 2Egelhoff v. Egelhoff (2001)
What happened
David Egelhoff named his wife as beneficiary of employer life insurance and a pension. Shortly after they divorced, he died without a will. Washington State had a law that automatically revoked a former spouse's beneficiary designation on divorce. The Supreme Court held that federal law (the Employee Retirement Income Security Act, or ERISA) overrides that state law for employer plans, and the benefits went to the former wife rather than his children.
What it teaches
Many states have "revocation on divorce" laws, but they cannot be relied on for 401(k)s, pensions and employer life insurance. The only reliable fix is a new form.
Source
Egelhoff v. Egelhoff, 532 U.S. 141 (2001), syllabus.
Case study 3The 2017 tax law moves the tax on alimony
What happened
Public Law 115-97, section 11051, repealed the rules that had made alimony deductible to the payer and taxable to the recipient, for divorce and separation instruments executed after December 31, 2018. The 2025 tax law (P.L. 119-21) left that change in place.
What it teaches
Tax rules can change the economics of a settlement overnight. The date an agreement is signed, and whether a later modification opts into the new rule, determines the tax on every payment for years.
Source
26 U.S.C. 71 (repealed by Pub. L. 115-97, sec. 11051); IRS Topic 452, Alimony and separate maintenance (2026).

Decisions

Three households, three marriage decisions

Scenario 1 · Maya

Suppose Maya and her partner, who also earns about $62,000, plan to marry next year. She still has $28,000 of student loans at 6.8%; her partner has none and $15,000 saved. How should they set up their money?

Merge and pay offIt removes a 6.8% debt, which is a good return, but it spends the partner's whole cushion and settles a question they have not discussed. If they choose it, they should do it knowingly and keep an emergency fund (INV-019).
Yours, mine, oursA common, workable structure. With equal incomes, 2026 tax is the same married or single ($10,520 either way), so tax need not drive the timing. They should update beneficiaries and discuss how the loan will be repaid.
Never discuss itSeparate accounts are fine; silence is not. Money is one of the subjects couples most often disagree about, and the law will treat much of what they earn as shared regardless.
The principle: agree on the structure out loud; the account layout matters less than both people understanding it.
Scenario 2 · Jordan

Suppose Jordan wants to marry a partner who also earns $88,000, the year before Ava files her first financial aid application. What should Jordan weigh?

Personal onlyIt is personal, and the finances should not decide it. But going in blind can surprise a family with a smaller aid offer right when Ava starts college.
Know the costsRight. Federal need analysis includes a stepparent's income if the parent and stepparent are married as of the application date (20 U.S.C. 1087oo(f)(4)). Knowing this, Jordan can plan savings, compare colleges' net prices, and decide with open eyes (INV-096).
The principle: do not let tax or aid rules make the decision — but do the arithmetic before, not after.
Scenario 3 · Ruth

Suppose Ruth, 68, wants to marry a widower she met at church. He has his own Social Security of $30,000 a year and $20,000 of other income. Anna worries about her inheritance. What should Ruth consider?

Nothing changesMuch changes. With Ruth's $34,800 of Social Security and an assumed $38,400 of IRA withdrawals and interest, their combined 2026 federal tax rises about $1,960 if they marry, largely because the Social Security taxation thresholds for couples are not twice the single ones. And without planning, her new spouse could gain rights to her estate.
Plan it properlyRight. A prenup can keep each spouse's assets for their own children. Pennsylvania is not a community property state, so her IRA stays hers, but her will, beneficiary forms and power of attorney all need review.
Avoid marriage for the benefitUnnecessary. SSA says remarrying at 60 or later does not end survivor benefits, so Ruth would keep her $2,900 a month.
The principle: in a later-life marriage, protect both families on paper before the wedding.

Six things people say about marriage, divorce and money

Apply it

What marriage and divorce rules mean for each household

Structure first, paperwork second. If Maya marries, the tax effect at equal incomes is zero. The work is the conversation about her loans, a yours-mine-ours structure, and a new 401(k) beneficiary form, which her spouse would otherwise inherit by default.
Arizona makes it "ours." In a community property state, both 401(k)s are shared property no matter whose name is on them. Their $650 joint-return bonus is small; their bigger task is naming each other and a guardian for Sofia and Leo in their wills (INV-084).
A remarriage would cost Jordan real money. Losing head-of-household status and adding a stepparent's income to Ava's FAFSA are known costs worth planning for, not reasons to avoid a relationship.
Protect the survivor. The Harpers' $820,000 of 401(k) money would go to each other by default. If either had children from an earlier marriage or wanted a trust, the other spouse would need to sign a written consent.
Remarriage is possible without losing Walter's benefit. At 68 Ruth keeps her survivor benefit if she remarries, but a joint return would cost about $1,960 more, and a prenup protects Anna's inheritance.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

Marcus earns $85,000 and Elena $60,000. Using the 2026 brackets and standard deductions in Tab 3, how large is their marriage bonus, in dollars?

Marcus as single: $85,000 − $16,100 = $68,900 taxable; $5,800 + 22% × ($68,900 − $50,400) = $9,870. Elena as single: $60,000 − $16,100 = $43,900; $1,240 + 12% × ($43,900 − $12,400) = $5,020. Total $14,890. Joint: $145,000 − $32,200 = $112,800; $11,600 + 22% × ($112,800 − $100,800) = $14,240. Bonus = $14,890 − $14,240 = $650: $6,500 of Marcus's income moves from the 22% bracket into the 12% bracket that Elena did not fill.
Exercise 2

Under a QDRO, a 45-year-old former spouse takes $20,000 in cash directly from the 401(k) and is in the 22% bracket. Ignoring state tax, how much does she keep, in dollars?

Distributions to an alternate payee under a QDRO are exempt from the 10% additional tax (26 U.S.C. 72(t)(2)(C)). Tax is 22% × $20,000 = $4,400, so she keeps $15,600. From an IRA the 10% would also apply: $20,000 − $4,400 − $2,000 = $13,600.
Exercise 3

A woman was married 14 years, is now unmarried and at her full retirement age. Her former husband's full-retirement-age benefit is $2,400 a month; her own is $900. What is the most Social Security will pay her each month, in dollars?

The divorced-spouse benefit at her full retirement age is up to 50% × $2,400 = $1,200. SSA pays the higher of her own benefit ($900) or the spousal amount, not both, so the most she receives is $1,200. Her former husband's benefit is unchanged.
Exercise 4

A settlement offers one spouse $100,000 of a pre-tax 401(k). If that spouse expects to withdraw it in the 24% bracket, what is its approximate after-tax value, in dollars?

$100,000 × (1 − 0.24) = $76,000. Offered instead $100,000 of cash, the same spouse would be $24,000 better off. Divide assets by their after-tax value, not their statement value.
