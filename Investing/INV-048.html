Factor Investing

A "factor" is a trait shared by many stocks — being cheap, small, profitable, recently rising or unusually calm — that has been linked to higher average returns. This module starts from the simplest model of risk and return, shows where it failed, explains the factors that researchers found instead, and measures, year by year since 1927, how long each has gone without paying.

The question this module answersAre there rewarded risks beyond "the market," and is it worth tilting my portfolio toward them?

Start here

From "one risk" to several

INV-003 made a simple claim: to expect a higher return, you must accept more risk. That is still true. The question this module asks is, which risks? Not every risk is rewarded — owning one company instead of a hundred adds risk with no extra expected return (INV-004).

The first formal answer, from the 1960s, was that only one kind of risk earns a reward: exposure to the stock market as a whole. Decades of data then showed that some groups of stocks earned more (or less) than that theory predicted. Researchers named these groups "factors," and deliberately tilting a portfolio toward them is called factor investing. Figure 48.1 shows the four best known over nearly a century. Each line compounds a long-short portfolio: for example, the value line is value stocks minus growth stocks.

The first model

The capital asset pricing model

In 1964 William Sharpe published a model of how assets should be priced if investors hold diversified portfolios. Its logic is elegant: company-specific risk can be diversified away for free, so the market should not pay for it; only the risk you cannot diversify, the tendency to move with the whole market, should earn a premium.

Beta
What it is
A measure of how much an investment tends to move when the market moves. A beta of 1 moves with the market; 1.5 tends to rise or fall 50% more; 0.5 about half as much.
Used for
Estimating market risk and, in the CAPM, the return an investment should be expected to earn.
What it means for you
A high-beta fund is a more aggressive way to own the market, not a different asset. Lowering the overall beta of your portfolio is mostly done by holding bonds, not by picking "defensive" stocks.
Capital asset pricing model (CAPM)
What it is
The theory that an asset's expected return equals the risk-free rate plus its beta times the market's expected premium over the risk-free rate.
Used for
Estimating required returns (INV-006), and as the benchmark against which "alpha" is measured (INV-043).
What it means for you
It explains why diversification is free and market risk is paid. It does not fully explain which stocks earned more.
Expected return = risk-free rate + β × (expected market return − risk-free rate)

Example: with Treasury bills at 4%, an expected market premium of 5% and a beta of 1.2, the CAPM expects 4 + 1.2 × 5 = 10%. The testable prediction is that higher-beta portfolios should earn proportionally more. Figure 48.2 tests it.

Eugene Fama and Kenneth French (1992) reported the same pattern across the cross-section of stocks: once size is allowed for, "the relation between market β and average return is flat." The CAPM's central idea — diversify away what is not rewarded — survives. Its prediction that beta alone explains returns does not.

Three factors, then five

Market, size, value — then profitability and investment

Fama and French (1993) proposed that three factors explain most differences in average stock returns: the market, size (small companies minus big ones, SMB) and value (high book-to-market minus low, HML). In 2015 they added profitability (robust minus weak, RMW) and investment (companies that invest conservatively minus those that invest aggressively, CMA).

Size premium
What it is
The extra average return of small companies over large ones. Kenneth French's SMB factor is the average return on small-stock portfolios minus that on big-stock portfolios, using the NYSE median market value as the dividing line.
Used for
Explaining why small-company funds behave differently from large-company funds, and designing small-cap tilts.
What it means for you
Since 1982 the US size premium has been close to zero on average. Small stocks still diversify a large-company portfolio, but a reliable premium is doubtful (Case studies).
Profitability and investment factors
What it is
RMW: the return of companies with robust operating profitability minus those with weak profitability. CMA: companies that invest conservatively minus those that invest aggressively.
Used for
Completing the five-factor model; profitability is the measurable core of "quality" investing (INV-046).
What it means for you
Many "value" and "quality" funds now combine several of these traits. Know which ones a fund targets before you buy it.
Excess return of a portfolio ≈ a + b(market) + s(SMB) + h(HML) + r(RMW) + c(CMA)

The letters b, s, h, r and c are the portfolio's loadings: how strongly it behaves like each factor. A small-value fund might have s = 0.8 and h = 0.6; an S&P 500 fund has loadings near zero on everything except the market. The intercept "a" is alpha: what the factors cannot explain.

Practitioner depthHow the factors are built

Each June, French sorts NYSE, AMEX and NASDAQ stocks into two size groups at the NYSE median and three groups by book-to-market (30th and 70th NYSE percentiles), giving six value-weighted portfolios. HML is the average of the two value portfolios minus the average of the two growth portfolios; SMB is the average of the three small minus the three big. RMW and CMA use the same size split with sorts on operating profitability and on investment. Momentum uses prior returns from 12 months to 2 months before formation and is rebalanced monthly. The market factor is the value-weighted return of all these stocks minus the one-month Treasury bill.

Winners keep winning, for a while

Momentum and the Carhart four-factor model

Momentum
What it is
The tendency of stocks that have risen most over the past 3 to 12 months to keep outperforming, and of recent losers to keep lagging, for several more months.
Used for
Momentum strategies and funds; and, in the Carhart model, separating a fund manager's skill from simply riding recent winners.
What it means for you
Momentum has had the highest average premium of the major factors and the most violent crashes. It also requires frequent trading, which raises costs and taxes.

Narasimhan Jegadeesh and Sheridan Titman (1993) documented that buying past winners and selling past losers generated significant returns over 3- to 12-month holding periods, and that part of the gain reversed over the following two years. It is one of the hardest anomalies to reconcile with market efficiency, because it uses only past prices — the weak form of INV-043. Mark Carhart (1997) added momentum to the Fama–French three factors to evaluate mutual funds, and found that the apparent "hot hands" of top funds were mostly the one-year momentum effect.

Going furtherWhy momentum crashes

After a long bear market, the "loser" side of a momentum portfolio is full of the most beaten-down, riskiest stocks. When the market turns up sharply, those stocks rebound hardest. A strategy that is short them loses heavily. That is what happened in 1932 (−20.5%) and above all in 2009. The pattern means momentum's risk is concentrated in exactly the moments when investors most want relief.

The anomaly that contradicts the textbook

Calmer stocks, similar or better returns

Low-volatility anomaly
What it is
The finding that stocks with low past volatility (or low beta) have earned about as much as, or more than, high-volatility stocks, with far less risk — the opposite of what the CAPM predicts.
Used for
Minimum-volatility and low-beta funds, often marketed to cautious investors.
What it means for you
Low-volatility stock funds are still stock funds: they fall in crashes, just usually less. They also tilt toward particular industries and can lag badly in strong rallies.

Notice where the anomaly lives. The four calmer quintiles line up roughly as theory predicts — a bit more return for more risk — and then the most volatile fifth falls off a cliff. Its average yearly return (10.7%) was not far below the others; its compound return was, because large swings drag down compounded growth (volatility drag, INV-003). The anomaly is largely about avoiding lottery-like stocks.

Two explanations are well supported. Andrea Frazzini and Lasse Pedersen (2014) argue that many investors cannot or will not borrow, so to seek higher returns they buy high-beta assets instead of leveraging safe ones, bidding them up; a "betting against beta" factor that is long leveraged low-beta assets and short high-beta assets earned significant risk-adjusted returns across many markets. Malcolm Baker, Brendan Bradley and Jeffrey Wurgler (2011) point to benchmarks: managers judged against an index are reluctant to hold low-beta stocks that will lag in rising markets, which limits arbitrage.

From paper to portfolio

What factor funds deliver, and what they cost

The factor returns in this module are long-short, before costs, rebalanced as often as needed, and include the smallest stocks. A real fund is usually long-only, holds larger and more liquid stocks, trades less often and charges a fee. It therefore captures only part of the paper premium. The SEC's guidance on "smart beta" and other non-traditional index funds puts the trade-offs plainly: these funds "may behave very differently than the market," "will not necessarily outperform the market," may have limited histories, and "typically have higher expenses than traditional index funds."

Factor investing
What it is
Deliberately tilting a portfolio toward stocks with traits linked to higher long-run returns, usually through rules-based ("smart beta") index funds.
Used for
Seeking a return above the market's, or a different risk profile, at lower cost than traditional active management.
What it means for you
It is an active bet in index-fund clothing: you accept tracking error against the market, possibly for decades, for an uncertain premium.
Expected gain from a tilt ≈ loading × expected factor premium − (fund cost − index-fund cost) − extra trading and tax costs

Suppose a value fund has a value loading of 0.5, you expect the value premium to be 3% a year from here, and the fund costs 0.12 points more than a total-market fund. The expected gain is about 0.5 × 3 − 0.12 = 1.38 points a year — if the premium appears. If you expect only 1%, the gain is 0.38 points, with years of tracking error in exchange.

Going furtherThe "factor zoo" and why to be selective

Researchers have published hundreds of candidate factors. Campbell Harvey, Yan Liu and Heqing Zhu (2016) argued that, after so many tests on the same data, a new factor should clear a t-statistic above 3.0, and that many published findings are likely false. R. David McLean and Jeffrey Pontiff (2016) found published predictors lost about 58% of their returns after publication. The factors in this module are the ones with the longest out-of-sample record and the most plausible explanations. A fund built on a factor you have never heard of, backed only by a backtest, deserves great skepticism.

The real cost of factor investing

How long can a premium hide?

Every factor in this module has a positive long-run average. Every one has also spent years — sometimes decades — below its previous high. For an investor, those droughts are the real price of a factor tilt: not the fee, but the discipline of holding a strategy that looks wrong, year after year, while a simple index fund does better.

Ask a concrete question before tilting: if my value or small-cap fund trailed a total-market fund by two points a year for 15 years, would I still hold it? Most investors, including professionals, would not. That is one reason premiums can persist: they are genuinely hard to collect.

⚠️

Reading the tables fairly. Long-short factor returns are more extreme than anything a long-only fund would deliver; a fund with half the exposure would have had roughly half the premium and half the swings, plus its costs. The lesson — that droughts are long and common — carries over.

Real history

Three episodes

Case study 1The size premium after its discovery
What happened
Rolf Banz published evidence in 1981 that small companies had earned more than large ones. In French's data the size factor averaged 4.7% a year from 1927 to 1981 — and −0.2% a year from 1982 to 2025. Compounded, it lost 27.5% over those 44 years.
What it teaches
A premium measured before it was widely known may shrink once investors act on it, as McLean and Pontiff found for published predictors in general. Small stocks remain a legitimate part of a total-market portfolio; a large bet on the size premium alone is hard to justify from the US record since 1982.
Source
Banz, R. W. (1981). The relationship between return and market value of common stocks. Journal of Financial Economics, 9(1), 3–18; Kenneth R. French Data Library, SMB factor.
Case study 2The momentum crash of 2009
What happened
Momentum had averaged 10.8% a year from 1927 to 2008. In 2009, as the market rebounded from the financial crisis and the most battered stocks soared, the momentum factor lost 83.5% in a single year. At the end of 2025 its compounded value was still less than a quarter of its 2008 peak.
What it teaches
A high average premium can hide a crash risk that arrives all at once. Any fund that captures momentum carries some of that crash risk.
Source
Kenneth R. French Data Library, momentum factor (Mom), annual returns.
Case study 3Low volatility in the technology boom, 1999
What happened
In 1999 the calmest fifth of US stocks returned 1.1% while the most volatile fifth returned 92.8%. The next year the positions reversed: calm stocks +11.6%, volatile stocks −37.1%.
What it teaches
Low-volatility strategies are designed to lag in euphoric rallies, and their investors are tested exactly when everyone else is getting rich. The long-run advantage came with at least one year that felt unbearable.
Source
Kenneth R. French Data Library, Portfolios Formed on Variance, value-weighted quintiles.

Decisions

To tilt or not to tilt

Scenario 1 · Jordan

Jordan watches a video arguing that a young investor should put 30% of her stocks in a small-cap value fund to "capture the factor premiums." Her 401(k) offers one at 0.30%, and a total-market index fund at 0.02%. What should she weigh?

Proven premiumsThe long-run averages are real in the data, but "proven" overstates it: the US size premium has been near zero since 1982 and value lagged from 2007 to 2020.
Informed, modest tiltRight. A tilt can be rational for someone with a 40-year horizon and the temperament to hold it. The 0.28-point extra cost on 30% of her stocks is about 0.08 points on the whole portfolio.
All inMaximum exposure means maximum tracking error. Few investors hold a 100% factor bet through a 15-year drought.
The principle: factor tilts are optional; the market is the default.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers

The Harpers' advisor proposes replacing their index funds with five factor ETFs "to add return and reduce risk." The ETFs cost 0.15%–0.25%, on top of his 1.25% fee. How should they evaluate it?

Accept on reputationThe research is real; the promise of both higher return and lower risk from the same tilt is not. Low-volatility funds aim at lower risk; value and size aim at higher return with more risk.
Ask for numbersRight. Five overlapping factor funds may largely offset each other, leaving market exposure at a higher cost. In a taxable account, selling index funds to buy them can realize gains.
The principle: every tilt needs a stated reason, a cost, and a plan for the years it lags.

Six claims about factors

Apply it

Factors and each household

A candidate for a small tilt, if she wants one. Jordan has the longest horizon and the least money at stake. A modest value or small-value tilt is defensible; skipping it is too.
Know what their funds load on. Their growth fund loads negatively on value; their value and dividend funds positively. Together they roughly cancel; one market fund does the same.
Gaps first, tilts later. In Figure 48.10 a tilt adds 0.38 to 2.38 points a year, depending on a future premium no one can know, and the premium could be zero. Monica's bigger, surer gains are elsewhere: topping up her emergency fund by $4,200, using the $8,000 catch-up contribution open to her at 50 and over in 2026, and setting up the special-needs plan for Eli. A tilt can wait, or never happen.
Tilt or tax? Any factor strategy in their taxable account must beat its own tax and switching costs. Their 401(k)s are the cheaper place to implement a tilt.
Low volatility is not a bond. A minimum-volatility fund might suit part of Ruth's stock holdings, but it does not replace the bonds and cash that fund her near-term spending.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

Treasury bills yield 4%, the expected market premium is 5%, and a stock's beta is 1.2. What return does the CAPM expect, in percent?

4 + 1.2 × 5 = 4 + 6 = 10%.
Exercise 2

A fund has a market loading of 1.0, a size loading of 0.3 and a value loading of 0.4. With a 4% risk-free rate and expected premiums of 5% (market), 2% (size) and 3% (value), what is its expected return, in percent?

4 + 1.0 × 5 + 0.3 × 2 + 0.4 × 3 = 4 + 5 + 0.6 + 1.2 = 10.8%.
Exercise 3

A factor fund has a 0.5 loading on a factor you expect to pay 3% a year, and costs 0.15 points more than a total-market fund. What net gain per year do you expect, in percentage points?

0.5 × 3 = 1.5 points of expected premium, less 0.15 of extra cost = 1.35 points, before extra trading and taxes.
Exercise 4

A strategy loses 83.5% in one year. What percentage gain would it need to recover to where it started?

After the loss 0.165 remains. 1 ÷ 0.165 = 6.06, so it needs a gain of about 506%.
