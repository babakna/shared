Real Estate Taxes

A rental property is taxed differently from almost anything else you can own. You deduct part of the building's cost every year even while it rises in value, you may not be allowed to use the losses that creates, and when you sell, part of that deduction comes back. This module walks through the whole life cycle with 2026 rules, including what the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21) changed and what it left alone.

The question this module answersWhat will taxes actually do to a rental property's return, from the year I buy it to the year I sell it?

Start here

You already know the ordinary case

If you own a home you live in, the tax story is short: you may deduct mortgage interest and property tax if you itemize, and when you sell, most people owe nothing on the gain. For most households that is the whole of real estate taxation, and it is a generous deal.

A rental property is different in three ways, and each one surprises new landlords. First, the tax code treats the building as if it were wearing out, so you deduct a slice of its cost every year — called depreciation — even when the property is gaining value. Second, the losses those deductions create often cannot be used against your salary. Third, when you sell, the depreciation you took is taxed back at a special rate, unless you roll the gain into another property through an exchange or hold it until death. Concept 1

Deducting a building that is not wearing out

The tax code assumes buildings wear out, so it lets owners recover their cost through yearly deductions. Land does not wear out, so it is never depreciated. The deduction is a real reduction in taxable income, even though no cash leaves your pocket that year. That is why a rental can show a tax loss while putting money in your bank account.

Depreciation (for rental real estate)IRS Publication 527
What it is
A yearly deduction for part of the cost of a building and its improvements, spread over a recovery period set by law: 27.5 years for residential rental property and 39 years for nonresidential real property such as offices and stores. Both use the straight-line method: the same amount every full year.
Used for
Reducing the taxable income from the rental each year, and tracking your adjusted basis — what you paid, plus improvements, minus depreciation — which sets the gain when you sell.
What it means for you
On a $320,000 building, depreciation is about $11,636 a year. In the 24% bracket, that is roughly $2,793 less tax a year if you can use it. It is not optional in effect: the IRS reduces your basis by the depreciation "allowed or allowable," so skipping it still costs you at sale.
Annual depreciation = (price + buying costs − land value) ÷ 27.5
First-year depreciation = annual amount × (12.5 − month placed in service) ÷ 12

Two details decide the number. The first is the land split. You must divide the purchase price between land and building in proportion to their fair market values; if you are unsure, the IRS allows you to use the ratio in the local property-tax assessment (Pub. 527 gives the example of a $200,000 purchase with a $136,000 house and $24,000 land assessment, an 85/15 split). The second is the mid-month convention: residential rental property is treated as placed in service in the middle of the month, so a property first available for rent in July gets 5.5 months of depreciation in year one — 1.667% of the building's basis, the figure in the IRS table.

Going furtherRepairs versus improvements

A repair that keeps the property in ordinary working condition — fixing a leak, repainting a room — is deducted in full the year you pay it. An improvement that makes the property better, restores it, or adapts it to a new use — a new roof, a kitchen remodel, a new heating system — is added to basis and depreciated, usually over 27.5 years, starting when it is placed in service. Pub. 527's Table 1-1 lists examples. The distinction matters in the year of the expense, not over the property's life.

Practitioner depthConverting your home to a rental

When a former residence becomes a rental, its basis for depreciation is the lower of its adjusted basis or its fair market value on the conversion date. That rule stops owners from depreciating a decline in value that happened while they lived there. Pub. 527's example converts a house with a $147,000 depreciable basis on August 1: the first-year percentage is 1.364%, a $2,005 deduction.

Concept 2

When you sell, depreciation comes back

Every dollar of depreciation lowered your basis by a dollar, so it raises your gain on sale by a dollar. The tax code taxes that slice of the gain at its own rate. For a residential building depreciated on a straight line, the slice is called unrecaptured section 1250 gain, and it is taxed at your ordinary income rate but no more than 25% (IRS Topic 409). The rest of the gain — the part from the price actually rising — is ordinary long-term capital gain, taxed at 0%, 15% or 20%.

Unrecaptured section 1250 gainthe "25% gain"
What it is
The part of the long-term gain on selling real property that is due to depreciation. It is the smaller of the depreciation you took and your total gain.
Used for
Taxing back the benefit of depreciation at up to 25%, instead of the lower capital gains rates. For 2026, the 15% capital gains rate applies to married couples filing jointly with taxable income above $98,900 and up to $613,700 (Rev. Proc. 2025-32).
What it means for you
If your bracket is 24% when you depreciate and 25% applies when you sell, depreciation was mainly a timing benefit: you save tax now and repay most of it later, having had the use of the money in between.

With the defaults — a $400,000 rental with 20% land, placed in service in July, sold in December of year 10 for $560,000 with 6% selling costs — depreciation totals $109,576, adjusted basis is $290,424, and the gain is $235,976. Of that, $109,576 is unrecaptured §1250 gain taxed at 24% ($26,298) and $126,400 is capital gain at 15% ($18,960). If the 3.8% net investment income tax (NIIT) applies ($8,967), total federal tax is $54,225 and the seller keeps $472,175 of the $526,400 realized, before paying off any mortgage.

Going furtherWhy NIIT often applies to landlords

The 3.8% net investment income tax applies to the smaller of net investment income or the amount by which modified adjusted gross income exceeds $250,000 (married filing jointly), $200,000 (single or head of household) or $125,000 (married filing separately). Net investment income includes rental income and gains. The thresholds are fixed dollar amounts in the law, and a large gain in one year can push an otherwise modest income over them.

Practitioner depthSection 1245 property is recaptured differently

Appliances, carpets and other personal property in a rental are §1245 property. Gain on them is ordinary income to the extent of depreciation allowed or allowable, at your full rate with no 25% cap (Pub. 544). This matters most after a cost segregation study, which moves part of the purchase price into §1245 classes.

Concept 3

A loss on paper is not always a deduction

The passive activity rules exist to stop investors from using paper losses on property to shelter their salaries, and for most people they do. A rental is passive by default, even if you do the work yourself, and a loss from a passive activity can offset only passive income. The unused loss is not forfeited; it is suspended and carried forward.

The special $25,000 allowanceIRS Publication 925
What it is
An exception that lets an individual who actively participates in a rental deduct up to $25,000 of rental losses against wages and other income. It shrinks by 50 cents for every dollar of modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) above $100,000 and is gone at $150,000. For married people filing separately who lived apart all year, the figures are $12,500, $50,000 and $75,000; those who lived together get none.
Used for
Letting ordinary landlords with moderate incomes use rental losses. Active participation is a low bar: approving tenants, setting rents and approving repairs count, as long as you (with your spouse) own at least 10% of the property. Limited partners generally do not qualify.
What it means for you
At a household MAGI of $145,000, the allowance is $25,000 − 50% × $45,000 = $2,500. Above $150,000 it is zero: a large tax loss from depreciation simply waits. These dollar amounts are fixed in the statute; they are not among the inflation adjustments the IRS published for 2026 in Rev. Proc. 2025-32.

Real estate professional status

Real estate professional status removes the passive label from rentals in which you materially participate. You qualify for a year only if both are true: more than half of all the personal services you performed in all trades or businesses were in real property trades or businesses in which you materially participated, and you performed more than 750 hours in them. Hours as an employee do not count unless you own more than 5% of the employer. On a joint return, one spouse must meet both tests alone. A full-time engineer or nurse cannot qualify on the side, because their job alone makes up more than half their working hours.

Going furtherThe seven material participation tests

Material participation is a higher bar than active participation. Pub. 925 lists seven tests; meeting any one is enough. The most used: more than 500 hours in the activity; your participation was substantially all of anyone's; or more than 100 hours and at least as much as any other individual, including a cleaner or manager. Keep a contemporaneous log of dates, hours and tasks. Hours spent reviewing statements as an investor generally do not count.

Practitioner depthWhen suspended losses come back

Suspended losses carry forward without limit. They are allowed in full in the year you dispose of your entire interest in the activity in a fully taxable transaction to an unrelated person (Pub. 925). A 1031 exchange is not a fully taxable disposition, so losses stay suspended and follow you. Gifts and deaths have their own rules.

Concept 4

Swapping instead of selling

A 1031 exchange, named for its section of the Internal Revenue Code, lets you sell investment or business real property and buy other real property without paying tax on the gain now. The gain is not forgiven; it is carried into the new property through a lower basis. Since January 1, 2018, only real property qualifies; equipment, vehicles, art and other personal property no longer do (IRS news release IR-2018-227). U.S. and foreign real property are not like-kind to each other.

Qualified intermediary
What it is
An independent party who, under a written exchange agreement, holds the sale proceeds and acquires the replacement property for you. The agreement must limit your right to receive, pledge or borrow the money.
Used for
Avoiding "constructive receipt": if you can touch the cash, the sale is taxable. Your own agent, attorney or broker in the past two years is generally a disqualified person.
What it means for you
Arrange the intermediary before the sale closes. Cash or other non-like-kind property you receive — called boot — is taxable up to your gain, and so is debt relief you do not replace.
Going furtherExchanges and your home

A property can qualify for both a 1031 exchange and the home sale exclusion if it was your main home and later a rental. Pub. 523 says the §121 exclusion is applied first, then §1031 defers the rest, including the depreciation gain. In the other direction, if you acquired a home through a like-kind exchange, you cannot use the home sale exclusion on it until you have owned it for five years.

Concept 5

The home sale exclusion, and renting before you sell

The home sale exclusion in §121 lets you exclude up to $250,000 of gain on your main home, or $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly. You qualify if you owned the home and lived in it as your main residence for at least 24 months of the 5 years before the sale, and did not use the exclusion on another home in the previous 2 years. The 24 months need not be continuous. P.L. 119-21 did not amend §121, so these amounts are unchanged for 2026; they are not among the IRS's 2026 inflation adjustments.

Nonqualified use§121(b)(5)
What it is
Time after 2008 when neither you nor your spouse used the property as your main home. Gain allocated to that time cannot be excluded.
Used for
Stopping people from turning a long-held rental into a "home" for two years to exclude the whole gain.
What it means for you
Renting after you move out is not nonqualified use, as long as you sell within the 5-year window — in practice within about 3 years of moving out. Renting before you move in is. Either way, depreciation you took after May 6, 1997 is taxed; it is never excluded.

Pub. 523 also shows the reverse: "Finley" rented a property for 2 years, then lived in it for 2, and sold a year after moving out. Two of the five years were nonqualified use, so 40% of the gain (after setting aside $20,000 of depreciation) — $120,000 — was taxable, $180,000 was excluded, and the $20,000 of depreciation was taxed under §1250.

Going furtherA surviving spouse's larger exclusion

An unmarried surviving spouse can still use the $500,000 limit if the home is sold within 2 years of the spouse's death and the other tests are met (Pub. 523). After that window, the single $250,000 limit applies. Separately, a home owned with a spouse who died generally receives a new basis for the inherited share at death, which can shrink the gain itself (INV-090).

Concept 6

Pulling deductions forward, and the tax you pay every year

A cost segregation study is an engineering analysis that splits a building's cost into parts with shorter recovery periods: carpets, appliances and certain fixtures (5 or 7 years), and land improvements such as paving and fencing (15 years). Those parts can then be depreciated faster than the 27.5-year building.

Bonus depreciationthe special depreciation allowance, §168(k)
What it is
An extra first-year deduction for qualified property — tangible property with a recovery period of 20 years or less. P.L. 119-21 permanently restored the 100% allowance for property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025. Property acquired before January 20, 2025 remains under the old phase-down, which was 40% for property placed in service in 2025 (IRS Pub. 946).
Used for
Deducting the entire cost of the short-life parts found by a cost segregation study in the first year. The 27.5-year building itself does not qualify.
What it means for you
A large first-year deduction is worth the most to someone who can use it: a real estate professional, or an owner of a short-term rental who materially participates (INV-070). For most people with jobs and incomes above $150,000, it becomes a suspended passive loss that waits for future rental income or a sale.

In the default, a study moves 20% of a $320,000 building ($64,000) into 5-year property. With 100% bonus depreciation the first-year deduction rises from $5,333 to about $68,267, an extra $62,933 — worth about $15,104 of deferred tax in the 24% bracket, if the loss can be used. The total deducted over the building's life does not change; only the timing does, and the §1245 recapture at sale is at full ordinary rates.

Property tax basics

Property tax is set locally. In Colorado, for example, the state's Division of Property Taxation describes three pieces: the assessor sets the property's actual value, the legislature sets the assessment rate, and each taxing district sets a mill levy. A mill is one-tenth of a cent: $1 of tax for every $1,000 of assessed value. Other states use different names and exemptions, but the arithmetic is the same.

On a rental, property tax is a rental expense deducted on Schedule E (Pub. 527). On your own home, it is an itemized deduction subject to the state and local tax (SALT) cap, which P.L. 119-21 raised to $40,400 for 2026, reduced by 30% of MAGI above $505,000 but not below $10,000 (half these amounts if married filing separately). The higher cap is scheduled to rise 1% a year through 2029 and to fall back to $10,000 in 2030.

Real law, real numbers

Three changes and examples that shape the math

Case study 1The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act narrows like-kind exchanges, 2018
What happened
Starting January 1, 2018, exchanges of machinery, equipment, vehicles, artwork, collectibles, patents and other personal or intangible property no longer qualify under §1031. Only real property held for business or investment does. Property held primarily for sale, such as a developer's inventory, never qualified.
What it teaches
Real estate kept a privilege that most other assets lost, and that privilege exists only as long as Congress keeps it. Plans that depend on "exchange until you die" rest on current law.
Source
Internal Revenue Service (2018). Like-kind exchanges now limited to real property, IR-2018-227, November 19, 2018.
Case study 2P.L. 119-21 restores 100% bonus depreciation, 2025
What happened
Under the 2017 law, bonus depreciation had been phasing down, to 40% for property placed in service in 2025. Section 70301 of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025, made the 100% allowance permanent for qualified property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025, with an election to take 40% instead in the first tax year ending after that date.
What it teaches
The acquisition date matters: property is treated as acquired when a written binding contract is signed, so a property under contract before January 20, 2025 stays under the old phase-down even if it closes later. Cost segregation studies became more valuable again — but only for owners who can use the losses.
Source
Public Law 119-21, §70301 (2025); Internal Revenue Service (2026), Publication 946 (2025), How to depreciate property.
Case study 3The IRS's own home-to-rental examples
What happened
In Pub. 523, "Taylor" lived in a home for three years, rented it for 23 months and sold it for a $227,000 gain: $200,000 was excluded and $27,000 of depreciation was taxed. "Finley" did the opposite order — rental first, then home — and lost the exclusion on 40% of the non-depreciation gain.
What it teaches
The order of use matters more than the total time. Renting out a former home is compatible with the exclusion if you sell within the five-year window; turning a rental into a home is not a way to erase its gain.
Source
Internal Revenue Service (2026). Publication 523 (2025), Selling your home.

Decisions

Two households, two tax pitches

Scenario 1 · The Harpers

At a dinner party, Tom hears that buying a rental and ordering a cost segregation study could create a $70,000 first-year loss to "wipe out" some of their $260,000 of wages. What should the Harpers expect?

Deduct against wagesTheir MAGI is well above $150,000, so the $25,000 allowance is zero, and neither qualifies as a real estate professional while working full time. The loss would be suspended until they have passive income or sell.
Know the rule, then judge the dealRight. The short-term rental route is real but demands hours, records and a property that works as a business (INV-070). A tax deduction that only defers tax is never a reason to buy a mediocre property.
Avoid it entirelyComplexity is a cost, not a verdict. A good rental with suspended losses can still be a good investment; the losses are banked, not lost.
The principle: run the numbers before tax, then find out which tax box you are actually in.
Scenario 2 · The Riveras

The Riveras plan to move to a bigger house in Phoenix. Their current home is worth about $420,000 with a $310,000 mortgage at 3.1%. Should they sell it or keep it as a rental?

Keep indefinitelyPossible, and the cheap mortgage helps cash flow (INV-065, INV-066). But after the five-year window closes, the whole gain, including what built up while they lived there, becomes taxable on sale. That trade must be deliberate.
Sell nowSimple and tax-free up to $500,000 of gain, and it frees equity toward the new house and their thin emergency fund.
Rent with a deadlineA reasonable middle path: it keeps the exclusion for the gain so far, but the $41,000 in checking should first cover the reserves a landlord needs, and depreciation claimed while renting will be taxed at up to 25%.
The principle: the exclusion has a clock. Decide before it runs out, not after.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

You buy a rental for $340,000, including buying costs; $65,000 is land. What is the full-year depreciation, in dollars?

Building basis = $340,000 − $65,000 = $275,000. $275,000 ÷ 27.5 = $10,000 a year.
Exercise 2

A married couple filing jointly actively participates in a rental with an $18,000 loss. Their MAGI is $130,000 and they have no other passive income. How much of the loss can they deduct this year, in dollars?

Allowance = $25,000 − 0.5 × $30,000 = $10,000. The other $8,000 is suspended and carried forward.
Exercise 3

You sell a rental at a $150,000 gain after taking $60,000 of straight-line depreciation. You are in the 32% bracket. What is the federal tax on the depreciation part of the gain, ignoring the NIIT, in dollars?

Unrecaptured §1250 gain is the lesser of $60,000 and $150,000 = $60,000, taxed at your rate but no more than 25%: $60,000 × 0.25 = $15,000.
Exercise 4

A $320,000 residential building is placed in service in July. What is the first-year depreciation, to the nearest dollar?

Full year: $320,000 ÷ 27.5 = $11,636.36. Mid-month convention: July counts as 5.5 months, so $11,636.36 × 5.5 ÷ 12 = $5,333 (1.667% of basis).
