Index Investing and the Bogleheads

In 1976 a small new fund company launched a fund that did not try to pick winners; it simply bought the whole index. Critics called it "un-American." Fifty years later, index funds hold most of the money in US long-term funds. This module explains how they work, how to build a complete portfolio from three of them, and what the serious criticisms are.

The question this module answersIs owning the whole market, cheaply, good enough — and what are the catches?

Start here

Buying the haystack instead of looking for the needle

If you were told that a handful of stocks will produce most of the market's gains over the next 30 years, but not which ones, what would you do? You would buy all of them. That is an index fund.

INV-004 showed that most individual stocks lag Treasury bills over their lifetimes and a few huge winners create the market's wealth. INV-043 showed that about nine in ten professionally managed funds trail their index over 20 years, largely because of their costs. Put those two findings together and you get the case for indexing: own everything, pay almost nothing, and collect the market's return minus a sliver. Investors have moved accordingly (Figure 44.1).

Index fund
What it is
A mutual fund or ETF that tries to match the return of a market index, such as the S&P 500, by holding the securities in it (or a representative sample), rather than choosing which to buy.
Used for
Owning a whole market or segment cheaply, with little trading and minimal manager risk.
What it means for you
You give up any chance of beating the market in exchange for a near-certainty of not falling far behind it. Over long periods, that trade has beaten most alternatives.

From ridicule to majority

How indexing began

In 1975 John C. Bogle founded The Vanguard Group with an unusual structure: the company is owned by its funds, which are owned by the people who invest in them, so there are no outside shareholders to pay. In 1976 Vanguard launched the First Index Investment Trust, now the Vanguard 500 Index Fund, offering ordinary investors a low-cost fund that simply tracked the S&P 500. Vanguard's own history records that industry insiders ridiculed it as "un-American" and "a sure path to mediocrity." Bogle became known as the "father of indexing."

Bogleheads
What it is
The community of investors, named after Bogle, who follow his principles: spend less than you earn, invest early and regularly, diversify broadly, keep costs and taxes low, stay the course, and use index funds as the core.
Used for
A free forum and a large wiki where members answer each other's questions; the source of the three-fund portfolio's popularity.
What it means for you
You do not need to join anything to use the approach. Its value is that it is simple enough to follow for 40 years, including through crashes.
Going furtherWhy the ownership structure mattered

A fund company with outside shareholders has a reason to keep fees high: the fees are its profits. A company owned by its own funds can pass savings back as lower expense ratios. Competition then did the rest; other large firms now offer broad index funds at similar or even lower prices. For you, the lesson is to compare expense ratios for the same index across providers, because the product has become a commodity.

Inside the index

Capitalization weighting, explained with three companies

An index is a list of securities with a rule for how much of each to hold. Most stock indexes, including the S&P 500, weight each company by its market capitalization: its share price times the number of shares outstanding. The SEC's investor bulletin puts it simply: in a market-cap-weighted index, securities with higher market capitalization account for a greater share of the index. (A few indexes, such as the Dow Jones Industrial Average, are price-weighted instead.)

Capitalization weighting
What it is
Holding each company in proportion to its total market value. A company worth 5% of the index's total value gets 5% of every dollar you invest.
Used for
Building indexes that mirror the market itself. It is the only weighting every investor could hold at once, and it needs almost no trading: when prices move, the weights move with them automatically.
What it means for you
Your index fund owns the biggest companies most heavily. If a few giants dominate the market, they dominate your fund too.
Tracking error
What it is
How far a fund's returns differ from its index's. For index funds it comes from costs, cash held, sampling (holding only some of the securities) and trading around index changes. For active funds it measures how different the manager dares to be.
Used for
Judging how faithfully an index fund does its job, and how much a manager's results can wander from the benchmark.
What it means for you
For a broad index fund, expect a return a little below the index each year, by roughly the expense ratio. Much larger gaps are a warning sign; the expense ratio is the usual floor.
Practitioner depthIndex providers make choices

"Passive" does not mean "rule-free." The S&P 500 is chosen by a committee under published eligibility rules; other providers, such as CRSP, FTSE Russell and MSCI, use different size cutoffs, rebalancing dates and treatments of new listings. Those choices cause small but real differences between funds that all claim to track "US stocks." When a company is added to a popular index, index funds must buy it, which can move its price; academics call this the index effect. For a long-term investor the differences between major broad indexes are small (next tab), but they are why comparing two "total market" funds is not always apples to apples.

Which index?

Five hundred companies or several thousand?

The two most common US stock index funds track the S&P 500 or a "total market" index. S&P describes the S&P 500 as 500 leading companies covering approximately 80% of available US market capitalization. A total-market fund adds the remaining mid-sized and small companies. As of September 24, 2026, the iShares Core S&P 500 ETF held 504 securities and the iShares Core S&P Total U.S. Stock Market ETF held 2,460; both charged a 0.03% expense ratio.

Total stock market index fund
What it is
A fund that tracks an index of nearly all publicly traded US companies, large and small, weighted by market value.
Used for
Owning the entire US stock market in one fund, including the small and mid-sized companies the S&P 500 leaves out.
What it means for you
Because the largest companies dominate by value, a total-market fund behaves almost exactly like an S&P 500 fund. Either is a sound core holding.

In individual years the two differ more: by an average of 2.2 percentage points, and by more than 5 points in only five of the 98 years (1929, 1933, 1955, 1973 and 2020). Small companies have their own cycles, so a total-market fund will sometimes lead and sometimes trail. Over the last 20 years the whole market compounded at 11.07% a year and the S&P 500 at 10.90%.

💡

The practical rule. Use whichever broad, low-cost US index fund your 401(k) or brokerage offers. Do not hold both an S&P 500 fund and a total-market fund expecting diversification; about 80% of what they own is the same.

A complete portfolio in three funds

The Bogleheads' three-fund portfolio

A US stock index fund is not a whole portfolio: it has no bonds and no companies outside the United States. The most popular Boglehead answer, set out by Taylor Larimore in The Bogleheads' Guide to the Three-Fund Portfolio (Wiley, 2018), adds exactly those two missing pieces.

Three-fund portfolio
What it is
A total US stock market index fund, a total international stock index fund and a total US bond market index fund, held in proportions you choose. The publisher describes it as holding over 15,000 securities worldwide in three funds.
Used for
A complete, diversified, very low-cost portfolio that is easy to rebalance and to hold for decades.
What it means for you
Your only real decisions are how much in stocks versus bonds (INV-025, INV-049) and how much of your stocks outside the US (INV-054). Everything else is already decided by the market.

With the builder's default — 80% stocks, 30% of that international — the mix is 56% US stock, 24% international stock and 20% bonds, with a blended expense ratio of 0.035% (0.56 × 0.03 + 0.24 × 0.05 + 0.20 × 0.03). The same mix at the industry's 2025 asset-weighted averages for equity and bond mutual funds (0.40% and 0.36%) costs 0.392%. On $100,000 over 30 years at 6% before costs, the cheaper version ends about $57,900 ahead from costs alone.

Going furtherVariations and one-fund alternatives

Common variations add a small-value fund (INV-046, INV-048), inflation-protected Treasuries (TIPS, INV-010) or real estate. The simplest alternative is a single target-date or balanced index fund, which holds essentially the same three pieces and rebalances them for you (INV-050); the price is a slightly higher expense ratio and less control over the mix. In a workplace plan without good international or bond index options, investors often hold the missing pieces in an IRA instead, and look at the accounts together.

Practitioner depthWhere to hold each fund

Across several accounts, the three funds need not sit in each one. Bond funds, whose interest is taxed as ordinary income, are often placed in tax-deferred accounts; broad stock funds, which are relatively tax-efficient, in taxable accounts; and the international fund in a taxable account can allow the foreign tax credit. That is asset location (INV-036). The overall 80/20 or 60/40 mix is what matters; each account does not have to match it.

Where the edge comes from

Low costs are the whole point

Index funds do not have better stocks than active funds; by construction they have the stocks. Their advantage is what they do not spend, measured mostly by tracking error and cost: no research analysts, little trading, and in many cases very low expense ratios. The SEC's investor bulletin makes the same point and adds a caution: "not all index funds have lower costs than actively managed funds," so check before you buy.

Taxes are the second advantage in taxable accounts. A fund that rarely trades realizes few capital gains, so it distributes few taxable gains to you each year, and your money compounds untaxed until you sell. Index funds, and ETFs in particular, tend to distribute fewer gains than actively managed funds (INV-007). Inside a 401(k) or IRA this does not matter, because nothing is taxed until withdrawal.

The other side

Five serious criticisms, and what the evidence says

Indexing has become the default, which is exactly when its weaknesses deserve the most attention. Here are the criticisms serious people make, with the best responses.

1. Concentration: "the S&P 500 is really a bet on a few giant companies"

Fair point. Cap weighting means an index fund owns more of whatever has risen most. When a few companies dominate, so does their risk. Response: an index fund is exactly as concentrated as the market itself; anyone who holds less of the giants must, by Sharpe's arithmetic, be matched by someone holding more. Investors who worry can hold a total-market fund (slightly less concentrated), add international stocks (the three-fund portfolio does), or add a modest equal-weight or value tilt — accepting that such tilts will sometimes lag.

Equal weighting
What it is
Holding every company in an index in the same amount, regardless of size, and rebalancing back to equal amounts periodically.
Used for
Reducing dependence on the largest companies; it tilts toward smaller and cheaper stocks.
What it means for you
It is an active bet against the giants, with more trading and higher cost. In the first half of 2026 the S&P 500 Equal Weight Index beat the cap-weighted index by about 2%; in other periods it has trailed for years.

2. "Passive investing is a bubble"

The argument: index funds buy whatever is in the index at any price, so as they grow, fewer investors check whether prices make sense, and money flows push up the biggest stocks. What the research says:

  • Valentin Haddad, Paul Huebner and Erik Loualiche (American Economic Review, 2025) estimates that the rise of passive investing over 20 years has made demand for individual stocks about 11% more inelastic: prices move more for a given flow of money. That is a real effect.
  • Vladyslav Sushko and Grant Turner of the Bank for International Settlements (2018) reviewed evidence that joining the S&P 500 increases a stock's correlation with the index, and noted that bond indexes, which weight issuers by the market value of their outstanding debt, steer passive bond funds toward the largest and potentially more leveraged borrowers. They also found passive mutual fund flows were fairly stable during stress, while ETF flows were more volatile.
  • Against the bubble view: index funds were 52% of fund assets at the end of 2025, and funds own only part of the market; active traders still set prices every second. Grossman and Stiglitz's logic (INV-043) implies that if indexing ever made prices badly wrong, the reward for active research would rise and pull money back.

Verdict: a legitimate research question about how markets work, not a reliable signal of a crash, and not a reason to abandon diversified low-cost investing.

3. "Indexing guarantees mediocrity"

True by design: you will get the market's return, minus a little. But over the 20 years to mid-2026, that "mediocre" result beat 92.6% of active large-cap funds (INV-043).

4. "The S&P 500 is not diversified enough"

An S&P 500 fund owns only large US companies. The three-fund portfolio answers this with international stocks and bonds; INV-004 showed why no single country should be assumed to lead forever.

5. "Index funds make it easy to trade too much"

ETFs trade all day, and a cheap fund bought and sold in a panic can do more damage than an expensive one held patiently. The low cost only counts if you stay the course (INV-061 on the behavior gap).

Real history

Three episodes

Case study 1"A sure path to mediocrity," 1976
What happened
Vanguard launched the First Index Investment Trust in 1976, tracking the S&P 500 at low cost for individual investors. Industry insiders ridiculed it as "un-American" and "a sure path to mediocrity." The fund survives today as the Vanguard 500 Index Fund, and index funds held $19.1 trillion by the end of 2025.
What it teaches
The best ideas in personal finance are often boring and initially mocked, and the people mocking them may be paid by the alternative.
Source
Vanguard, "Our history" (corporate.vanguard.com); Investment Company Institute (2026), 2026 Investment Company Fact Book.
Case study 2Buffett's instructions for his own family
What happened
In his letter covering 2013, Warren Buffett — one of the most successful active investors in history — wrote that his will instructs a trustee, for his wife's benefit, to "Put 10% of the cash in short-term government bonds and 90% in a very low-cost S&P 500 index fund. (I suggest Vanguard's.)" He added that the "know-nothing" investor who diversifies and keeps costs minimal is "virtually certain to get satisfactory results."
What it teaches
Even the investors most likely to beat the market recommend indexing for people who are not professional stock pickers. (A 90% stock allocation is his choice for a very wealthy beneficiary; your own mix depends on your plan, INV-025.)
Source
Berkshire Hathaway Inc. (2014). 2013 letter to shareholders.
Case study 3When the giants stumbled, first half of 2026
What happened
In the first half of 2026 market leadership broadened: the S&P MidCap 400 rose 17% and the S&P SmallCap 600 24%, against 10% for the S&P 500, and the Magnificent 7's weight in the S&P 500 fell from 34.9% to 31.6%. The S&P 500 Equal Weight Index beat the cap-weighted index by about 2%.
What it teaches
Concentration cuts both ways. A total-market or three-fund investor automatically owned the mid and small companies that led, without having to predict the rotation — and still 67% of active large-cap managers trailed the S&P 500 in that "stock picker's market."
Source
S&P Dow Jones Indices (2026). SPIVA U.S. Scorecard, Mid-Year 2026.

Decisions

Simplify, or not?

Scenario 1 · The Riveras

Marcus and Elena hold six overlapping large-cap funds across two 401(k)s. Both plans offer a total US stock index fund, an international index fund and a bond index fund. What should they do?

Keep all sixThey own the same large companies several times over, with no bonds or international exposure, and some of the funds are likely higher-cost active funds (INV-004, INV-043).
ConsolidateRight. Inside 401(k)s, switching funds has no tax cost. At a 0.035% blend versus a 0.392% average-fund blend, $62,000 plus $20,000 a year for 30 years at 7% ends roughly $187,000 higher, before any difference in returns.
Go all technologyThis concentrates the risk they already have too much of. The best performer of the past is not a plan.
The principle: count the decisions you make, not the funds you own; three broad funds cover what six narrow ones miss.
Scenario 2 · Ruth

Ruth's $780,000 IRA is spread across 14 mutual funds a former broker chose, several with sales loads already paid. She finds the statements overwhelming. Is indexing right for a 68-year-old retiree?

Only for the youngIndexing is about costs and diversification, which matter at every age. What changes with age is the stock/bond mix, not the case for low cost.
Index core, retiree mixRight. Inside an IRA, selling funds to simplify triggers no tax. Loads paid years ago cannot be recovered by staying; the question is only what the funds cost from here. Her withdrawal plan (INV-076) and the required minimum distribution (RMD) rules (INV-081) are easier with three funds than 14.
The principle: simplicity is a feature in retirement; it makes withdrawals, rebalancing and, later, her daughter Anna's job much easier.

What indexing looks like for each household

One fund may be enough. A low-cost target-date index fund in her 401(k) is effectively a three-fund portfolio that rebalances itself. Her priorities are the full match and her 6.8% loans (INV-020).
Three funds, two accounts, one plan. Consolidating six overlapping funds into three broad ones adds the bonds and international stocks they lack, at no tax cost inside the 401(k)s.
Index the rest of the portfolio. Jordan's $84,000 401(k) can hold the three funds; the employer stock is the concentration to unwind gradually, and Ava's college money needs a shorter-term plan (INV-034).
Index core, tax-aware transition. Moving the $220,000 of technology stocks into index funds must be staged around capital-gains taxes (INV-035, INV-037). If their advisor's value is planning, an index core lets them judge the 1.25% fee on that basis.
Fewer funds, clearer withdrawals. Three or four index funds in her IRA make it simple to take withdrawals from whichever is above target, and simple for Anna to understand.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A portfolio is 60% in a US stock index fund (0.03%), 20% in an international index fund (0.05%) and 20% in a bond index fund (0.03%). What is its blended expense ratio, in percent?

0.60 × 0.03 = 0.018; 0.20 × 0.05 = 0.010; 0.20 × 0.03 = 0.006. Total = 0.034%.
Exercise 2

What does that portfolio cost per year on $500,000, in dollars?

500,000 × 0.00034 = $170 a year. At a 1% all-in cost it would be $5,000.
Exercise 3

Seven companies are 31.6% of the S&P 500, and the S&P 500 is about 80% of the value of the whole US market. Roughly what share of a total-market fund do those seven companies make up, in percent?

0.316 × 0.80 = 0.253, about 25.3%. A total-market fund is slightly less concentrated, but not dramatically so.
Exercise 4

In the three-company index of Figure 44.3, Big Co is worth $600 billion out of $1,000 billion. How many dollars of each $1,000 invested go to Big Co?

Weight = 600 ÷ 1,000 = 60%, so $600 of every $1,000.
