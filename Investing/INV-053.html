ESG and Values-Based Investing

Some investors want their money to avoid businesses they object to; others believe environmental, social and governance information helps predict risk. Both travel under the label "ESG," which is part of the confusion. This module separates the ideas, shows why ratings agencies disagree about the same companies, measures what ESG funds cost, reviews the evidence on returns, and explains where US regulation stands in 2026.

The question this module answersCan I invest in line with my values, and what does it cost me in fees, diversification and return?

Start here

You already make values choices with your money

You might pay a little more for coffee from a company you like, or avoid a store whose practices bother you. Investing can work the same way: a fund can be built to leave out certain industries, favor companies with better records on pollution or labor, or press companies to change.

The ordinary broad index fund does none of this. It owns every company in proportion to its size, and for most investors that remains a sound default. Values-based investing asks you to accept some deviation from "the whole market" in exchange for aligning your portfolio with what you care about. The practical questions are how large that deviation is, what it costs, and whether the label on the fund means what you think it means. Figure 53.1 lays out the range of approaches, which are often lumped together.

The vocabulary

Five terms to get straight

ESG investingenvironmental, social, governance
What it is
Investing that takes into account a company's environmental record (emissions, pollution), social record (labor, safety, community) and governance (board independence, pay, shareholder rights).
Used for
Either aligning a portfolio with values, or using non-financial information to judge financial risk, or both.
What it means for you
"ESG" on a fund name tells you the manager uses these factors somehow. Only the prospectus tells you how.
Exclusionary screeningnegative screening
What it is
Removing companies from a portfolio because of what they do, such as making tobacco or weapons or extracting fossil fuels, usually above a revenue threshold.
Used for
Avoiding ownership of businesses an investor objects to.
What it means for you
The easiest approach to understand and verify: the prospectus lists what is excluded.
Impact investing
What it is
Investing with the intention of producing measurable social or environmental results alongside a financial return, often in private companies, projects or community lending.
Used for
Directing capital to specific outcomes, such as affordable housing or renewable power.
What it means for you
The most direct route to impact, usually with less liquidity and higher costs; rarely available in a 401(k).
Shareholder engagementstewardship, proxy voting
What it is
Using the rights that come with owning shares — voting at annual meetings and talking with management — to influence how a company is run.
Used for
Seeking change from inside rather than by selling.
What it means for you
Your index fund's manager votes the shares it holds on your behalf, so its voting policies are part of what you choose when you choose the fund.
Greenwashing
What it is
Marketing that makes an investment or company sound more environmentally or socially responsible than its actual practices justify.
Used for
(By sellers) attracting money that wants a responsible label.
What it means for you
Check holdings and methods, not names. The SEC has brought enforcement cases over exactly this (see "Case studies").

Aggregate confusion

Why one company can be "good" and "bad" at the same time

ESG rating
What it is
A score or grade assigned to a company by a data provider, summarizing its environmental, social and governance performance or risk.
Used for
Building ESG indexes and funds, screening holdings and marketing products.
What it means for you
An ESG fund inherits its rating provider's judgments, which can differ sharply from another provider's.

You might expect different agencies' ratings of the same company to agree closely. ESG ratings do not. In "Aggregate Confusion: The Divergence of ESG Ratings" (Review of Finance, 2022), Florian Berg, Julian Kölbel and Roberto Rigobon compared ratings from six prominent providers: KLD (Kinder, Lydenberg and Domini), Sustainalytics, Moody's ESG (Vigeo-Eiris), S&P Global (RobecoSAM), Refinitiv (Asset4) and MSCI. The correlations between the ratings ranged from only 0.38 to 0.71.

The authors mapped all six methods onto a common set of categories and decomposed the disagreement into three sources:

  1. Measurement (56%). Raters assess the same attribute — say, labor practices — using different indicators and reach different answers.
  2. Scope (38%). Raters include different attributes: one counts lobbying, another does not.
  3. Weight (6%). Raters weigh the same attributes differently. Surprisingly, this matters least.

The researchers also found a "rater effect": a rater's overall view of a firm seems to influence how it measures the firm's individual categories. Their conclusion is a call for more attention to how the underlying data are generated. For an investor, the implication is practical: two funds that both say "ESG leaders" can hold quite different companies, because they rely on different raters.

Going furtherWhat to do about rating disagreement

If you care about a specific issue, look for a fund that screens on that issue directly, with a published list of exclusions or criteria, rather than one that relies on an overall ESG score. A published exclusion list is easier to check than a composite score. MIT Sloan's Aggregate Confusion Project continues this research.

Inside a real fund

What an ESG index fund leaves out, and what it charges

Vanguard's ESG U.S. Stock ETF (ticker ESGV) is a useful example because its prospectus spells out its method. It tracks the FTSE US All Cap Choice Index, which excludes companies that the index provider determines have specified involvement in, or revenue from: adult entertainment, alcohol, tobacco, cannabis, gambling, chemical and biological weapons, cluster munitions, anti-personnel mines, nuclear weapons, conventional military weapons, civilian firearms, nuclear power, and coal, oil or gas. It also excludes companies that do not meet certain labor, human rights, environmental and anti-corruption standards, and certain diversity criteria.

Why would excluding hundreds of companies have helped over the five years 2020 through 2024? Excluding coal, oil and gas companies removes much of the energy sector, which is a small slice of broad indexes (energy was 4.0% of the MSCI ACWI (All Country World Index) at the end of August 2026). Every excluded company leaves more weight in the rest of the market. The most likely explanation for the gap is therefore sector mix, not ESG skill: over this window, what the fund left out happened to lag what it kept. In a period when energy or the other excluded industries lead, the same fund can trail.

The fee gap here is small. The bigger "cost" of an exclusion portfolio is its tracking difference from the market: excluded sectors will sometimes lead and sometimes lag, and you will not know in advance. The calculator shows how each piece compounds.

With the defaults ($50,000 plus $6,000 a year for 30 years at 7% before fees), the 0.06-point fee difference alone costs about $14,000, about 1.4% of the ending balance. Now set the ESG fund's return difference to +0.5% a year: it ends about $101,000 ahead. At −0.5% it ends about $116,000 behind. The uncertainty around what the exclusions will do dwarfs the fee.

What research says

Do values cost return? Theory and evidence

There are two ways to think about ESG and returns, and they point in opposite directions over different horizons.

The expected-return argumentPástor, Stambaugh and Taylor, 2021
What it is
If many investors prefer to hold "green" companies, they bid up their prices, which lowers those companies' future expected returns. In the authors' model, green assets have low expected returns both because investors enjoy holding them and because they hedge climate risk.
Used for
Understanding why a popular preference can reduce the long-run return of the preferred assets.
What it means for you
A values portfolio may reasonably be expected to earn slightly less over the long run — a price some investors are happy to pay.
The realized-return twist"Dissecting green returns"
What it is
The same authors found that green stocks and bonds did deliver high returns in recent years, but attributed this to unexpectedly strong increases in environmental concern, not to high expected returns. When tastes shift toward green, green prices jump.
Used for
Explaining why strong past performance can coexist with lower expected future returns.
What it means for you
Do not extrapolate a decade of ESG outperformance. It may have come from a one-time shift in preferences that cannot repeat indefinitely.

Put together, the research suggests a sober view. Screening on values is a legitimate preference that probably costs a little expected return, perhaps offset or even reversed for long stretches when preferences shift your way. Using ESG information to beat the market faces the same hurdle as any active strategy: if the information is widely known, it is likely already in prices (INV-043).

Practitioner depthWhy study results conflict

Studies of ESG performance disagree partly because they use different ratings (which, as the previous tab showed, disagree), different periods (tech-heavy, energy-light portfolios did well in the late 2010s and badly in 2022), and different definitions of "ESG." Before trusting any single study, check which rating it used and whether its results survive controlling for sector and factor exposures such as size, value and quality (INV-048).

Regulation

Where US rules stand

US rules on ESG investing have moved in several directions since 2022. As of September 2026, the main pieces are these.

  1. Fund names. In September 2023 the SEC amended the Investment Company Act "Names Rule" so that funds whose names suggest a focus — including terms referencing ESG factors — must adopt a policy to invest at least 80% of their assets accordingly, review compliance at least quarterly, and generally get back into compliance within 90 days. In March 2025 the SEC extended the compliance dates to June 11, 2026 for fund groups with $1 billion or more in net assets and December 11, 2026 for smaller groups.
  2. Fund ESG disclosures. A 2022 SEC proposal to require detailed ESG disclosures from funds and advisers was formally withdrawn on June 12, 2025; the SEC said it did not intend to issue final rules on it.
  3. Company climate disclosures. The SEC adopted climate-related disclosure rules in March 2024 and stayed them on April 4, 2024 pending litigation. It voted on March 27, 2025 to end its defense, and on May 29, 2026 proposed rescinding the rules entirely. As of September 2026 the rescission was a proposal, not a final rule.
  4. Anti-fraud rules still apply. Whatever the disclosure rules, a fund or adviser that misdescribes its ESG process can be charged with making misleading statements, as the DWS case shows.
  5. Retirement plans. The Labor Department's November 2022 rule says plan fiduciaries may consider ESG factors, including the economic effects of climate change, when they are relevant to a risk-and-return analysis; allows a "tiebreaker" choice on other grounds when investments equally serve the plan's financial interests; and removed special restrictions on ESG funds as default investments. Rules in this area have changed between administrations, so check the Labor Department's current guidance.

Real enforcement and rulemaking

Three cases every ESG investor should know

Case study 1DWS: saying one thing, doing another (2023)
What happened
On September 25, 2023 the SEC charged DWS Investment Management Americas, a Deutsche Bank subsidiary, in two actions. In the ESG action, the SEC's order found that DWS marketed itself as a leader in ESG, but from August 2018 until late 2021 failed to adequately implement certain provisions of its own global ESG integration policy as it had led clients and investors to believe. DWS agreed to a $19 million penalty in that action, part of $25 million in total penalties.
What it teaches
An ESG label is a claim about a process. The only way to know whether the process is followed is disclosure, oversight and, occasionally, enforcement.
Source
U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, press release 2023-194 (September 25, 2023).
Case study 2The Names Rule: 80% means 80%
What happened
Before 2023, the Names Rule's 80% requirement applied to names suggesting particular types of investments. The September 2023 amendments extended it to names suggesting investments with particular characteristics, such as "growth," "value" or ESG terms, and required that name terms be consistent with their plain English meaning or established industry use.
What it teaches
Once the rule applies, a fund called "Sustainable Leaders" must define "sustainable" and keep at least 80% of assets consistent with that definition. It does not mean the definition matches yours; it means it must be stated and followed.
Source
SEC press releases 2023-188 (September 20, 2023) and 2025-54 (March 14, 2025).
Case study 3The climate disclosure rules that never took effect
What happened
In March 2024 the SEC approved rules requiring virtually all public companies to disclose climate-related information such as greenhouse gas emissions and the management of climate-related risks. It stayed them within a month pending litigation in the Eighth Circuit, voted in 2025 to stop defending them, and on May 29, 2026 proposed rescinding them in their entirety, arguing they exceeded its statutory authority.
What it teaches
Investors who want climate data cannot count on a mandatory US standard. ESG ratings and fund screens therefore rest on voluntary company disclosures and on providers' own estimates, one reason ratings disagree.
Source
SEC press release 2026-49 (May 29, 2026).

Decisions

Values in real portfolios

Scenario 1 · Jordan

Jordan wants her savings to avoid fossil fuels. Her 401(k) offers only broad index funds and a target-date fund. What are her realistic options?

Skip the 401(k)She would give up her employer's 50% match on 6% of pay, an immediate 50% return on those dollars. That is a very expensive way to express a value.
Match first, ESG where she canRight. She captures the match, aligns her extra savings, and uses her voice as a participant. The Labor Department's 2022 rule allows plans to offer ESG options when fiduciaries consider them prudent.
Stop savingThe costliest option by far. Time in the market at 24 is worth more than any fund choice.
The principle: never give up a guaranteed benefit, such as a match, to express a preference you can express elsewhere.
Scenario 2 · The Riveras

Elena finds a "clean energy" fund that rose sharply over the past five years and wants to move a large part of their 401(k)s into it. What should they weigh?

Buy it heavilyA thematic fund is concentrated in one industry. Past returns may reflect a jump in demand for green assets (Pástor, Stambaugh and Taylor) rather than higher future returns.
Small slice, broad coreRight. A diversified core with values screens can express their preferences without betting their retirement on one sector's next decade.
The principle: values belong in the whole portfolio; concentration belongs, if anywhere, in a small slice.
Scenario 3 · Ruth

Ruth wants her IRA to reflect causes she and Walter supported, but she depends on it for retirement income. An advisor offers a portfolio of individual "high-ESG-rated" stocks at 1.25% a year. What should she ask?

Ask the questionsRight. Ratings disagree (correlations of 0.38–0.71 across providers), and a 1.25% fee is large next to an ESG index fund. For causes, the charitable strategies in INV-039 may do more good per dollar.
Trust the ratingA different provider might rate the same companies very differently; the label does not settle the question.
The principle: pay for values alignment you can verify, at a price you understand.

Values, households and trade-offs

Match first, values second. The full 401(k) match comes before any fund preference; an IRA with an exclusion index fund is where she has full control.
A broad core with screens if they want them. An ESG index fund can replace one of their overlapping large-cap funds without adding concentration. A thematic fund is a separate, small bet.
Values are cheap to express in an index fund. On her $370,000 of invested savings, an ESG index fund at 0.09% would cost about $333 a year against $111 at 0.03%, a $222 difference. New contributions and her Roth IRA can switch without tax; selling her taxable funds could realize gains. The values decision that matters most for her is making sure what she leaves supports Eli without costing him his benefits (INV-102).
