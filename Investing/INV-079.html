Medicare and Retiree Health Care

Medicare is not free, not automatic for everyone, and not complete. Signing up late can raise your premiums for life, and income two years earlier can raise them further. This module explains each part, the choice between Medicare Advantage and Medigap, the 2026 premiums and income brackets, how HSAs interact with Medicare, and how to cover the years before 65.

The question this module answersWhat will health care cost me in retirement, and which Medicare choices and deadlines matter most?

Start here

Health insurance at 65: what changes

Most working Americans get health insurance through an employer, and the employer handles enrollment. At 65 that changes: Medicare becomes the main coverage for most people, and the enrollment, the choices and the deadlines become yours.

Many people assume Medicare simply starts at 65 and covers everything. For those already collecting Social Security, Parts A and B do start automatically. For everyone else, including anyone delaying Social Security to 67 or 70 as INV-078 often recommends, you must sign up, and missing the window can mean a penalty added to your premium for as long as you have the coverage. Original Medicare also has no yearly limit on out-of-pocket costs, so most people add something to it. Figure 79.1 shows the pieces.

What each part covers and costs

The 2026 numbers

Medicare Part Ahospital insurance
What it is
Coverage for inpatient hospital stays, skilled nursing facility care, hospice, inpatient rehabilitation and some home health care.
Used for
Hospital bills. About 99% of beneficiaries pay no premium because they or a spouse worked at least 40 quarters (about ten years) in Medicare-covered jobs.
What it means for you
"Premium-free" is not "cost-free": in 2026 you pay a $1,736 deductible for each benefit period, and a benefit period can recur several times in a year.
Medicare Part Bmedical insurance
What it is
Coverage for doctors' services, outpatient hospital care, durable medical equipment and many preventive services.
Used for
Most day-to-day medical care. You pay a monthly premium whether or not you use services, a yearly deductible, and usually 20% of the Medicare-approved amount after that.
What it means for you
The standard premium for 2026 is $202.90 a month, $2,434.80 a year per person, deducted from Social Security if you receive it. Higher incomes pay more (tab 5).
Medicare Part Dprescription drug coverage
What it is
Drug coverage sold by private plans under Medicare rules, either as a standalone plan with Original Medicare or built into most Medicare Advantage plans.
Used for
Outpatient prescription drugs. Premiums, formularies and pharmacy networks vary by plan and are re-set every year.
What it means for you
Since 2025 there is a yearly cap on what you pay out of pocket for covered drugs: $2,100 in 2026. Even if you take few drugs, joining when first eligible avoids a lifetime penalty.

Going furtherWhat Original Medicare does not cover

Original Medicare does not cover routine physical exams, eye exams or most dental care, and generally does not cover care outside the United States. Most important for planning, it has no yearly limit on your out-of-pocket costs: 20% of a large bill is still large. That is the gap that Medigap policies, Medicare Advantage out-of-pocket limits, or retiree coverage from a former employer are designed to fill. Long-term custodial care in a nursing home is not covered by any part of Medicare (INV-083).

The big choice

Medicare Advantage or Original Medicare with Medigap?

Medicare AdvantagePart C
What it is
A private health plan approved by Medicare that replaces Original Medicare's way of paying for care. It must cover everything Parts A and B cover, usually includes drug coverage, and may add extras such as some vision, hearing and dental benefits.
Used for
Getting all coverage from one plan, often with low or $0 plan premiums and a yearly out-of-pocket limit.
What it means for you
You usually must use the plan's network for non-emergency care, may need referrals and prior approval, and the plan's rules can change every year.
MedigapMedicare Supplement Insurance
What it is
A private policy that pays some or all of Original Medicare's deductibles, copayments and coinsurance. Policies are standardized: in most states there are ten plans, lettered A–D, F, G and K–N, and the same letter gives the same benefits from every insurer.
Used for
Making costs under Original Medicare predictable, while keeping the freedom to see any provider in the US who takes Medicare.
What it means for you
You pay a monthly Medigap premium on top of Part B and a separate Part D plan, but your bills in a bad health year are small and predictable. Since the benefits by letter are identical, price is the only difference between insurers.
⚠️

The one-way door. Federal law gives you a single six-month Medigap Open Enrollment Period, starting the first month you have Part B and are 65 or older. During it, an insurer must sell you any Medigap policy it offers and cannot charge more for health problems. After it ends, insurers in most states can use medical underwriting to deny you or charge more, except in specific "guaranteed issue" situations or where state law gives more rights. So choosing Medicare Advantage at 65 and switching to Medigap at 75 may not be possible if your health has declined.

Going furtherReading a Medicare Advantage plan

Compare five things about any Medicare Advantage plan, every year, using Medicare's Plan Finder or a free State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) counselor: whether your doctors and hospitals are in the network; the in-network (and out-of-network) out-of-pocket maximum; the drug formulary and pharmacy network for your prescriptions; prior-authorization rules for the care you expect; and what happens if you travel or spend part of the year in another state. A $0 premium plan with a high out-of-pocket maximum can cost more in a bad year than a Medigap policy with a premium.

Deadlines that cost money

When to sign up, and what happens if you miss it

Initial Enrollment Period
What it is
Your first chance to sign up for Medicare: seven months, starting three months before the month you turn 65 and ending three months after it.
Used for
Enrolling in Parts A and B, and joining a Part D or Medicare Advantage plan, without penalties.
What it means for you
Signing up before your birthday month means Part B starts the month you turn 65; signing up in the birthday month or later means it starts the following month.

Other enrollment windows

  • General Enrollment Period: January 1–March 31 each year, for people who missed their initial period and do not qualify for a special one. Coverage starts the month after you sign up, and late penalties may apply.
  • Special Enrollment Period for job-based coverage: if you or your spouse are still working and covered by that employer's group plan, you can delay Part B without penalty and sign up any time while covered or within 8 months after the coverage or the employment ends, whichever happens first. COBRA and retiree coverage do not count as current job-based coverage for this purpose.
  • Open Enrollment: October 15–December 7 each year, to switch between Original Medicare and Medicare Advantage, change Advantage plans or change Part D plans, effective January 1.
  • Medicare Advantage Open Enrollment: January 1–March 31, for people already in an Advantage plan to switch plans or return to Original Medicare (and join a drug plan).
Late enrollment penalty
What it is
A surcharge added to your premium, usually for as long as you have that coverage, if you did not enroll when first eligible and had no qualifying coverage in the meantime.
Used for
Discouraging people from joining only when they become sick.
What it means for you
Part B: 10% of the standard premium for each full 12 months you could have had it. Part D: 1% of the national base premium ($38.99 in 2026) for each month without creditable drug coverage. Both are, for most people, for life; see late enrollment penalty.
Creditable drug coverage
What it is
Drug coverage, typically from an employer or union, that is expected to pay at least as much as standard Part D coverage.
Used for
Delaying Part D without a penalty. Your plan must tell you each year whether its coverage is creditable.
What it means for you
Keep those annual notices. A gap of 63 days or more without creditable coverage after your initial period starts the penalty clock.

Missing your Initial Enrollment Period is the usual cause. Medicare.gov's own examples for 2026: waiting two full years to sign up for Part B, without a special enrollment period, adds a 20% penalty, making the premium $243.50 a month instead of $202.90. Waiting 14 months to join a drug plan without creditable coverage adds a 14% penalty: 14% of $38.99, about $5.50 a month, added to the plan's premium. If you must buy Part A and delay it, its premium can rise 10% for twice the number of years you delayed.

Income-related premiums

How income two years ago sets your premium today

IRMAAincome-related monthly adjustment amount
What it is
An extra amount added to the Part B and Part D premiums of beneficiaries whose modified adjusted gross income (MAGI: adjusted gross income plus tax-exempt interest) is above set thresholds.
Used for
Making higher-income beneficiaries pay a larger share of Part B's cost: 35%, 50%, 65%, 80% or 85%, instead of about 25%.
What it means for you
For 2026 premiums, SSA generally uses your 2024 tax return. A large Roth conversion, capital gain or final-year bonus can raise premiums two years later. This surcharge is called IRMAA. Roughly 8% of Part B enrollees pay it.

The default is a married couple filing jointly with $250,000 of 2024 MAGI, both on Medicare with a $40 drug plan. They land in the first IRMAA bracket: each pays $284.10 for Part B and $14.50 extra for Part D, $677.20 a month for the household, or $8,126 a year. Had their MAGI been $218,000, they would have paid $2,297 less for the year. The brackets are cliffs: one dollar over a threshold triggers the full surcharge for that tier.

Going furtherWhen income falls: Form SSA-44

Because IRMAA looks back two years, a new retiree's first Medicare premiums are often based on their last full working year. SSA will use a more recent year if your income went down because of a "life-changing event": marriage, divorce or death of a spouse; you or your spouse stopping or reducing work; loss of income-producing property; the end of an employer pension plan; or an employer settlement. You file Form SSA-44 with documentation. Retiring is the most common qualifying event.

Practitioner depthIRMAA and Roth conversions

A Roth conversion adds its full amount to MAGI in the year of conversion (INV-038). For someone 63 or older, that income can set IRMAA two years later. The cost is a step function, so the useful question is not "how much can I convert?" but "how much can I convert before the next threshold?" For a joint filer in 2026 terms, converting enough to go from $218,000 to $218,001 of MAGI costs about $2,297 a year for a couple, while converting another $56,000 up to $274,000 costs nothing more. Thresholds are adjusted for inflation over time, so plan with the current year's table.

Two transitions

HSAs meet Medicare, and the years before 65

Health savings accounts and Medicare

A health savings account (INV-033) is one of the best places to pay retirement health costs, but it has two Medicare traps.

  • Contributions stop at Medicare. Beginning with the first month you are enrolled in Medicare, your HSA contribution limit is zero (IRS Publication 969). For 2026 the full-year limits are $4,400 for self-only and $8,750 for family coverage, plus $1,000 if you are 55 or older, prorated by month in the year you enroll.
  • Part A can be retroactive. If you sign up for premium-free Part A after 65, or apply for Social Security, Part A coverage starts up to six months back (never before 65). Contributions made during those months become excess contributions. Medicare.gov's advice: you and your employer should stop contributing to your HSA six months before you retire or apply for Social Security.
  • Premiums are qualified expenses. After 65, HSA money can pay Medicare Part B, Part D and Medicare Advantage premiums tax-free, but not Medigap premiums.

Recent rules with real effects

Three changes worth understanding

Case study 1The Part D redesign
What happened
The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 restructured the standard Part D benefit, creating a yearly out-of-pocket cap of $2,000 in 2025, after which enrollees pay nothing for covered drugs. CMS set the 2026 cap at $2,100, adjusted for the annual increase in average Part D drug spending, with a $615 maximum deductible and 25% coinsurance in between.
What it teaches
Drug costs, once a large unknown in retirement budgets, now have a known maximum for covered drugs. A retiree on expensive specialty drugs can budget about $2,100 a year plus premiums, where before the redesign there was no ceiling.
Source
Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (2025). Final CY 2026 Part D redesign program instructions [Fact sheet].
Case study 2Medicare.gov's Part B penalty example
What happened
Medicare.gov illustrates the rule with a person who waited two full years to sign up for Part B without a special enrollment period: a 20% penalty on the $202.90 standard premium, for a 2026 premium of $243.50 a month.
What it teaches
The penalty is a percentage of each year's standard premium, so it grows as premiums grow, and it lasts as long as you have Part B. The most common way people fall into it is relying on COBRA or retiree coverage, which does not qualify for the job-based special enrollment period.
Source
Medicare.gov. Avoid late enrollment penalties.
Case study 3The return of the 400% subsidy limit
What happened
For tax years 2021 through 2025, Congress temporarily removed the rule that household income must be no more than 400% of the federal poverty line to receive the ACA premium tax credit. For 2026 the limit applies again; the IRS notes that above 400% no credit is allowed and any advance payments must be repaid in full.
What it teaches
For early retirees buying Marketplace coverage, income just above the limit can cost thousands of dollars of subsidy. Choosing which accounts to draw from before 65 is now partly a health-insurance decision.
Source
Internal Revenue Service (2026). Questions and answers on the premium tax credit, Q7; Eligibility for the premium tax credit.

Decisions

Three Medicare decisions

Scenario 1 · The Harpers' Roth conversions

At 63, with little other income, the Harpers plan to convert pre-tax 401(k) money to Roth. Their baseline MAGI will be about $90,000. How big should the conversion be?

All at onceA $500,000 conversion would push their MAGI past $410,000, into a bracket costing about $12,710 a year in extra premiums for two, on top of high income tax. Two years later is when that bill arrives.
Size to brackets and thresholdsRight. From a $90,000 base, converting about $128,000 keeps MAGI at the $218,000 threshold. IRMAA is one input alongside income tax brackets and, before 65, Marketplace subsidies (INV-038, INV-042).
Skip conversionsAvoiding IRMAA can cost more than it saves if large RMDs later push them into higher tax brackets and IRMAA anyway (INV-081).
The principle: treat IRMAA thresholds as speed bumps to plan around, not walls to avoid at any cost.
Scenario 2 · Ruth's $0 plan pitch

Ruth has Original Medicare, a Medigap Plan G policy and a Part D plan. A caller urges her to switch to a $0-premium Medicare Advantage plan with dental coverage. What should she weigh?

Switch for the $0 premiumThe saving is real in healthy years, but her costs in a bad year could rise to the plan's out-of-pocket maximum, and she may need network approval for specialists she sees now.
Weigh the one-way doorRight. Ruth's six-month Medigap open enrollment ended years ago. If she drops Plan G and later wants it back, an insurer in most states can refuse or charge more based on her health. A free SHIP counselor can compare plans with her at no cost.
The principle: the option to return to Medigap is valuable and, for most people over 65, not guaranteed.
Scenario 3 · Jordan's parent at 65

Jordan's father turns 65 next spring. He has retiree coverage from his old employer and is delaying Social Security. Should he wait on Part B?

WaitThis is the common mistake. The special enrollment period that lets people delay Part B applies to coverage based on current employment. Retiree coverage and COBRA do not qualify, so waiting could trigger a lifetime penalty.
Sign up nowRight. Because he is not yet on Social Security, enrollment is not automatic. He should also ask his retiree plan how it works with Medicare: if it expects Medicare to pay first, going without Part B could leave large bills as well as a penalty.
The principle: only coverage from current work lets you delay Part B safely; everything else starts the penalty clock.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

Someone went three full years without Part B and without qualifying coverage. What is their 2026 monthly Part B premium, in dollars (to the nearest ten cents)?

Penalty = 3 × 10% = 30% of $202.90 = $60.87. Premium = $202.90 + $60.87 = $263.77, which Medicare rounds to $263.80 a month, for as long as they have Part B.
Exercise 2

A married couple, both on Medicare with Part D, has 2024 joint MAGI of $218,500 instead of $218,000. How much more do they pay in 2026 premiums for the year, in dollars?

$218,500 is in the first IRMAA bracket: +$81.20 for Part B and +$14.50 for Part D, per person per month. ($81.20 + $14.50) × 12 months × 2 people = $2,296.80, from $500 of extra income.
Exercise 3

Someone joins a drug plan after 20 months without creditable drug coverage. What is their 2026 monthly Part D penalty, in dollars (to the nearest ten cents)?

20 months × 1% = 20% of the $38.99 national base beneficiary premium = $7.798, rounded to $7.80 a month, added to the plan's premium.
Exercise 4

Someone with self-only HDHP coverage turns 65 and enrolls in Medicare in July 2026, having been HSA-eligible January through June. Using the 2026 limit of $4,400 plus the $1,000 catch-up, what is their HSA contribution limit for 2026, in dollars?

Six eligible months: ($4,400 + $1,000) × 6 ÷ 12 = $2,700. This follows the method of IRS Publication 969's example. If Part A were made retroactive into those months, the limit would shrink further.
