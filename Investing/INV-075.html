How Much You Need

"How much do I need to retire?" has no single answer, but it has a method. Estimate what you will spend, subtract the income that arrives no matter what, turn the gap into a portfolio target, and test that target against how long you might live. This module walks through each step with real Social Security and life-table numbers.

The question this module answersHow big does my nest egg need to be, and how would I know if I am on track?

Start here

Three numbers, not one

You have probably heard a round number: "a million dollars," "ten times your salary," "80% of your income." Each is a reasonable starting guess for somebody. None is built from your life.

Round numbers survive because the real question is hard. It depends on what you will spend, on Social Security and any pension, on taxes, on markets, and on how long you live, which nobody knows. But the question breaks into three smaller ones that you can answer: What will I spend each year? How much of that arrives as guaranteed income? How large a portfolio can safely fill the gap for as long as I might live? Figure 75.1 shows how they fit together.

What this module covers

  1. Replacement ratios: the quick percentage-of-income method, and when it misleads.
  2. Spending-based estimates: building a retirement budget, and how spending really changes with age.
  3. The 25× rule: where it comes from and six ways it can be wrong for you.
  4. Longevity: what the Social Security Administration's (SSA) life tables say about how long one person, or the longer-lived of a couple, may live.
  5. A retirement-needs calculator that puts the pieces together.

The quick method

What share of your paycheck do you need to replace?

The fastest estimate starts from your current income. If you earn $100,000 and expect to need 75% of it, your retirement budget is $75,000 a year. The percentage has a name.

Replacement ratioalso called replacement rate
What it is
Retirement income as a percentage of income just before retirement. A $75,000 retirement income after a $100,000 final salary is a 75% replacement ratio.
Used for
Setting a first-pass target decades before retirement, when a detailed budget would be guesswork; and comparing how much of a paycheck Social Security replaces at different earnings levels.
What it means for you
It is a shortcut for your spending, not a law of nature. People who save a lot now, or who will have a paid-off house, often need much less than 75%; people with rising health costs or big travel plans may need more.

The Social Security Administration's own retirement booklet puts the conventional wisdom plainly: "Most financial advisers say you will need about 80% of pre-retirement income to live comfortably in retirement, which includes your Social Security benefits, investments, and other personal savings." The same booklet reports how much of a worker's average wages Social Security itself replaces when benefits start at 67: as much as 78% for very low earners, about 42% for medium earners and about 28% for high earners (SSA, Retirement Benefits, January 2026).

Why the number is below 100%

Several things you pay for today disappear or shrink at retirement. Stripping them out of the Harpers' $260,000 income, in Figure 75.2, shows why a ratio can badly overstate the need for a household that saves heavily.

  • Saving stops. The Harpers put about $45,000 a year into their 401(k)s and individual retirement accounts (IRAs). That money is not spending.
  • Payroll taxes stop. Wages carry a 6.2% Social Security tax (on earnings up to $184,500 in 2026) and a 1.45% Medicare tax. On the Harpers' pay that is $19,890 a year.
  • Income taxes usually fall, because taxable income is lower and part of Social Security is untaxed. They rarely fall to zero, especially with a large pre-tax 401(k).
  • Some big costs end: a mortgage paid off, children through college, commuting and work clothes.
  • Some costs rise: health insurance before 65 and Medicare premiums after (INV-079), and often travel in the early years.
Going furtherWhy a single ratio misleads at both ends of the income scale

A low earner saves little, pays little income tax and gets a large share of pay back from Social Security's progressive formula (INV-078), so the 78% that Social Security replaces may cover most of what is needed. A high earner saves more, pays more tax that goes away, and gets only about 28% from Social Security, so the ratio needed from savings is larger but the ratio of total spending may be smaller. Applying one percentage to everyone therefore overstates the need of some households and understates others. Use a replacement ratio for people decades from retirement; switch to a budget within about ten years of it.

Practitioner depthA replacement ratio built from components
Needed ratio ≈ 1 − saving rate − payroll-tax rate − (income tax now − income tax later)/pay − ending costs/pay + new costs/pay

For the Harpers: 1 − 17.3% − 7.7% − about 11% (their $40,000 of income tax today, less roughly $12,000 in retirement, divided by pay) − 20.7% + 9.1% ≈ 53%. The ratio is an output of the budget, not a substitute for it. Payroll tax is 6.2% on wages up to the 2026 taxable maximum of $184,500 plus 1.45% on all wages (SSA, contribution and benefit base).

The better method, closer to retirement

Build the budget you will actually live on

Within ten years or so of retiring, a budget beats a ratio. Start from what you spend today (a year of bank and card statements is the honest source), then adjust line by line for what will change. Keep four kinds of spending separate, because they behave differently over time.

Retirement spending smile
What it is
The pattern, found in household survey data, that inflation-adjusted spending tends to fall during retirement, falling faster in the early and late years, while health spending rises.
Used for
Deciding whether to assume that spending rises exactly with inflation every year, which most calculators do, or drifts down in real terms.
What it means for you
Assuming full inflation increases forever is conservative. It is a sensible default, but it can make you save or work longer than you need to. Do not assume the decline until you see it in your own spending.

David Blanchett studied retired households in the RAND version of the Health and Retirement Study. He found that although the basket of goods retirees buy tends to rise in price faster than general inflation, largely because of medical costs, actual retiree spending declined in real terms by approximately 1% a year on average, with larger changes in early and late retirement (Blanchett, Journal of Financial Planning, 2014). Figure 75.3 shows what that difference does to thirty years of spending.

Going furtherSeparate the floor from the extras

Many planners divide the budget into a floor (essentials and health care, which must be paid in every market) and flexible spending (travel, gifts, upgrades). The floor is the part worth covering with guaranteed income: Social Security, a pension or an income annuity (INV-080, INV-082). The flexible part can come from the portfolio, and can be trimmed in a bad year, which is exactly what the guardrail strategies in INV-076 rely on. For the Harpers, an $80,000 floor is mostly covered by the $76,000 of Social Security they expect if both claim at full retirement age.

From a gap to a nest egg

The 25× rule and its limits

Once you know the yearly gap your savings must fill, you need a way to turn it into a lump sum. The most widely quoted shortcut multiplies by 25.

Rule of 25the 25× rule
What it is
A target nest egg equal to 25 times the yearly amount you plan to withdraw. It is the 4% withdrawal rate turned upside down: 1 ÷ 0.04 = 25.
Used for
Converting a spending gap into a savings target in one step, and checking whether a portfolio can support a given budget.
What it means for you
A $40,000 gap needs about $1 million under the rule of 25. It is a planning rule from US history over 30-year retirements, not a promise; the rest of this tab explains when to use a different multiple.
Nest egg needed = (yearly spending + taxes − guaranteed income) ÷ withdrawal rate    e.g. $61,000 ÷ 0.04 = $1,525,000

The 4% figure traces to William Bengen's 1994 study of historical US returns, which found that a first-year withdrawal of 4% of the portfolio, raised each year for inflation, had never exhausted a 50/50 stock-and-bond portfolio in less than 33 years. INV-076 tests the rule on the full 1928–2025 record. For now, Figure 75.4 shows how much the multiple changes with the withdrawal rate you are willing to assume.

Six limits of the rule

  1. It was built for about 30 years. Someone retiring at 50 or planning to 100 needs a lower rate, and so a higher multiple (INV-071).
  2. It ignores taxes. A dollar in a pre-tax 401(k) is not a spendable dollar. If a quarter of every withdrawal goes to tax, you need to include that tax in the gap, as the calculator's tax line does, or divide the after-tax gap by 0.75 first.
  3. It ignores fees. The research used index returns with no costs. A 1% advisory fee is a quarter of a 4% withdrawal (INV-007).
  4. It assumes rigid spending. People who can cut spending after a bad year can start higher; people who cannot should start lower (INV-076).
  5. It says nothing about valuations or today's yields. The 1928–2025 record includes starting points far better and far worse than today; see sequence risk in INV-077.
  6. It applies to the gap, not your whole budget. The most common mistake is to multiply total spending by 25 and forget that Social Security will pay a large share of it.
Practitioner depthAdjusting the multiple for taxes and fees

If the portfolio is entirely pre-tax and your average tax rate on withdrawals is t, and all-in costs are f a year, a rough adjusted target is gapafter tax ÷ (1 − t) ÷ (w − f), where w is the withdrawal rate. With t = 15%, f = 1% and w = 4%, a $40,000 after-tax gap needs $40,000 ÷ 0.85 ÷ 0.03 = about $1.57 million, not $1 million. This treats the fee as a straight reduction in the sustainable rate, which is an approximation.

The unknown horizon

How long might you need the money?

The single hardest input is the one nobody knows: how long you will live. Planners call this longevity risk. Planning to "life expectancy" sounds prudent but is not, because life expectancy is an average and about half of people outlive it.

Period life table
What it is
A table giving, for each age, the chance of dying within a year and the average remaining lifetime, based on the death rates observed in one year. The Social Security Administration publishes one for the population covered by Social Security.
Used for
Estimating life expectancy and the odds of reaching a given age; the SSA uses its tables in the annual Trustees Report.
What it means for you
It uses death rates for one past year and assumes no future improvement, so it tends to understate how long people alive today will live. Your own health, income and family history move the odds a lot.

The SSA's current period life table is the 2023 table, as used in the 2026 Trustees Report. It gives a man who has just turned 65 a life expectancy of 18.12 years (to about 83) and a woman 20.66 years (to about 86). But the spread around those averages is wide, and for a married couple the relevant question is how long the longer-lived spouse will live.

Going furtherPeriod versus cohort tables

A period table answers: if today's death rates at every age never changed, how long would someone live? A cohort table follows people born in the same year and builds in expected improvements in mortality, so it usually shows longer lives for people who are young today. SSA publishes both kinds for Trustees Report work. For personal planning, the period table is a transparent, conservative-on-spending starting point; the direction of error is that you may live longer than it suggests, which argues for a later planning age, not an earlier one.

Practitioner depthThe arithmetic of joint survival
P(survive a → T) = lT ÷ la    P(at least one) = 1 − (1 − p1)(1 − p2)    P(both) = p1 × p2

lx is the table's "number of lives" at exact age x out of 100,000 births. For a man, l65 = 79,084 and l90 = 19,063 in the 2023 table, so the chance of reaching 90 from 65 is 24.1%. The independence assumption slightly overstates the chance that at least one survives, because married partners share income, habits and environment; it is a standard simplification in planning software.

Put it together

A retirement-needs calculator

The calculator starts with the Harpers, who are 57 and 55 and plan to stop work in about six years. Toggle between the two ways of estimating spending and watch the target move.

What the Harpers' numbers show

With a 75% replacement ratio, the Harpers "need" $195,000 a year. Subtracting the $76,000 of Social Security they expect if both claim at full retirement age leaves a $119,000 gap, and 25 times that is $2,975,000. Their $1.1 million, plus $45,000 a year for six years at a 4% real return, projects to about $1.69 million: 57% funded.

With their $125,000 budget plus about $12,000 of income tax on withdrawals, the gap is $61,000 and the target is $1,525,000: 111% funded. The same household is either badly behind or comfortably ahead, depending on the spending estimate. That is why a budget matters within ten years of retirement: the difference here is $1.45 million of target.

⚠️

Three things this calculator leaves out. Taxes are a single number you supply; the account mix matters (INV-042 on tax-aware withdrawals). Returns are steady, with no bad sequence; INV-077 shows why that is optimistic. And the target assumes a roughly 30-year retirement at the withdrawal rate you choose. For year-by-year projections with taxes, Social Security timing and Monte Carlo, use Babak's Retirement Planner (Resources tab).

Practitioner depthThe calculator's formulas
Spending = income × ratio, or budget + tax    Gap = max(0, spending − guaranteed income)    Target = gap ÷ w    Bt = Bt−1(1 + r) + C

All values are in today's dollars, so r is a real (after-inflation) return and Social Security, which is inflation-indexed, can be entered at today's value. A pension without cost-of-living increases loses real value each year, so entering its full nominal amount overstates it; a common fix is to enter about two-thirds of it.

Evidence and official data

Three sources worth knowing

Case study 1Social Security's own replacement rates
What happened
SSA's 2026 retirement booklet states that benefits starting at 67 replace as much as 78% of average wages for very low earners, about 42% for medium earners and about 28% for high earners, and notes that most advisers suggest needing about 80% of pre-retirement income in total.
What it teaches
Social Security alone was never designed to replace a middle or high income. The gap between 80% and 42% is roughly what a medium earner's savings must fill; for a high earner it is closer to 52 points. Check your own figure in your my Social Security statement rather than using the averages.
Source
Social Security Administration (2026). Retirement Benefits (Publication No. 05-10035).
Case study 2Retirees spend less in real terms as they age
What happened
Using the RAND Health and Retirement Study and its consumption survey, David Blanchett found that real retiree spending fell by about 1% a year on average, faster in early and late retirement, even though the prices of what retirees buy rose faster than general inflation. He called the shape the "retirement spending smile."
What it teaches
Assuming spending rises with inflation every year is a conservative planning default. It is a reasonable place to start, but a retiree who sees their own spending drifting down has more room than a rigid model suggests.
Source
Blanchett, D. (2014). Exploring the retirement consumption puzzle. Journal of Financial Planning (May 2014), 34–42.
Case study 3The 2026 Trustees Report and the "83% scenario"
What happened
The 2026 Trustees Report projects that the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance trust fund can pay full scheduled benefits until the fourth quarter of 2032, after which continuing income would cover 78% of them; on a combined basis with the disability fund, reserves last until the third quarter of 2034, when 83% would be payable, unless Congress acts.
What it teaches
Congress has changed the program's finances before and may again, but a prudent plan tests what happens if benefits are cut. For the Harpers, 80% of $76,000 is $60,800: the gap rises by $15,200 and the 25× target by $380,000. INV-078 covers the report in detail.
Source
Social Security and Medicare Boards of Trustees (2026). A summary of the 2026 annual reports.

Decisions

Setting a target in real life

Scenario 1 · The Harpers

An online calculator told the Harpers they need $3 million. Their own budget says about $1.5 million. Tom wants to work until 67 "to be safe." What should they do first?

Trust the $3 millionThat figure came from a 75% replacement ratio. For a couple saving $45,000 a year and about to finish a mortgage and college costs, the ratio (which already includes income tax) overstates their need by about $58,000 a year: $195,000 against a $137,000 budget including tax. Working five extra years on a wrong input is an expensive mistake.
Measure, rebuild and stress-testRight. Even with Social Security at 80% and a 3.5% withdrawal rate, their budget-based target is about $2.18 million: above their projection, but a gap they can close with a year or two more work, a later Social Security claim for Tom, or a modest spending cut.
Retire at 62 regardlessA budget that has not been tested against a year of real statements is a hope. Health insurance before 65, without an employer plan, can be one of the largest costs in the budget (INV-079). Test first.
The principle: the target is only as good as the spending estimate; measure spending before you trust any number.
Scenario 2 · Ruth

Ruth, 68, spends about $60,000 a year. Social Security pays her $34,800. She has $780,000 in her IRA and $60,000 in certificates of deposit (CDs) and savings. A friend says she "doesn't have enough to last." Is she on track?

No, she needs $1.5 millionThis is the most common error: applying 25× to the whole budget. Social Security, which is inflation-adjusted and lasts for life, already covers 58% of her spending.
Yes, with a long horizonRight. Her gap (plus taxes on IRA withdrawals, which she must add) is roughly 3.2–4% of her IRA. From the SSA table, a 68-year-old woman has about a 23% chance of reaching 93 and a 9% chance of reaching 97, so a plan to her mid-90s is sensible. Her CDs are a cushion for bad years (INV-076).
The principle: multiply the gap, not the budget, and plan for a long life rather than an average one.
Scenario 3 · Monica

Monica, 58, earns $92,000 and plans to work to 67. She has $370,000 in retirement accounts plus $30,000 of cash, and spends about $5,200 a month on essentials for herself and Eli. An online calculator using a 75% ratio says she needs $1,035,000. What target should she use?

Use the ratio75% of $92,000 is $69,000; less her $27,600 Social Security estimate, the gap is $41,400 and 25 times that is $1,035,000. It is a reasonable first guess, but at nine years out Monica knows her real spending, and a ratio ignores it.
A budget, then the catch-upRight. $62,400 of essential spending plus about $3,500 of income tax, less $27,600 of Social Security, leaves a $38,300 gap and a $957,500 target at 4%. Her $370,000, plus $14,720 a year (her 12% and the 4% match) at a 5% real return, projects to about $736,000 at 67: 77% funded. Adding the full catch-up schedule ($8,000 a year, $11,250 at ages 60 to 63, in 2028–2031) raises that to about $841,000 (88%). Her mortgage payment of about $804 a month ends in 2038, which lowers the budget from 70.
Count Eli's benefitsEli's Supplemental Security Income (SSI) from 18, and the benefit he can draw on her record once she claims (INV-078), are his money, paid for his needs. They can fairly pay his share of the food and housing costs, but the plan should first work on Monica's own income, because those benefits will be all Eli has after she is gone.
The principle: match the method to the distance; ratios far away, budgets up close.

Six things people say about "the number"

Apply it

What this module means for each household

A savings rate, not a number. At 24, a target 40 years out is mostly guesswork. Saving 15% of pay, including her employer's match, is the practical translation of any reasonable target. A woman her age has about a 31% chance of reaching 90 (SSA table), so her plan should run long.
Ratio-based for now. At 35 and 34, 75% of $145,000 less roughly $62,400 of future Social Security gives a gap near $46,000 and a 25× target of about $1.16 million in today's dollars. Their $41,000 of idle checking could start closing it.
Budget time, and a long horizon. Nine years from 67, Monica should plan from her budget: a $957,500 target against about $736,000 projected (77% funded), or $841,000 with her catch-up contributions (88%). From the SSA table a 58-year-old woman has about a 21% chance of reaching 93, so her plan should run to the mid-90s, and it must leave room for Eli's lifetime needs (INV-084).
Budget, then stress-test. The Harpers are within ten years of retiring, so the budget is the right method. With Social Security cut to 80% and a 3.5% withdrawal rate they are about $490,000 short of the stress-tested target; one or two more working years or a later claim for Tom closes most of it.
The gap is small; the horizon is long. Ruth's portfolio needs to supply about $25,000 a year plus taxes. Her real planning question is not "enough?" but how to draw it tax-efficiently before and after her required minimum distributions (RMDs) begin at 73 (INV-081).

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A couple plans to spend $70,000 a year (taxes included). Social Security will pay them $32,000. Using the 25× rule, what nest egg do they need, in dollars?

Gap = $70,000 − $32,000 = $38,000. Target = $38,000 × 25 = $950,000. Multiplying the whole $70,000 by 25 would have given $1.75 million, overstating the need by $800,000.
Exercise 2

If you plan on a 3.5% withdrawal rate instead of 4%, what multiple of your yearly gap do you need? (Two decimal places.)

Multiple = 1 ÷ 0.035 = 28.57. A $38,000 gap would need about $1,085,700 instead of $950,000.
Exercise 3

You need $40,000 a year after tax from a traditional 401(k), and expect an average tax rate of 15% on withdrawals. How much must you withdraw before tax each year, in dollars?

Gross withdrawal = $40,000 ÷ (1 − 0.15) = $47,058.82. At 25×, that raises the target from $1,000,000 to about $1,176,500.
Exercise 4

In the SSA 2023 period life table, 79,084 of 100,000 men are alive at 65 and 19,063 at 90. What is a 65-year-old man's chance of reaching 90, in percent (one decimal)?

19,063 ÷ 79,084 = 0.241, or 24.1%. For a woman, 30,504 ÷ 87,399 gives 34.9%.
