Building a Portfolio

Most people build a portfolio backwards: they buy whatever a friend, a headline or a 401(k) enrollment form suggests, and end up with a pile of accounts instead of a plan. This module builds one forwards, in four steps — goals, allocation, funds, accounts — and then does it five times, once for each household.

The question this module answersStarting from what I have and what I want, what exactly should I own, and in which account should each piece sit?

Start here

You already own a portfolio — the question is whether you designed it

If you have a 401(k), a savings account and perhaps a brokerage app, you already have a portfolio. It may simply be an accidental one: the fund your employer picked by default, a few stocks bought on a tip, and cash that was never meant to sit for years.

Accidental portfolios are not always bad. A target-date fund chosen by default in a 401(k) is a perfectly sound portfolio for many people. The trouble starts when the pieces multiply and nobody looks at them together. The Riveras own six overlapping funds across two 401(k)s and have $41,000 sitting in checking. Jordan has 40% of their retirement savings in one company. The Harpers have $220,000 in 25 technology stocks. None of them decided on those portfolios; they accumulated them.

Building a portfolio on purpose follows the same order every time, and Figure 55.1 draws it. Each step answers one question and hands its answer to the next. Skipping a step — picking funds before you know your allocation, or choosing an allocation before you know what the money is for — is how accidental portfolios happen.

What this module covers

  1. Sorting your money by goal and time horizon, so each dollar gets the right job.
  2. Choosing an asset allocation, with 98 years of data on what each stock/bond mix did.
  3. Filling the allocation with a small number of broad, low-cost funds, such as a three-fund portfolio.
  4. Placing each fund in the right kind of account, and the order in which to fund accounts, with 2026 limits.
  5. A complete worked portfolio for Maya, the Riveras, Jordan, the Harpers and Ruth, and a builder that reproduces each one.

Step 1

Give every dollar a job and a date

A portfolio is not one pot of money. It is a set of goals that happen to be held in the same name. A dollar Jordan will spend on Ava's tuition in three years and a dollar Jordan will spend at age 75 have different jobs, and they should be invested differently even though they sit side by side on a net-worth statement.

Portfoliothe whole collection of your investments
What it is
Everything you own for future spending, across every account: workplace plans, IRAs, brokerage accounts, savings and CDs. It is measured and managed as one whole, not account by account.
Used for
Deciding your overall mix of stocks, bonds and cash, and checking that mix against your goals.
What it means for you
A 401(k) that is 100% stocks can be perfectly reasonable if your IRA holds all the bonds. What matters is the combined mix.
Time horizonwhen a goal's money will be spent
What it is
The number of years until you need the money for a particular goal — and, for retirement, the long stretch over which you will spend it.
Used for
Matching each goal to investments whose short-term swings it can survive.
What it means for you
Money needed within a few years should not depend on the stock market being up at the moment you need it. Money needed in 20 or 40 years usually should be mostly in stocks, because it has time to recover from bad years.

Separate the money that is not really "investing"

Before any allocation work, pull out three kinds of money and set them aside, because they follow their own rules:

  1. The emergency fund (INV-019). Three to six months of essential expenses is the common guideline. It lives in cash and is not part of the investment allocation. Ruth's $60,000 of CDs and savings plays this role in retirement: it can cover a year or more of the gap between her spending and her Social Security, so she never has to sell IRA investments in a bad month.
  2. Short-horizon goals. Jordan's $20,000 for Ava starts being spent in three years. It belongs in CDs, Treasury bills or a short-term bond fund — or in a 529 plan's conservative option (INV-034) — not in the retirement allocation.
  3. Debt that costs more than investing is likely to earn. Maya's student loans at 6.8% are a guaranteed "return" of 6.8% if she pays them down. Stage 3 (INV-020) covers the trade-off; the short answer is that she should capture her full employer match first, then split extra money between the loans and investing.

What is left is the long-term portfolio, and it is usually dominated by one goal: retirement. For most households, Steps 2 to 4 are about that pool.

Going furtherThe order in which to fund goals

Planners often use a priority ladder for new savings: (1) contribute enough to a workplace plan to get the full employer match, which is an immediate return no investment can beat; (2) build a starter emergency fund; (3) pay off high-interest debt such as credit cards; (4) finish the emergency fund; (5) fund Roth or pre-tax retirement accounts toward your target savings rate; (6) fund other goals such as a 529 plan; (7) invest in a taxable account. The order is a convention, not a law, and it bends to circumstances: a 529 may move up the list for a family whose child starts college soon.

Practitioner depthGoals as liabilities

Institutional investors such as pension funds start from their liabilities: a schedule of payments they owe. A household can do the same. List each goal with an amount and a date, discount it to today at a safe rate, and the result is the "liability" each part of the portfolio must fund. Money matched to near-term liabilities is invested to be there on the date (cash, bonds that mature on schedule); money for distant or flexible liabilities can take equity risk. The present-value tools in INV-002 do this arithmetic.

Present value of a goal = amount ÷ (1 + r)years    e.g. $80,000 of tuition in 3 years at 4% = 80,000 ÷ 1.043 = $71,120

Step 2

Choose the mix before you choose anything else

With the long-term pool identified, the most important single decision is how to split it between stocks, which grow faster but fall harder, and high-quality bonds, which grow slowly but cushion falls. Vanguard's researchers state the consensus plainly: "A portfolio's asset allocation is the major determinant of a portfolio's risk-and-return characteristics" (Jaconetti, Kinniry and Zilbering, 2010).

What it is
The percentage of your portfolio in each broad asset class, usually stated as stocks / bonds, for example 60/40.
Used for
Setting the portfolio's expected growth and, more importantly, how far it can fall in a bad year.
What it means for you

Three inputs set the stock share

Stage 4 covers these in depth; here is how they combine. Risk capacity is how much loss your finances can absorb: long horizons, secure jobs and pensions raise it. Risk tolerance is how much loss you can sit through without selling: that is psychological, and it is often lower than people think until they live through a crash. Risk need is how much growth your goals require: if you can reach your goal with a 50/50 mix, there is little reason to take the risk of 90/10. The allocation you choose should respect the lowest of the three.

Then split the stocks and the bonds

A stock/bond ratio is only the first cut. Two more choices finish the allocation:

  • US versus international stocks. No country leads forever (INV-004 tells the story of Japan after 1989). This module uses 30% of stocks international as its default for all five households, a moderate choice between a US-only portfolio and one weighted by each country's share of world markets. INV-054 covers the evidence and the trade-offs.
  • What kind of bonds. The bond side exists to be steady when stocks are not. That argues for high-quality, intermediate-term bonds — a total US bond market index fund or Treasuries — rather than high-yield "junk" bonds that tend to fall with stocks. Retirees may add Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (INV-010).
US stocks = portfolio × stock share × (1 − international share)
International = portfolio × stock share × international share
Bonds = portfolio × (1 − stock share)

For the Riveras' $77,000 at 80% stocks with 30% of stocks international: US stocks 77,000 × 0.8 × 0.7 = $43,120; international 77,000 × 0.8 × 0.3 = $18,480; bonds 77,000 × 0.2 = $15,400.

Going furtherRules of thumb such as "your age in bonds"

Simple formulas — "hold your age in bonds," or "110 minus your age in stocks" — are starting points that encode one true idea: risk capacity usually falls as the horizon shortens. They ignore everything else that matters, such as a pension, a very large or very small portfolio relative to spending, or a job that is itself stock-like. Target-date funds replace the formula with a professionally designed glide path (INV-050). Use a formula to sanity-check your answer, not to produce it.

Practitioner depthWhy the worst year, not the average, should decide

Investors tend to choose allocations by looking at average returns and then abandon them after large losses. Morningstar's 2025 Mind the Gap study found that the average dollar in US funds earned about 1.2 percentage points a year less than the funds themselves over the ten years ended 2024, because of poorly timed buying and selling (Ptak, 2025). A useful discipline is to translate the worst year into dollars: for the Harpers' $1.1 million, the worst year of a 60/40 mix (−27.3%) is a loss of about $300,000 on paper. If that number would make them sell, the allocation is too aggressive for them, whatever the averages say.

Step 3

Fill each slot with one broad, cheap fund

Once you know the slots — US stocks, international stocks, bonds — each needs a fund. The goal is not to find the fund that will do best next year; nobody can reliably do that. It is to own each slot as completely and cheaply as possible, so your results match the market's minus as little as possible.

Three-fund portfolio
What it is
A portfolio made of a US total stock market index fund, an international stock index fund and a US bond market index fund, held in chosen proportions.
Used for
Owning thousands of companies and bonds worldwide with three low-cost holdings that are easy to rebalance.
What it means for you
You can build a complete, diversified portfolio in any account that offers these three building blocks. Many 401(k) plans do.
Target-date fundthe one-fund alternative
What it is
A single fund holding stocks and bonds in a mix that becomes more conservative as its target year approaches.
Used for
Delegating steps 2 and 3, and rebalancing, to the fund manager.
What it means for you
Excellent for one account and one goal. Awkward if you hold several accounts, because a target-date fund cannot see the rest of your portfolio; mixing it with other funds undoes its design.

Inside a 401(k): you choose from a menu

A workplace plan offers a fixed list of funds, often 15 to 30. Read the plan's annual fee disclosure, which lists every option and its expense ratio. Then look for three things: an S&P 500 or total US market index fund, an international index fund, and a bond index fund. If the menu has them, use them. If the plan's index options are poor but it offers a reasonable target-date series, the target-date fund may be the best choice inside that plan — and you can then treat that account as a fixed block when you plan the rest of your portfolio.

ETF or mutual fund?

For the same index, an exchange-traded fund (ETF) and an index mutual fund usually behave almost identically. ETFs trade throughout the day and tend to distribute fewer capital gains in taxable accounts; mutual funds make automatic monthly investing easy. In a 401(k) you normally get mutual funds or collective trusts; in an IRA or brokerage account, either works (INV-012).

💡

Fewer funds, not more. The Riveras' six large-cap funds hold largely the same companies. Replacing them with the three building blocks lowers cost, adds bonds and international stocks they did not have, and makes rebalancing a two-minute job. Morningstar found that investors in all-in-one allocation funds captured almost all of their funds' returns (a gap of about 0.1 point a year), while sector-fund investors lost about 1.5 points a year to bad timing (Ptak, 2025).

Going furtherCore and satellite

Some investors want room for a few individual stocks, a sector they believe in, or a values-based fund (INV-053). A disciplined way to do it is core and satellite: keep the large majority, often 90% or more, in the broad index core, and cap the satellites at a small, fixed share you would not miss if it went to zero. Jordan's employer stock is a satellite that grew too big; the Harpers' 25 technology stocks are a satellite that became the whole taxable account.

Step 4

Put each fund in the account where it is taxed least

The same fund can be held in three kinds of account, and each taxes it differently. Pre-tax accounts (traditional 401(k), 403(b), traditional IRA) give a deduction now and tax every dollar withdrawn later as ordinary income. Roth accounts give no deduction but tax nothing on qualified withdrawals. Taxable brokerage accounts tax interest and dividends each year and gains when you sell. Because the accounts differ, where you hold each piece of the allocation changes what you keep.

Asset locationnot the same as asset allocation
What it is
Deciding which account holds which investment, while the overall allocation stays the same.
Used for
Reducing the tax drag on a portfolio without changing its risk.
What it means for you
With money in more than one kind of account, a sensible location can add to after-tax returns for free. INV-036 gives the full evidence; this step applies its usual defaults.

Practitioner depthPre-tax dollars are not the same as Roth dollars

A dollar in a pre-tax account is partly owed to the IRS; a Roth dollar is entirely yours. Some advisors therefore compare allocations on an after-tax basis — multiplying pre-tax balances by (1 − expected tax rate in retirement). With the Harpers' $820,000 pre-tax and an assumed 22% future rate, the after-tax value is about $640,000. Placing all the bonds in the pre-tax account makes the after-tax portfolio slightly more stock-heavy than the pre-tax numbers suggest. The simple dollar-for-dollar approach used in this module is standard practice and is fine for most households; just know that the adjustment exists.

From plan to trades

Inside 401(k)s and IRAs, moving to the new portfolio costs no tax: exchange the old funds for the new ones on the same day. In taxable accounts, change gradually and tax-aware, as Jordan and the Harpers do. Write the target mix, the funds, the rebalancing rule and the date of the next review into an investment policy statement (INV-028). From then on the portfolio needs three habits: direct new money to whatever is underweight, rebalance when a band is breached (INV-056), and review once a year (INV-058).

Real rules, real plans

How the largest portfolios default

Case study 1The Labor Department's default-investment rule (2007)
What happened
In October 2007 the Department of Labor issued a regulation (29 CFR 2550.404c-5, implementing section 404(c)(5) of the Employee Retirement Income Security Act) describing "qualified default investment alternatives" (QDIAs): where a 401(k) may place money for workers who never choose. It named three long-term types — a target-date or life-cycle fund, a balanced fund with a risk level suitable for the plan's participants as a whole, and a professionally managed account — and allowed a capital-preservation fund only for the first 120 days after a worker's first contribution (apart from a grandfathered stable-value option for money invested in it before December 24, 2007). Plans generally must give notice at least 30 days before eligibility and at least 30 days before each later plan year.
What it teaches
Regulators decided that the right default for long-term retirement money is a diversified mix of stocks and bonds, adjusted for age or risk — exactly Steps 2 and 3 of this module — and that cash is only a temporary parking place.
Source
29 CFR § 2550.404c-5(e)(4) (72 FR 60478, Oct. 24, 2007), Fiduciary relief for investments in qualified default investment alternatives (Legal Information Institute, Cornell Law School).
Case study 2The federal Thrift Savings Plan: five index funds, one default
What happened
The Thrift Savings Plan (TSP), the federal employees' 401(k)-style plan, offers five core funds — G (government securities), F (fixed income index), C (common stock index), S (small cap stock index) and I (international stock index) — plus Lifecycle (L) funds that invest only in those five. Until September 2015, automatically enrolled workers defaulted into the G Fund; the Smart Savings Act (Public Law 113-255) changed the default to an age-appropriate L Fund. By December 31, 2025, 53% of accounts of active employees under the Federal Employees Retirement System held at least one L Fund and 37% were invested only in L Funds; the youngest participants held just 1.35% of their assets in the G Fund.
What it teaches
A plan covering millions of workers is built from a handful of broad index funds, and a sensible default changed how the youngest savers are invested. A household can copy the design: a few index funds, or one target-date fund, chosen on purpose.
Source
Federal Retirement Thrift Investment Board (2026). Annual report of the Thrift Savings Plan required by §105 of the TSP Enhancement Act of 2009 (June 30, 2026).
Case study 3Morningstar's Mind the Gap, 2025
What happened
Morningstar estimated that the average dollar in US mutual funds and ETFs earned 7.0% a year over the ten years ended December 31, 2024, while the funds themselves returned 8.2% — a 1.2-point yearly gap, equal to about 15% of the funds' return, caused by when investors bought and sold. The gap was about 0.1 point for allocation funds and 1.5 points for sector funds.
What it teaches
How a portfolio is built changes how its owner behaves. Fewer, broader holdings and automatic rebalancing leave fewer occasions for costly decisions.
Source
Ptak, J. (2025, August 13). Mind the Gap 2025. Morningstar Portfolio and Planning Research.

Decisions

Three households, three construction problems

Scenario 1 · The Riveras' $41,000

The Riveras have $41,000 in checking earning almost nothing, beyond their $9,000 emergency fund. Sofia is 7 and Leo is 4. What should the money do?

All into the 401(k)sThey cannot simply deposit a lump sum into a 401(k); contributions come from payroll, up to $24,500 each for 2026. They could raise their payroll deferrals and spend the cash instead, which works but leaves the college goal unfunded.
Give each dollar a jobRight. Step 1 first: the reserve, then Roth IRAs (tax-free growth, contributions withdrawable in an emergency), then 529s, whose tax-free growth for education is decades long for Leo. The Roth money follows the 80/20 plan.
Wait for a dipMarket timing rarely works (INV-052). Cash earning almost nothing loses to inflation every year it waits; this is the "cash drag" of INV-007.
The principle: idle money is a decision too; assign it to goals in priority order.
Scenario 2 · Jordan's allocation

Jordan counts Ava's $20,000 college fund and the $56,000 of employer stock as part of a 70/30 retirement portfolio. The employer stock is "stocks," so the portfolio looks close to target. Is it?

Stocks are stocksOne company is not "stocks." Its risk is far higher than a total-market fund's, and it is tied to Jordan's paycheck (INV-004).
Separate and capRight. Take the college money out of the retirement plan entirely; then treat employer stock as a satellite limited to about 10% of the portfolio, and reach the 70/30 target with index funds.
The principle: an allocation only means something if the pieces in each slot are diversified and share the same goal.
Scenario 3 · The Harpers' technology stocks

The Harpers want their $220,000 taxable account to hold the international fund in their new plan, but selling the technology stocks at once would realize large capital gains. What is the best path?

Sell everything nowRemoves the concentration at once, but a large one-year gain can push part of the gain into the 20% long-term rate (above $613,700 of taxable income for joint filers in 2026) and add the net investment income tax (INV-030). Spreading sales over years usually costs less.
Never sellAvoids tax but leaves a quarter of their savings in one sector just before retirement. Holding low-basis stock until death gets a step-up in basis for heirs (INV-090), but that is an estate strategy, not a diversification plan.
Transition over yearsRight. The pre-tax 401(k)s can be adjusted instantly with no tax, so the overall allocation can be close to target from day one even while the taxable account changes slowly.
The principle: manage the allocation across all accounts at once; fix the tax-free accounts immediately and the taxable one patiently.
