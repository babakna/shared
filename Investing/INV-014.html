Alternatives and Private Markets

Private equity, private credit and hedge funds used to be for pension funds and the very wealthy. New products now offer them to ordinary investors and, increasingly, 401(k) plans. This module explains how these investments work, who is allowed to buy them, what they cost, and why their smooth reported returns can mislead.

The question this module answersWhat am I actually buying when I invest outside the public markets, and is the extra cost and lock-up worth it?

Start here

The investments you cannot sell tomorrow

Everything in Stage 1 traded on public markets: you can see a price every second and sell with a tap. A large share of the world's companies, loans and buildings is owned privately instead, through funds that buy stakes in businesses, lend to companies directly or own real estate, including non-traded real estate investment trusts (REITs), and that report their value only occasionally.

The pitch is easy to understand. University endowments and pension funds hold a lot of private equity and hedge funds; their reported returns often look steady; and the managers argue that patient capital earns a premium for giving up the ability to sell. Some of that is real. But the same features that make these investments look attractive, infrequent prices and locked-in money, also hide risk and make high fees hard to see. The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) sets the rules on who may buy these investments and how the retail versions must let investors out.

Buying companies off the market

Private equity and venture capital

A private equity firm raises a fund from investors, uses that money (often plus substantial borrowing) to buy whole companies or large stakes, tries to improve them, and sells them years later. Venture capital does the same with young, fast-growing companies. The investor is a limited partner; the firm running the fund is the general partner.

Commitment and capital call
What it is
You commit a sum, say $250,000, but pay nothing up front. The manager sends capital calls over the first several years as it finds deals, and returns cash as it sells companies. A fund commonly runs about ten years, sometimes longer.
Used for
Matching investors' money to deals as they occur, so cash is not idle.
What it means for you
You must hold cash ready for calls you cannot time, and you cannot sell your stake except, occasionally, at a discount to specialist buyers.

What has private equity actually delivered? A landmark study, by Robert Harris, Tim Jenkinson and Steven Kaplan in the Journal of Finance (2014), used cash-flow data on nearly 1,400 US buyout and venture funds from Burgiss, a data provider sourced from over 200 institutional investors. They found that average US buyout funds outperformed the S&P 500 by 20% to 27% over the life of the fund, more than 3% a year. Venture capital funds outperformed public equities in the 1990s but underperformed in the 2000s.

Public market equivalentthe fair benchmark
What it is
A comparison that invests each of a fund's cash flows in a public index on the same dates. A ratio above 1.0 means the fund beat the index.
Used for
Measuring private funds fairly, because their money goes in and out at irregular times.
What it means for you
Ask any private fund manager for results compared with an index this way, not just an internal rate of return (IRR), which can look high on small, early sales.
Going furtherWhy the average is not what you will get

Averages across 1,400 funds include the best-known firms, which often accept only large institutions. Results across funds are widely spread, and an investor buying through a retail feeder fund pays an extra layer of fees on top. The research question "did buyouts beat the index on average?" is different from "will the fund I can buy beat an index fund after all its fees?"

Practitioner depthWhy IRR flatters

Internal rate of return is the discount rate that sets a fund's cash flows' present value to zero. A fund that returns 1.5 times the money within two years shows a very high IRR on a small base; it says nothing about how long the rest of your commitment will be tied up. Harris, Jenkinson and Kaplan report that, within a vintage year, a fund's multiple of invested capital and its IRR predict its performance relative to public markets well; look at all three measures together.

Lending and "absolute return"

Private credit, hedge funds and "2 and 20"

Private credit means loans made to companies by funds rather than banks, usually floating-rate loans to mid-sized businesses. It has grown quickly. A Federal Reserve staff note (August 2026) estimates that private credit and leveraged loans were each about $1.4 trillion at the end of 2025, together about 45% of lending to private nonfinancial companies. The appeal is a higher interest rate than public bonds; the trade-offs are less liquidity, less transparency and credit risk that is tested only in a downturn.

A hedge fund is a lightly regulated pooled fund that can use strategies ordinary funds cannot: short selling, heavy borrowing, derivatives and concentrated bets. "Hedge" is a history, not a promise; many take large market risk. The SEC's investor bulletin lists the terms to expect:

TermWhat the SEC says is typical
Management fee1–2% of net asset value a year
Performance fee15–20% of profits, often subject to a high-water mark and a hurdle rate
RedemptionsFour times a year or fewer
Lock-upOften one year or more, during which you cannot cash in
SuspensionManagers may suspend redemptions, including in times of market distress

Source: Investor.gov (SEC Office of Investor Education and Advocacy), "Hedge Funds."

Performance fee, high-water mark and hurdle
What it is
A performance fee is the manager's share of profits. A high-water mark means it is charged only on gains above the fund's previous peak. A hurdle rate is a minimum return that must be earned first.
Used for
Aligning the manager with investors, at least in theory.
What it means for you
You share the upside with the manager but keep all of the downside. The SEC notes that a performance fee could motivate a manager to take greater risks.

At the defaults, a fund earning 10% a year before fees delivers about 6.4% to its investors after 2% and 20%. Over ten years $250,000 grows to about $465,000, against about $645,000 in an index fund earning the same 10% before a 0.05% fee. Fees and their lost compounding absorb about 46% of the gain. To merely tie the index fund, the manager's investments must earn about 14.4% a year before fees, beating the market by more than four percentage points every year.

Going furtherPrivate credit in a downturn

Most private credit loans pay a floating rate, so income rises when short-term rates rise. That also raises the borrowers' interest bills. The Federal Reserve staff note cited above estimates the market at about $1.4 trillion, roughly the size of the leveraged loan market; unlike those loans, private loans are rarely traded, so losses show up in reported values slowly, the smoothing problem of the next tab.

Practitioner depthThe effect of a hurdle

Take a fund earning 10% before fees with a 2% management fee and 20% performance fee. Without a hurdle, the investor keeps (10 − 2) × 0.8 = 6.4%. With an 8% hurdle measured after the management fee, the value after that fee has grown exactly 8%, so no performance fee is due and the investor keeps 8%. Only gains above the hurdle are shared. Fund documents define whether the hurdle applies before or after the management fee and whether a "catch-up" later gives the manager its full share; read them.

Net return ≈ (gross return − management fee) × (1 − performance fee share), when above the hurdle and high-water mark

Why the line looks calm

A price that is estimated, not traded

A public stock's price is set by thousands of trades a day. A private company, an office building or a loan held by a private fund has no daily trade. Its value is estimated periodically by the manager or an appraiser, who looks at recent sales and models. Those estimates move slowly and lag the market. The result is smoothing: reported returns that swing less, and seem less related to stocks, than the investments truly are.

Return smoothing
What it is
The tendency of appraisal-based or model-based values to understate the true ups and downs of illiquid investments, spreading each shock over several reporting periods.
Used for
Understanding why private funds report low volatility and low correlation with public markets.
What it means for you
Low reported volatility is not low risk. When you need the money in a crisis, the price someone will actually pay may be far below the last reported value.

Mila Getmansky, Andrew Lo and Igor Makarov studied 908 hedge funds and found their returns were often highly serially correlated: one month's return predicted the next, unlike stocks. The most likely explanation was illiquidity. They showed that for portfolios of illiquid securities, reported returns tend to be smoother than true economic returns, understating volatility and inflating risk-adjusted measures such as the Sharpe ratio (Journal of Financial Economics, 2004).

With the defaults, the same S&P 500 returns for 2000–2025, reported through a simple smoothing rule, show volatility of 9.8% instead of 17.8% and a worst year of −14.9% instead of −36.5%, and the Sharpe ratio rises from 0.43 to 0.62. Nothing about the investments changed; only the way they were valued.

Going furtherWhy smoothing distorts portfolio advice

Portfolio models reward assets with high return per unit of volatility and low correlation with stocks. Feed them smoothed data and they recommend large private allocations. Correct for smoothing and much of the apparent diversification disappears, because private companies and buildings are exposed to the same economy as public ones.

Practitioner depthUnsmoothing a series

If reported rt = (1 − w) Rt + w rt−1, the true return can be estimated as Rt = (rt − w rt−1) ÷ (1 − w), with w estimated from the reported series' first-order autocorrelation. For this one-lag model the reported standard deviation is roughly √[(1 − w) ÷ (1 + w)] times the true one, about half at w = 0.6. Getmansky, Lo and Makarov generalize this to several lags and derive a smoothing-adjusted Sharpe ratio.

Private assets for everyone

Interval funds, non-traded REITs and the 401(k)

Because traditional private funds require large minimums and investor qualifications, firms have built products that package private assets for ordinary investors. Each solves the access problem by promising partial, scheduled liquidity. Each should be judged on what happens when many investors want out at once.

Interval fundSEC Rule 23c-3
What it is
A registered closed-end fund that makes repurchase offers at fixed intervals of three, six or twelve months, for between 5% and 25% of its shares, at net asset value. It may deduct a repurchase fee of no more than 2% of the proceeds.
Used for
Holding private credit, real estate and other less liquid assets while registered with the SEC and open to non-accredited investors.
What it means for you
If requests exceed the amount offered, you receive only part of your request. "Quarterly liquidity" is a ceiling, not a guarantee.

Some closed-end funds instead offer to repurchase shares only when their boards decide to (often called tender-offer funds), so there is no fixed schedule at all.

Non-traded REIT
What it is
A real estate investment trust registered with the SEC but not listed on an exchange.
Used for
Selling real estate exposure through brokers, usually emphasizing high distributions and stable values.
What it means for you
Among the risks the SEC's investor bulletin lists: shares cannot readily be sold and a liquidity event may not come until more than 10 years after you invest; up-front fees can reach 15% of the offering price; distributions may be paid from offering proceeds (your own money) and borrowings; and values rest on periodic appraisals that may not be accurate or timely.

Private assets in 401(k) plans

On August 7, 2025, the President signed Executive Order 14330, "Democratizing Access to Alternative Assets for 401(k) Investors." It directs the Secretary of Labor, within 180 days, to reexamine the Department of Labor's guidance on a fiduciary's duties when offering an asset allocation fund that includes alternative assets, to consider rescinding the Department's December 21, 2021 supplemental statement on private equity, and to clarify the criteria fiduciaries should use to balance potentially higher expenses against the goals of greater long-term net returns and broader diversification. Check the Department of Labor's site for the rules and guidance that followed. For a participant, the practical form is most likely a target-date or balanced fund with a slice of private assets, not a direct private fund.

Going furtherQuestions to ask about any retail private-markets product
  • What are all the layers of fees: the product's, the underlying funds', any performance fees and sales charges?
  • How exactly can I get money out, how much at a time, and who can suspend or limit that?
  • How are the holdings valued, by whom, and how often?
  • What would have happened to my request in 2008, 2020 or 2022?
Practitioner depthWhat Rule 23c-3 requires

Under the rule, an interval fund repurchases shares for cash at net asset value on the repurchase pricing date and must pay by a set payment deadline. The repurchase offer amount, between 5% and 25% of shares outstanding, is set before each offer, and the only fee it may deduct from proceeds is a repurchase fee of no more than 2%, meant to cover the costs of the repurchase. The fund's prospectus states its interval and its policy on the offer amount.

The SEC's gates

Accredited investors and qualified purchasers

Companies and funds that sell securities must register them with the SEC or use an exemption. Many private offerings, including most private equity and hedge funds, rely on exemptions that allow sales only to investors who meet wealth or knowledge tests. The theory is that such investors can bear the risk and evaluate offerings without the protection of full SEC disclosure.

Accredited investorRule 501(a) of Regulation D, for individuals
What it is
An individual qualifies by any one of these: income over $200,000 in each of the two most recent years ($300,000 jointly with a spouse or spousal equivalent) with a reasonable expectation of the same this year; net worth over $1,000,000, alone or jointly, excluding the primary residence; holding a Series 7, Series 65 or Series 82 license in good standing; or being a "knowledgeable employee" of the private fund.
Used for
Deciding who may buy private placements, hedge funds, many private equity funds and private REITs.
What it means for you
It is a gate, not a recommendation. The dollar amounts are fixed in the rule text, so more households cross them over time simply through inflation.

The net worth test counts a mortgage on your home only to the extent it exceeds the home's value (or rose in the 60 days before the purchase, other than to buy the home). The SEC's August 2020 amendments added the professional licenses and the knowledgeable-employee category, and allowed spousal equivalents to combine finances.

Qualified purchaserInvestment Company Act of 1940, section 2(a)(51)
What it is
A natural person who owns at least $5,000,000 in investments (with other tests for family companies, trusts and institutions).
Used for
Access to private funds open only to qualified purchasers, often the largest hedge and private equity funds.
What it means for you
A higher bar than accredited status, and still a test of wealth, not of whether an investment suits you.
Going furtherEntities and trusts

Rule 501(a) also lists entities: for example, corporations, partnerships, limited liability companies and certain nonprofits with total assets over $5,000,000 not formed to buy the securities offered; trusts with over $5,000,000 directed by a sophisticated person; and other entities owning investments over $5,000,000. Family offices and their clients have their own category.

Practitioner depthHow the home and its mortgage are counted

The rule excludes the primary residence as an asset. Debt secured by the home, up to its estimated fair market value, is not counted as a liability, except that any increase in that debt in the 60 days before the purchase (other than to buy the home) counts. Debt above the home's value counts as a liability. A cash-out refinance just before investing therefore does not raise your net worth for the test.

Real history

Two lessons in liquidity and leverage

Case study 1Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM), 1998
What happened
Founded in February 1994 by former Salomon Brothers vice chairman John Meriwether, with two Nobel laureates among its team, LTCM bet that price gaps between similar securities would close. Each trade earned little, so the fund borrowed heavily: at the end of 1997 it held about $30 of debt for every $1 of capital. After Russia devalued its currency and stopped paying its debt in August 1998, the gaps widened instead; the fund lost 44% of its value in August alone. On September 23, 1998, fourteen banks and brokerage firms, brought together by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, put up $3.625 billion for 90% of the fund. The Federal Reserve did not put its own money at risk.
What it teaches
Sophistication is not safety. A "market-neutral" strategy with heavy leverage can lose most of its capital in weeks when many investors rush for the same exits.
Source
Federal Reserve History, "Near Failure of Long-Term Capital Management."
Case study 2Blackstone Real Estate Income Trust (BREIT) limits withdrawals, 2022
What happened
BREIT, one of the largest non-traded REITs, allows repurchases of up to 2% of its net asset value (NAV) a month and 5% a quarter. In November 2022 requests exceeded both limits. BREIT's prospectus supplement filed with the SEC on December 15, 2022 reported that it fulfilled requests up to the 2% monthly limit, about $1.3 billion, which was about 43% of the shares submitted, and that only about 0.3% of NAV was available for repurchase in December under the quarterly limit.
What it teaches
The limits worked exactly as the documents said: they protected remaining investors from a forced sale of buildings. But investors who assumed they could leave whenever they wished learned that "monthly liquidity" meant "monthly, if not too many others want out."
Source
Blackstone Real Estate Income Trust, Inc., prospectus supplement (Form 424B3) filed with the SEC, December 15, 2022.

Decisions

Three offers, three households

Scenario 1 · The Harpers

A wealth manager offers the Harpers a private equity feeder fund: $250,000 minimum, a ten-year life, a 2% management fee plus 20% of profits, plus the feeder's own fee. They qualify by net worth. They plan to retire in five or six years. What should they do?

Commit $250,000That is more than their entire $220,000 taxable account and about 23% of their savings, locked up past their retirement date, with calls they must meet in cash. Buyout funds have beaten the S&P 500 on average in research, but the feeder's extra fee layer and the spread of results make that average an uncertain guide.
Decline or go smallA sensible course. Their bigger, surer gains are in diversifying 25 technology stocks, planning Roth conversions and cutting their 1.25% advisory fee (INV-007, INV-038).
Use the 401(k)sTheir plans almost certainly do not offer this fund, and withdrawing to fund it would trigger income tax and, before 59½, an additional 10% tax.
The principle: being allowed to invest is not a reason to invest; money you may need within ten years does not belong in a ten-year fund.
Scenario 2 · Ruth

Ruth keeps part of her savings in certificates of deposit (CDs). A broker offers her a non-traded REIT paying a 6% distribution, "twice what your CDs pay, with a stable share price." She is thinking of putting in $100,000 from her individual retirement account (IRA). What should she ask?

Take the 6%The SEC warns distributions may come from investors' own money and borrowing, fees can reach 15% up front, and a "stable" price may reflect infrequent appraisal. Ruth also needs cash from her IRA for required distributions starting in 2031.
Ask the questionsRight. A listed REIT index fund offers real estate exposure she can sell any trading day, at a small fraction of the cost (INV-069).
The principle: a distribution is not a return; judge total return, fees and your ability to get out.
Scenario 3 · The Riveras

Their 401(k) plan adds a new target-date fund that holds about 10% in private credit and private equity. Its expense ratio is higher than the plan's existing index target-date fund. Should they switch?

Switch at onceA difference in cost compounds for 30 years (INV-007). The new fund's promise of higher returns is exactly what the extra fee must pay for.
Compare firstRight. The plan's fiduciaries must choose prudently, but the Riveras still choose between options. If they cannot tell what the private sleeve adds after fees, the simpler fund is a fine default.
The principle: new access is not the same as a better investment; compare after all costs.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A hedge fund earns 12% before fees on $1,000,000 in a year. It charges 2% of starting assets and then 20% of the remaining gain (no hurdle, at a new high). What is the investor's net return, in percent?

Gross gain $120,000; management fee $20,000; remaining gain $100,000; performance fee $20,000. Net gain $80,000 = 8%. Fees took a third of the gross gain.
Exercise 2

A couple's joint income was $280,000 and $290,000 in the last two years. They own a home worth $700,000 with a $400,000 mortgage, $900,000 of investments, a rental property with $250,000 of equity and a $30,000 car loan. What is their net worth for the accredited investor test, in dollars?

Exclude the primary residence and its mortgage (the mortgage is below the home's value). $900,000 + $250,000 − $30,000 = $1,120,000. They qualify by net worth even though their income is below the $300,000 joint test.
Exercise 3

An interval fund with 10,000,000 shares offers to repurchase 5% of them. Holders ask to sell 1,250,000 shares in total, including your 2,000. The fund repurchases only the amount offered, pro rata. How many of your shares are bought back?

Offer: 5% × 10,000,000 = 500,000 shares. Fill ratio 500,000 ÷ 1,250,000 = 40%. You sell 2,000 × 0.40 = 800 shares and wait for the next window for the rest.
Exercise 4

A fund's reported return follows: reported = 0.5 × true return + 0.5 × last period's reported return. Last period it reported +5%. This period the true return is −37%. What does it report, in percent?

0.5 × (−37) + 0.5 × 5 = −18.5 + 2.5 = −16%. The other half of the loss will appear in later periods.
