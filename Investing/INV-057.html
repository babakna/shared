Measuring Performance

"How am I doing?" sounds like one question. It is really three: how did my investments do, how did my money do, and was it good enough given the risk? Each has its own number. This module teaches all of them, shows how to build a fair yardstick, and walks through an account statement line by line.

The question this module answersWhat return did I actually earn, compared with what, and was the risk worth it?

Start here

The balance went up. Did you do well?

Most people judge their investments by the balance. If it went up, things are fine. That works surprisingly often, and it fails in exactly the cases that matter.

A balance rises for two reasons: the investments grew, or you added money. A 401(k) that went from $80,000 to $95,000 in a year may have earned 10%, or it may have lost money while $20,000 of contributions made up the difference. Even once you separate the two, "10%" means little on its own. In a year when a comparable mix of index funds earned 20%, 10% is poor; in a year when it lost 15%, 10% is remarkable.

So measurement needs three things: a return that is computed correctly, a benchmark that is a fair comparison, and some account of the risk taken to get the result. Figure 57.1 shows how they fit together.

What this module covers

  1. Annualizing returns so periods of different length can be compared.
  2. Time-weighted versus money-weighted returns, with a calculator that includes deposits.
  3. Choosing a benchmark, and building a blended benchmark to match your allocation.
  4. Risk-adjusted measures: the Sharpe ratio, the Sortino ratio and tracking error.
  5. Reading an account statement, and checking a return yourself.

A common unit

Turn any period's return into a yearly rate

A statement might show 3.1% for the quarter, 18% since the account was opened four years ago, and 62% "since inception" for a fund. They cannot be compared until they are put in the same unit: a compound yearly rate.

Annualized returnalso called compound annual growth rate
What it is
The constant yearly rate that, compounded, produces the same total growth over the period. It is a geometric average, not a simple average of yearly returns.
Used for
Comparing results over periods of different length, and comparing a portfolio with its benchmark.
What it means for you
"Up 50% over five years" is 8.45% a year, not 10%. The simple average overstates what compounding delivers.
Annualized return = (1 + total return)1 / years − 1    e.g. (1.50)1/5 − 1 = 8.45% a year
Going furtherNever annualize a short period

A fund that rose 6% in three months has not "earned 26% a year" (1.064 − 1). Annualizing a short burst assumes it repeats, which it almost never does. The SEC's marketing rule for investment advisers requires performance to be shown for one-, five- and ten-year periods when it is advertised (17 CFR 275.206(4)-1(d)(2)), in part to stop cherry-picked short windows. For your own records, report returns for periods of a year or more as annualized, and shorter periods as simple, unannualized figures.

The key distinction

The fund's return and your return are different numbers

Time-weighted return
What it is
The return of the investments themselves, found by computing the return of each period between cash flows and linking them: (1 + r1)(1 + r2)… − 1. Deposits and withdrawals have no effect on it.
Used for
Judging a fund or a manager who does not control when clients add or remove money. Published fund returns are time-weighted.
What it means for you
Compare your funds with their benchmarks using this number.
Money-weighted returninternal rate of return
What it is
The single yearly rate that makes your starting balance plus every deposit, each growing from its own date, equal your ending balance. Dollars invested for longer, or in bigger amounts, count more.
Used for
Measuring what your own money earned, including the effect of when you added or withdrew it. Many brokers show it as "personal rate of return."
What it means for you
This is the number your retirement plan runs on. If it lags the time-weighted return, your timing cost you.

A two-year example

You invest $10,000. Year 1 returns +20%, so you have $12,000. Encouraged, you add $50,000 at the start of year 2. Year 2 returns −10%, and you end with $62,000 × 0.9 = $55,800 — less than the $60,000 you put in.

  • Time-weighted: 1.20 × 0.90 = 1.08, or 8% over two years: +3.92% a year. The investment itself did fine.
  • Money-weighted: solve 10,000(1 + r)2 + 50,000(1 + r) = 55,800. The answer is −6.05% a year. Most of your money arrived just before the bad year.
Money-weighted r solves: V0(1 + r)T + Σ Dt(1 + r)T − t = VT

At the default settings the four yearly returns link to +30.4%, or +6.86% a year time-weighted. But the $50,000 deposit arrived just before the −10% year, so your dollars earned only +3.73% a year. Move the deposit to year 3 or year 4 and watch the money-weighted return rise above the time-weighted one: the same funds, a different investor experience.

Going furtherA quick approximation you can do on a statement

When you know only the beginning and ending values and the net cash flow for a period, the simple Dietz method approximates the money-weighted return by assuming the flows arrived halfway through:

Return ≈ (End − Begin − Net flows) ÷ (Begin + ½ × Net flows)

For a year that began at $250,000, received $12,000 of contributions and ended at $284,800: (284,800 − 250,000 − 12,000) ÷ (250,000 + 6,000) = 22,800 ÷ 256,000 = 8.9%. The modified Dietz method weights each flow by the fraction of the period it was invested and is what many statements use.

Practitioner depthWhy published fund returns must be time-weighted

A fund manager does not decide when shareholders buy or sell. If fund returns were money-weighted, a manager whose fund attracted large inflows just before a downturn would look worse than one who happened to get inflows before a rally, with identical skill. Time-weighting removes that noise. The reverse is true for you: you do decide when to add money, so the money-weighted return is the fair measure of your decisions. The difference between the two, summed over all fund investors, is what Morningstar's Mind the Gap study measures (Case studies tab).

Compared with what?

A fair yardstick matches what you own

Benchmark
What it is
A market index, or a mix of indexes, that represents the investments you could have owned passively instead. A US total stock fund is compared with a total US market index; a bond fund with a broad bond index.
Used for
Separating what the market gave from what a fund, manager or decision added or cost.
What it means for you
Comparing a 60/40 portfolio with the S&P 500 alone is unfair in both directions: it looks terrible in bull markets and brilliant in crashes. The yardstick must carry the same risk.
Blended benchmark
What it is
A weighted mix of indexes in the same proportions as your target allocation, rebalanced to those weights.
Used for
Judging a multi-asset portfolio, an advisor or a target-date fund against the simplest low-cost alternative with the same allocation.
What it means for you
If you could have bought the three index funds yourself, the blended benchmark minus a few hundredths of a percent in fees is what you should expect at minimum from anyone you pay.
Blended benchmark return = Σ (target weight × index return)    e.g. 0.42 × 20% + 0.18 × 10% + 0.40 × 3% = 11.4%

Choosing the pieces

You ownA fair benchmarkCommon mistake
US total stock market fundA total US stock market indexComparing with the Nasdaq after a tech rally
International stock fundA developed-plus-emerging markets index outside the USComparing with the S&P 500
Bond fundA broad US investment-grade bond indexComparing with stocks, or with a savings rate
Target-date fundThe fund's own published composite benchmarkComparing with a different company's glide path
Whole portfolioBlended benchmark at your target weightsComparing with whatever did best this year
Going furtherBenchmarks and costs

Indexes have no expenses; real funds do. A total-market index fund charging 0.03% should trail its index by about that much, year after year. A persistent shortfall much larger than the expense ratio suggests poor tracking. For an actively managed fund or an advisor, the fair test is net of all fees against the benchmark: the SEC's marketing rule forbids advisers from advertising gross performance unless net performance is shown with at least equal prominence (17 CFR 275.206(4)-1(d)(1)).

Return per unit of risk

Sharpe, Sortino and tracking error

Two portfolios can earn the same return with very different rides. Risk-adjusted measures ask how much return you received for each unit of risk you bore. All three below are built on standard deviation, the typical size of yearly swings.

Sharpe ratioWilliam Sharpe, 1966; restated 1994
What it is
The average excess return over a risk-free rate (usually Treasury bills), divided by the standard deviation of that excess return. Sharpe originally called it the "reward-to-variability ratio."
Used for
Comparing strategies with different risk levels on one scale.
What it means for you
A riskier portfolio should earn more; the Sharpe ratio tells you whether it earned enough more to justify the extra swings.
Sharpe = average(Rp − Rf) ÷ standard deviation(Rp − Rf)
Sortino ratioSortino and Price, 1994
What it is
Like the Sharpe ratio, but the denominator counts only shortfalls: years when the return fell below a minimum acceptable return (here, the T-bill rate). This is the downside deviation.
Used for
Judging strategies whose ups and downs are lopsided, since investors mind losses far more than surprise gains.
What it means for you
Two funds with the same Sharpe ratio can differ on Sortino; the higher one delivered its return with fewer and smaller bad years.
Downside deviation = √[ Σ min(0, Rp − Rf)2 ÷ n ]    Sortino = average(Rp − Rf) ÷ downside deviation
Tracking error
What it is
The standard deviation of the difference between a portfolio's return and its benchmark's, period by period.
Used for
Measuring how far a portfolio typically strays from its yardstick. An index fund should have very low tracking error; an active fund, a higher one.
What it means for you
High tracking error means your results will often look very different from the market's, in both directions. You need the stomach for that, and a reason to expect the differences to be positive on balance.
Tracking error = standard deviation(Rp − Rb)    Information ratio = annualized (Rp − Rb) ÷ tracking error
Practitioner depthMeasurement choices change the answer

Sharpe himself noted that the ratio depends on the measurement interval: with independent returns, a ratio measured over T periods is roughly √T times the single-period ratio, so he recommended measuring over short intervals such as months and then annualizing (Sharpe, 1994). The tool here uses calendar years because that is the data available. Two further cautions: ratios estimated from a few years of data are very noisy, and Sharpe ratios can be flattered by strategies with rare but large losses that have not happened yet in the sample. Use them to compare like with like over long periods, never as a single deciding number.

The document you already get

Reading an account statement line by line

Brokerage firms must send customers a statement at least once every calendar quarter showing positions, balances and account activity (FINRA Rule 2231); most send one monthly. FINRA's investor guide lists the sections below and urges you to review each statement as soon as it arrives.

What to look for, section by section

Income summaryDividends and interest, and their sourcesWhether income was reinvested or paid out as you intended
FeesAdvisory, account and transaction chargesThat they match your agreement (INV-007)

⚠️

A red flag worth memorizing. FINRA: "Be wary if your statement always shows the same rate of return, regardless of market conditions, and especially if the return is above market norms. This can be a sign of fraud." Report any error or unauthorized activity in writing to both your brokerage firm and the clearing firm listed on the statement (INV-062).

When measurement went wrong

Three stories about the right number

Case study 1The Beardstown Ladies, 1998
What happened
An Illinois investment club became famous in the 1990s for a reported 23.4% average annual return. In 1998, after questions were raised, an audit by Price Waterhouse found the club had actually earned 9.1% a year over 1984–1993, below the stock market's roughly 15% with dividends reinvested. Time magazine reported that the club "were making incorrect entries into their computer. Nobody double-checked the math."
What it teaches
A return that is never checked against a benchmark, or computed independently, is a story, not a measurement. The simplest audit — would an index fund have done better? — would have shown the problem at once.
Source
Associated Press, "Beardstown Ladies sorry for slip-up," Deseret News (March 18, 1998); "Jail the Beardstown Ladies!", Time (March 30, 1998).
Case study 2Morningstar's Mind the Gap: time- versus money-weighted, at scale
What happened
Morningstar compares each fund's time-weighted total return with a money-weighted "investor return" computed from monthly flows. For the ten years ended December 31, 2024, US mutual funds and ETFs returned 8.2% a year while the average dollar invested in them earned 7.0%: a 1.2-point gap, about 15% of the funds' return. Among US equity funds the gap was 0.6 point; among sector funds, 1.5 points.
What it teaches
Both numbers are correct. The difference is the cost of buying after gains and selling after losses. Your money-weighted return is the one that pays for retirement.
Source
Ptak, J. (2025, August 13). Mind the Gap 2025. Morningstar Portfolio and Planning Research.
Case study 3The SEC's marketing rule, 2020
What happened
On December 22, 2020, the Securities and Exchange Commission adopted a new marketing rule for registered investment advisers. Among other things, an adviser may not show gross performance "unless the advertisement also presents net performance with at least equal prominence," and performance must be shown for one-, five- and ten-year periods ending no earlier than the most recent calendar year-end.
What it teaches
The rule writes the lessons of this module into law: judge results after fees, over standard long periods, not over a flattering window.
Source
SEC press release 2020-334 (December 22, 2020); 17 CFR § 275.206(4)-1(d)(1)–(2).

Decisions

Three performance questions

Scenario 1 · The Harpers' advisor review

The Harpers' advisor reports a 9.95% return this year after fees and notes that the balance grew by about $175,000. Their blended benchmark (42/18/40) returned 11.4%. What should they conclude?

Great yearThe balance includes their own contributions and a strong market. It says nothing about whether the advice added value.
Measure against the blend, over timeRight. A persistent shortfall near the fee means they are paying for the market's return. Good advice can still earn its keep through taxes and planning (INV-007, INV-103), but that should be explicit.
Fire the advisorPremature. One year of a 1.45-point gap is within normal tracking error for a managed portfolio. Gather three to five years of net returns first.
The principle: judge anyone you pay by net returns against a blended benchmark, over years.
Scenario 2 · Jordan's two numbers

Jordan's 401(k) statement shows a "personal rate of return" of 4.1% for the year, while the target-date fund Jordan owns reports 7.8%. Jordan moved most of the balance to cash in March and back in July. Is the fund at fault?

Switch fundsPunishes the fund for a timing decision it did not make; the next fund would suffer the same way.
It was the timingRight. The gap measures the move to cash. The fix is a rule against reacting to headlines (INV-061), not a new fund.
The principle: time-weighted return judges the fund; money-weighted return judges you.
Scenario 3 · The Riveras' disappointment

In a year when the S&P 500 returned 25%, the Riveras' 80/20 portfolio returned 20%. Marcus wants to go 100% stocks. What is the fair comparison?

S&P 500An all-US, all-stock index carries far more risk than their plan. In a year like 2008 (−36.6%) they would not want that comparison.
Blended benchmarkRight. Their allocation was chosen for their risk capacity and tolerance (INV-025). A good year for stocks is not a reason to change it; INV-058 covers what is.
The principle: a benchmark must carry the same risk as the portfolio it judges.

Apply it

Each household's yardstick

Her fund is her benchmark's twin. A target-date fund publishes its own composite benchmark; she checks once a year that the fund tracks it closely after its expense ratio, and that her contributions went in every paycheck.
An 80/20 blend, not the S&P 500. Their yardstick is 56% US stocks, 24% international and 20% bonds. With index funds, their time-weighted return should sit a few hundredths of a point below it.
Two measures, two lessons. Personal (money-weighted) return shows the cost of moving to cash; comparing employer stock with a total-market index shows what the concentration has added or cost.
Hold the advisor to the blend. Net-of-fee returns against a 42/18/40 blended benchmark, tracked over three to five years, plus a list of the planning work the fee covers.
The measure that matters is sustainability. In retirement, the question is less "did I beat the benchmark?" and more "is my money-weighted return, after withdrawals, keeping my plan on track?" (INV-076).

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

An account grew a total of 50% over five years with no deposits or withdrawals. What is its annualized return, in percent?

(1 + 0.50)1/5 − 1 = 1.0845 − 1 = 8.45% a year, not 10%.
Exercise 2

A fund returns +20% in year 1 and −10% in year 2. What is its time-weighted annualized return, in percent?

Link the years: 1.20 × 0.90 = 1.08. Annualize over two years: √1.08 − 1 = 3.92%.
Exercise 3

A blended benchmark is 42% US stocks, 18% international stocks and 40% bonds. The indexes return 20%, 10% and 3%. What does the benchmark return, in percent?

8.4 + 1.8 + 1.2 = 11.4%.
Exercise 4

A statement shows a beginning value of $250,000, deposits of $12,000 and an ending value of $284,800. Using the simple Dietz method, what was the return, in percent?

Gain from investing: 284,800 − 250,000 − 12,000 = $22,800. Average capital: 250,000 + 12,000 ÷ 2 = $256,000. Return 22,800 ÷ 256,000 = 8.91%.
