Why Invest at All?

Saving is essential, and for some money it is exactly right. But money kept in cash for decades quietly loses what it can buy. This module shows why, how much, and what the alternatives have actually done over 98 years of US data.

The question this module answersIf I already save, why should I take on the risk of investing — and which of my money should stay safe?

Start here

You already know how to save

Most people learn to save long before they learn to invest. A paycheck arrives, some of it is spent, and whatever is left over goes into a savings account. That habit is the single most important thing in personal finance, and nothing in this course replaces it.

Saving is also the right answer for a lot of money. The cash you need next month, the emergency fund that covers a lost job, the deposit for a car you will buy next year — that money should be safe and easy to reach, and a bank account does that well.

The problem shows up with money you will not need for a long time. Over years and decades, prices rise. A dollar kept in cash does not shrink in number, but it buys less and less. Figure 1.1 shows how much: a dollar held from 1928 to 2025 bought about 5 cents worth of what it bought at the start.

Three different jobs

Saving, investing and speculating are not the same thing

People often use these words loosely. They are worth separating, because each one suits different money, and most financial mistakes come from giving money the wrong job.

Savingkeeping money safe and available
What it is
Setting money aside in a form that does not lose its dollar value and can be reached quickly — a checking or savings account, a money market fund, a short certificate of deposit (CD).
Used for
Spending you expect within a few years, and the emergency fund that absorbs surprises such as a job loss or a car repair.
What it means for you
Your savings will not fall in a market crash — that is its whole purpose. The price of that safety is a return that usually only roughly keeps pace with inflation, and sometimes falls short of it.
Investingowning or lending for long-term growth
What it is
Putting money into assets — businesses (stocks), loans (bonds), property — that are expected to produce income or grow in value over time, accepting that their price will move up and down along the way.
Used for
Goals that are many years away: retirement, a child's education a decade out, financial independence.
What it means for you
You accept that your balance can drop, sometimes by a third or more, in exchange for a much better chance of growing faster than prices over long periods. It works best with money you will not need for at least five years.
Speculatingbetting on a price move
What it is
Buying something mainly because you expect someone to pay more for it soon, rather than for the income or growth the asset itself produces.
Used for
Short-term trading, buying whatever is rising fastest, many options and cryptocurrency strategies.
What it means for you
The result depends on predicting other people's behavior, and the typical outcome is worse than simply investing. If you do it at all, it belongs only with money you can afford to lose entirely.
What would you do?

Maya has just started her first job. She has $3,000 in a savings account, no emergency fund beyond that, and 6.8% student loans. A coworker says she should "get into the market" with the $3,000 now. What should she do with it?

Invest it allHer long horizon is real, but this money is also her only cushion. If her car breaks or she loses the job during a market fall, she would have to sell at a loss or put the bill on a credit card at 20%+ interest. Long-term money and emergency money are different jobs.
Build the emergency fund firstThis is the conventional first step, and the one most planners would suggest. Once she has a cushion — commonly three to six months of essential expenses — new savings can go to investing and to the loan. It also protects her investments later, because she will never be forced to sell them to pay a bill.
Pay the loan nowPaying off 6.8% debt is a guaranteed 6.8% "return," which is attractive. But emptying her only savings leaves her one surprise away from high-interest credit card debt. The loan-versus-invest question is real, and module INV-020 works it through in full; it just comes after the cushion.
The principle: money needed soon, or needed in an emergency, should be saved, not invested. Maya can still start investing through her employer's retirement plan — especially if it has a match — while she builds the cushion.
Going furtherIs investing just gambling with better marketing?

No, and the difference is structural. A casino game has a negative expected value for the player: the house keeps a cut of every bet, so the average bettor must lose. A company, by contrast, produces goods and profits whether or not anyone trades its shares. Owners of the whole market receive, on average, those profits — minus costs. Over 1928–2025, US stocks returned about 10% a year before inflation.

What turns investing into something like gambling is behavior: concentrating in one stock, using borrowed money, trading frequently, or needing the money at a fixed date. Those are covered in INV-004 (diversification), INV-060 (biases) and INV-016 (options).

The quiet cost of cash

Inflation: why a dollar buys less every year

You have seen inflation even if you have never measured it: a coffee, a movie ticket or a month's rent costs more today than it did ten years ago. Economists turn that everyday experience into a single number.

Inflationthe general rise in prices over time
What it is
The percentage increase in the overall price level over a period, usually a year. In the United States it is most often measured by the Consumer Price Index (CPI), which the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) publishes monthly by pricing a large basket of goods and services.
Used for
Adjusting wages, Social Security benefits, tax brackets and many contracts; and measuring whether an investment actually made you richer.
What it means for you
At 3% inflation, prices double in about 24 years. Money you hold for retirement must grow at least that fast just to stand still.
Purchasing power, nominal and realthree words you will see everywhere
What it is
Purchasing power is what money can buy. A nominal return is the percentage change in dollars. A real return is the change in purchasing power — the nominal return after removing inflation.
Used for
Comparing returns across decades with very different inflation, and planning in "today's dollars."
What it means for you
A savings account paying 4% while prices rise 3% is a real return of about 1%. One paying 0.5% while prices rise 7% — roughly the situation in 2021 — is a real loss of about 6%.
real return = (1 + nominal return)
      ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1
e.g. (1.04 ÷ 1.03) − 1 = 0.97%

Try a few start years in the calculator. Set it to 1973 through 1982 and you will see the worst decade for cash in modern US history: prices rose an average of 8.7% a year, more than doubling in ten years, and even Treasury bills (T-bills) — the safest short-term investment — lost ground after inflation.

Going furtherHow the CPI is built, and why your own inflation differs

The BLS prices tens of thousands of items each month, grouped into categories such as housing, food, transportation and medical care, each weighted by how much households spend on it. Housing is the heaviest category. The headline number is the CPI-U, for all urban consumers.

Your own inflation rate depends on what you buy. A renter in a fast-growing city, a family paying for childcare, or a retiree with high medical spending can each experience noticeably higher or lower inflation than the headline figure. That is one reason retirement plans usually assume a slightly conservative inflation rate rather than the latest reading.

"Core" inflation removes food and energy, which swing sharply month to month. It is useful for spotting the trend, but you still pay for food and fuel, so use the headline figure for personal planning.

Practitioner depthWhich CPI, the Fisher relation, and deflation

Several indexes, several uses. Social Security cost-of-living adjustments are based on the CPI-W (urban wage earners and clerical workers). Federal income-tax brackets have been indexed to the chained CPI-U (C-CPI-U) since the 2017 tax law; the chained index accounts for households switching to cheaper substitutes, so it tends to rise slightly more slowly. Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) and I bonds use the CPI-U. Module INV-008 covers the inflation-linked instruments.

The Fisher relation. The exact relationship is (1 + nominal) = (1 + real) × (1 + inflation). The common shortcut, real ≈ nominal − inflation, is fine at low rates and poor at high ones: at 12% nominal and 10% inflation the shortcut says 2%, the exact figure is 1.8%.

Deflation is falling prices. In 1930–1932 prices fell by 6–10% a year, which is why the calculator shows cash gaining purchasing power in those years — while unemployment rose to about a quarter of the workforce by 1933. Deflation sounds pleasant for savers but usually arrives with a collapsing economy and rising real debt burdens.

Where returns come from

There are only two ways to put money to work

Every investment, however complicated, is built from one of two ideas. You either lend your money to someone who pays you interest, or you own part of something that produces income or grows in value. Knowing which one you are doing tells you most of what you need to know about its risk.

Stock (equity)
What it is
A share of ownership in a company, entitling you to part of its profits (some paid as dividends) and its value.
For you
The main engine of long-term growth, with the largest swings along the way.
Bond (fixed income)
What it is
A loan to a government or company that pays interest and returns the principal on a set date.
For you
Steadier income and a cushion when stocks fall — though, as 2022 showed, not always.
Risk premiumthe extra return for accepting uncertainty
What it is
The expected return of a risky investment above a safe one. The equity risk premium is stocks' expected return above Treasury bills or bonds.
Used for
Setting expectations for long-term plans, and understanding why nobody can offer high returns without risk.
What it means for you
Any product promising stock-like returns with no chance of loss is either misdescribing its risk or its return. That single idea screens out most investment fraud (INV-062).
Going furtherWhere real estate, gold and funds fit

Real estate is ownership: you receive rent and any rise in value, and you carry the costs, vacancies and repairs. A mortgage makes you a borrower at the same time, which magnifies both gains and losses (INV-066).

Gold is neither. It pays no interest and produces no profits, so its return comes only from what the next buyer will pay. That makes it behave differently from stocks and bonds — sometimes usefully — but it has no built-in engine of growth (INV-013).

Funds (mutual funds, index funds, exchange-traded funds or ETFs) are containers. A stock fund is ownership, a bond fund is lending, a balanced fund is both. The container matters for costs and taxes; what is inside determines the risk (INV-012).

Going furtherWhy the US numbers may flatter the future

The United States had one of the best-performing stock markets of the twentieth century. Researchers who study many countries, notably Elroy Dimson, Paul Marsh and Mike Staunton (Triumph of the Optimists, 2002, and later yearbooks), find that most other developed markets returned less, and a few investors lost nearly everything when markets closed after wars and revolutions. Looking only at the country that turned out best is a form of survivorship bias.

The sensible reading: owning businesses has been rewarded across most countries and periods, but a plan should not depend on the next 30 years matching the best 98 in the best market. Many planners use lower expected returns than history shows, which you will see in the retirement stage.

Practitioner depthAverage return versus growth rate

The simple (arithmetic) average of stocks' 98 annual returns is 11.9%; the compound (geometric) growth rate is 10.0%. They differ because losses hurt more than equal gains help: a 50% loss followed by a 50% gain leaves you at 75%. The gap is roughly half the variance: 11.9% − ½(19.4%)² ≈ 10.0%. Always use the geometric rate to describe what an investor actually experienced; the arithmetic mean is the right input only for estimating a single year's expected return. INV-003 develops this.

Real history

Two decades that tested savers

Case study 11973–1982: the decade inflation ate everything
What happened
Oil shocks and loose monetary policy pushed consumer prices up an average of 8.7% a year for ten years; prices rose 12.3% in 1974 and 13.3% in 1979. A basket that cost $100 at the start of 1973 cost about $230 at the end of 1982.
How each asset did
After inflation, over those ten years: stocks −1.8% a year, 10-year Treasuries −2.5%, Treasury bills −0.2%, home prices roughly flat, and gold +11.9% a year.
What it teaches
No single asset protects you in every environment. Cash and T-bills, which feel safest, barely held their ground. Bonds with fixed payments were hurt worst by rising prices. The lesson for planning is not "buy gold"; it is that holding a mix, and including inflation protection in a long plan, matters (INV-008 covers TIPS and I bonds).
Source
Computed from Damodaran, NYU Stern, annual returns and CPI-U, 1973–1982.
Case study 22021–2022: near-zero interest meets 7% inflation
What happened
After years of very low interest rates, prices rose 7.0% in 2021 and 6.5% in 2022. Three-month Treasury bills paid about 0.04% in 2021. Many bank savings accounts paid similarly little.
The damage
Cash held through 2021 and 2022 lost roughly 12% of its purchasing power in two years. Then, in 2022, both stocks (−18.0%) and 10-year Treasuries (−17.8%) fell sharply as interest rates rose — the rare year when the usual cushion did not cushion.
What it teaches
"Safe" depends on what you are measuring. Cash was safe in dollars and unsafe in purchasing power. By 2023, T-bill yields had risen above 5%, and savers finally earned a real return again. Rates move; checking what your cash actually earns is part of managing it.
Source
CPI-U and returns from Damodaran, NYU Stern; 2021–2023.

Decisions

Give the money the right job

Each scenario below has a household, a sum of money and a decision. Choose first, then read every option's trade-offs — there is rarely one perfect answer, but there are clearly better and worse ones.

Scenario 1 · Jordan

Jordan has $20,000 saved toward their daughter's college, which starts in three years. A friend points out that stocks have averaged about 10% a year. Where should the $20,000 go?

All stocksThe 10% is a long-run average. Over three years anything can happen: US stocks fell in each of 2000, 2001 and 2002, leaving $20,000 at about $12,500. Tuition bills do not wait for a recovery.
Savings or short TreasuriesThe best fit for money with a fixed, near date. It will earn less, but the amount will be there. Jordan's long-term retirement money is where stock risk belongs.
Half and halfReasonable if the timing is flexible — for example, if only the last two years of tuition come from this pot. For the first-year bill, it still risks a shortfall at exactly the wrong moment. INV-034 covers 529 plans, many of which offer age-based portfolios that shift toward safety as the start date approaches.
The principle: a fixed deadline within about five years calls for safety, however strong the long-run averages look.
Scenario 2 · Ruth

Ruth is 68 and retired. A relative tells her, "You're retired now, so you should put your whole $780,000 individual retirement account (IRA) into CDs. You can't afford to lose anything." What do you think?

All CDsIt removes market risk and replaces it with inflation risk. At 68, Ruth has a meaningful chance of living into her 90s. At 3% inflation, her spending power from fixed CD income would fall by roughly half over 24 years.
Keep part of it growingThe approach most retirement research supports: enough in safe assets to cover several years of withdrawals, so she never has to sell in a crash, and the rest invested for the decades that follow. INV-076 and INV-077 work through exactly how.
All stocksHer Social Security does cover part of her spending, which raises her ability to take risk. But a 2008-style fall of a third or more in the first years of retirement, while she is withdrawing, can permanently damage the portfolio. That is sequence risk (INV-077).
The principle: "retired" is not the same as "short horizon." Much of a retiree's money may not be spent for 20 years.
Scenario 3 · The Riveras

The Riveras keep $41,000 in their checking account — more than four months of expenses plus a $9,000 emergency fund they hold separately. The checking account pays nothing. What should they do?

Leave itAt 3% inflation, $41,000 earning nothing loses about $1,200 of purchasing power a year. Over ten years that is more than $10,000 of buying power, simply from idleness.
Right-size the cash, invest the surplusThe usual answer. Their emergency fund plus a working balance covers surprises; money beyond that is long-term money and can go to retirement or college accounts, ideally tax-advantaged (Stage 5).
Prepay the mortgageA guaranteed 3.1% return, which is below what three-month Treasury bills yielded in September 2026 (roughly 3.9% to 4.2%), and below long-run stock returns. It also locks the money in the house, where it is hard to reach in an emergency. INV-064 compares prepaying and investing in detail.
The principle: too much cash is also a decision, and it has a cost.

Apply it

What this module means for each household

Build the cushion, then start small and early. Maya's biggest asset is time: 40 years of compounding. Her first steps are an emergency fund and enough retirement saving to capture any employer match. Keeping her long-term retirement money in cash would be the costly mistake for her.
Right-size the cash. The Riveras have more idle cash than their safety needs. Their question is how to split the surplus between college (a 10–14-year horizon) and retirement (30 years) — two different jobs with different accounts, covered in Stage 5.
Separate the college money from the retirement money. The college fund has a fixed three-year deadline and belongs in savings. Retirement, 20 years away and lasting 25 more, is Jordan's long money, and with one income it is also the money most at risk of being too small.
Watch the timeline, not just the balance. The Harpers' first retirement withdrawals are 5–8 years away, so their near-term money will need to become safer over the coming years — but most of their $1.1M will not be spent for decades.
Retired does not mean short horizon. Ruth's $60,000 cash and a few years of withdrawals should be safe. The rest may need to last into her 90s, which means inflation is one of her biggest risks, not the stock market alone.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Each exercise takes a minute. Type your answer and press Check; a worked solution is always available.

Exercise 1

A savings account pays 5% while inflation is 3%. What is the real return, in percent? (Use the exact formula.)

Real return = (1 + 0.05) ÷ (1 + 0.03) − 1 = 1.0194 − 1 = 1.94%. The shortcut 5% − 3% = 2% is close at low rates.
Exercise 2

Ruth keeps $100,000 in an account paying nothing. If inflation averages 3% a year, what will it buy in 20 years, in today's dollars?

Buying power = $100,000 ÷ (1.03)20 = $100,000 ÷ 1.8061 = $55,368. Almost half the buying power is gone.
Exercise 3

In 2021, three-month Treasury bills returned about 0.04% while inflation was 7.04%. What was the real return, in percent? (Negative numbers allowed.)

(1 + 0.0004) ÷ (1 + 0.0704) − 1 = 0.9346 − 1 = −6.54%. A "safe" asset lost more than 6% of its buying power in one year.
Exercise 4

Using the Rule of 72 (years ≈ 72 ÷ rate), roughly how many years does it take prices to double at 3% inflation?

72 ÷ 3 = 24 years. The exact answer is ln 2 ÷ ln 1.03 = 23.4 years. INV-002 explains why the rule works.
