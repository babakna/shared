Compounding and the Time Value of Money

Compounding is growth on top of growth. It is the reason small amounts saved early can outgrow large amounts saved late, the reason debt snowballs, and the reason a dollar today is worth more than a dollar next year. This module makes all three concrete.

The question this module answersHow much does time really matter — and how do I compare money now with money later?

Start here

Interest that earns interest

If you have ever had a savings account, you have seen interest: the bank pays you a small percentage for holding your money. Compounding is what happens when that interest is left in the account, so that next year's interest is paid on this year's interest too.

It sounds like a technicality. Over short periods it almost is: in the first year, compound and simple interest are identical. The difference appears slowly and then, after a decade or two, overwhelmingly. Figure 2.1 shows $10,000 growing at 7% a year for 40 years, once with simple interest (interest paid only on the original $10,000) and once with compound interest.

💡

The one idea. Growth is proportional to what you already have. That is why the curve bends upward, why time is so valuable, and why every percentage point of return — or of cost — matters more than it looks.

How it works

Compound growth, one step at a time

Start with $1,000 at 5%. After one year you have $1,000 × 1.05 = $1,050. After two years you have $1,050 × 1.05 = $1,102.50 — the extra $2.50 is interest on the first year's interest. After n years you have $1,000 × 1.05 × 1.05 × … n times.

Future value (FV)what money grows to
What it is
The amount a sum grows to after a number of periods at a given rate of return, with each period's growth added to the balance.
Used for
Projecting savings, retirement balances and college funds; showing the cost of waiting.
What it means for you
Every projection you will ever see from a bank, a retirement plan or an advisor is this formula with assumptions plugged in. Knowing the formula lets you question the assumptions.
FV = PV × (1 + r)n
PV = starting amount
r = rate per period
n = number of periods

With regular saving added each period, the formula gains a second part: the future value of a series of equal payments, called an annuity in finance (the same word as the insurance product, with a narrower meaning here).

FV = PV × (1 + r)n
   + PMT × [(1 + r)n − 1] ÷ r
PMT = payment added each period
Going furtherAnnual percentage rate (APR) versus annual percentage yield (APY), and how often interest compounds

Banks quote savings accounts in APY (annual percentage yield), which already includes compounding. Loans are quoted in APR (annual percentage rate), which usually does not. The same 12% APR compounded monthly is an APY of 12.68%; compounded daily it is 12.75%.

APY = (1 + APR ÷ m)m − 1
m = compounding periods per year

For savers, compare APYs. For borrowers, remember that a credit card's quoted APR understates what the balance actually grows at: a 22% APR compounded monthly grows a balance at about 24.4% a year.

Practitioner depthContinuous compounding and effective rates

As compounding becomes more frequent, the result approaches a limit: FV = PV × ert, where e ≈ 2.71828. At 12% the continuous APY is e0.12 − 1 = 12.75%, barely above daily compounding. Continuous compounding is mostly a mathematical convenience: log returns (ln(1 + r)) add across periods, which is why analysts use them for multi-period statistics.

The calculator above converts the annual rate to a monthly one as (1 + r)1/12 − 1, so a "7% a year" input really does produce 7% growth over a year. Dividing by 12 instead (0.583% a month) would overstate the annual result slightly, at 7.23%.

The biggest lever

Starting early: the famous comparison, honestly told

A classic illustration: Saver A invests $6,000 a year from 25 to 34 and then stops for good. Saver B invests $6,000 a year from 35 until 65. Saver B puts in three times as much money. Who ends up with more?

At 7% a year, Saver A — who invested $60,000 — ends with about $675,000, ahead of Saver B's $606,000 from $180,000 of contributions. That is the version usually told. Now move the return slider to 6%: Saver B wins, $503,000 to $481,000. At 5% Saver B wins comfortably.

⚠️

The honest lesson. "Ten years early beats thirty years late" is not a law; it depends on the assumed return. What is true at every return: each dollar invested early does far more work than a dollar invested late, and the best result by a wide margin is to start early and keep going. Try setting Saver A to invest for 40 years.

What would you do? · Denise

Denise's employer matches 50% of what she contributes to her 401(k), up to 6% of her $62,000 salary. She is thinking of contributing 3% for now and "catching up later." What does waiting cost?

3% nowShe leaves half the match unclaimed: 1.5% of salary, about $930 in the first year, that her employer would have paid. Projected over 41 years at 7% with 3% annual raises, contributing 3% instead of 6% leaves her with roughly half the balance — about $0.9M versus $1.8M in future dollars.
6% nowThe standard advice, for a reason: the match is an immediate 50% return on those dollars, which no investment reliably matches. On her salary, 6% is $310 a month before tax; because it comes out pre-tax, her take-home falls by less.
Nothing yetHer 6.8% loan is real, but skipping the match to repay it trades a guaranteed 50% return for a guaranteed 6.8% one. Most planners would capture the match first, then direct extra money to the loan (INV-020 works through the order in detail).
The principle: an employer match is the closest thing to free money in personal finance, and early dollars compound the longest.

Denise's projection assumes contributions each month, a 7% annual return, 3% yearly raises, and the plan's match formula unchanged. Illustrative, before taxes; real plans vary.

A shortcut worth memorizing

How long until money doubles?

Divide 72 by the annual percentage rate. The answer is approximately how many years it takes for money to double. At 8%, about 9 years; at 6%, about 12; at 3% inflation, prices double in about 24.

Rule of 72years to double ≈ 72 ÷ rate
What it is
A mental approximation of the doubling time for anything that compounds at a steady rate.
Used for
Quickly judging growth rates, inflation, fees and debt without a calculator.
What it means for you
At 7%, money doubles about every 10 years. A 25-year-old's savings can double four times before 65 — that is 16 times the original. A 55-year-old's can double once.

Practitioner depthWhy 72 works

Doubling means (1 + r)n = 2, so n = ln 2 ÷ ln(1 + r). For small r, ln(1 + r) ≈ r, giving n ≈ 0.693 ÷ r, the "Rule of 69.3" — exact for continuous compounding. Annual compounding makes the true answer a little longer, and 72 compensates while being divisible by 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9 and 12. The rule is most accurate near 8%.

The same logic gives a Rule of 114 for tripling (ln 3 ≈ 1.10) and a Rule of 144 for quadrupling (two doublings).

Compounding in reverse

Why a dollar today is worth more than a dollar later

If you can earn 5% a year, $100 today becomes $105 next year. Turn that around: a promise of $105 next year is worth only $100 today. That backwards step is called discounting, and the result is the present value.

Present value (PV) and discount rate
What it is
The value today of money received in the future, found by dividing by (1 + r) for each year of delay. The rate r used is the discount rate: what you could earn on the money elsewhere at similar risk.
Used for
Lump sum versus payments (pensions, lottery wins, legal settlements), comparing job offers, valuing bonds and businesses, deciding whether to prepay a loan.
What it means for you
You cannot compare dollars at different dates directly. Present value converts them all to today's dollars first, and the discount rate you choose can change the answer.
PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)n
PV of n equal yearly payments
   = PMT × [1 − (1 + r)−n] ÷ r
What would you do? · A pension choice

A retiring worker is offered either a $250,000 lump sum today or $18,000 a year for 20 years. Using the calculator's second mode, which is worth more?

The payments, because $360,000 > $250,000Adding future dollars as if they were today's ignores the time value of money. At 5%, those payments are worth about $224,000 today — less than the lump sum.
It depends on the rateRight. The break-even is about 3.8%: below it the payments are worth more, above it the lump sum is. And real pensions add questions the calculator leaves out — life expectancy (payments may last longer than 20 years), inflation adjustments, survivor benefits and the payer's financial strength. INV-080 covers the full decision.
Always the lump sumNot always. If the payments are generous relative to the lump sum, or you are likely to live long, or you would not invest the lump sum well, the payments can win.
The principle: never compare amounts at different dates without discounting them to the same date.
Going furtherChoosing a discount rate

The discount rate should reflect what the money could earn elsewhere at similar risk. A guaranteed pension payment is closer to a high-quality bond than to stocks, so it is usually discounted near bond yields. Using an expected stock return as the discount rate makes guaranteed payments look artificially cheap, because it compares a certain stream with an uncertain one.

What drives the balance

Early on, your saving does the work. Later, growth does.

Save $500 a month at 7%. After ten years you have about $85,500, of which $60,000 is your own money and $25,500 is growth. By year 19, the growth has overtaken everything you have put in. By year 40, growth is about 80% of a $1.24 million balance.

🌱

If you are starting out, focus on your savings rate: raising it from 5% to 10% of pay doubles your progress. Searching for a slightly better fund changes little yet.

🌳

If you have built a large balance, costs, taxes and your investment mix now move more dollars each year than your contributions do. That is why Stages 5 and 7 matter most to people like the Harpers.

Going furtherWhy "a 1% better return" is worth more than it sounds

Over 40 years of $500 a month, the ending balance is about $1.24 million at 7% and about $0.95 million at 6%. One percentage point of return — or of fees, which is the same thing in reverse — changes the outcome by almost a quarter. INV-007 shows the fee version with its own calculator.

Debt and fees

Compounding does not care whose side it is on

The same arithmetic that grows savings also grows debt. A credit card balance left unpaid grows at the card's rate; the Federal Reserve reports that the average interest rate on credit card accounts being charged interest was about 22% in 2025. At that rate, compounded monthly, an unpaid balance doubles in a little over three years.

⚠️

The math of paying off high-rate debt. Paying off a 22% card is a guaranteed, tax-free 22% return. No mainstream investment reliably beats that, which is why high-interest debt usually comes before investing beyond an employer match.

Fees compound against you in the same way, just more quietly: a 1% annual fee is taken from the whole balance every year, and the dollars removed lose all their future growth. INV-007 measures it.

Real history

Two stories of time doing the heavy lifting

Case study 1Benjamin Franklin's 200-year experiment
What happened
When Benjamin Franklin died in 1790, his will left £1,000 each to Boston and Philadelphia, to be lent at low interest to young tradesmen for 100 years; after that, about three-quarters of each fund could go to public works and the rest kept lending for another century. When the trusts ended around 1990, Boston's had grown to roughly $4.5 million and Philadelphia's to roughly $2 million to $2.5 million (contemporary accounts differ).
What it teaches
Compounding over very long periods produces results that seem out of proportion to the starting sum — even at modest rates and with money withdrawn midway. It also shows the difference that the rate makes: the two identical gifts ended far apart.
Source
The Franklin Institute, "Benjamin Franklin's Donor Story" (fi.edu), for the terms of the will; 1990 values from Snopes (citing The New York Times, 1990) and The Daily Pennsylvanian (December 1990).
Case study 2Ronald Read: the $8 million janitor
What happened
Ronald Read worked as a gas station attendant and later as a janitor at a J.C. Penney store in Brattleboro, Vermont. He lived frugally, bought shares of established dividend-paying companies and held them for decades. When he died in 2014 at 92, his estate was worth almost $8 million; he left $4.8 million to Brattleboro Memorial Hospital and $1.2 million to Brooks Memorial Library. Almost no one had known.
What it teaches
A modest income, a high savings rate, very low costs, patience and a long life can compound into a fortune. It is a story about time and behavior, not about brilliant stock picks.
The caution
Stories like this are memorable precisely because they are rare. Many patient individual-stock investors did far worse, and a single-company portfolio carries risks that a diversified fund does not (INV-004).
Source
The Washington Post, "The remarkable life and lessons of the $8 million janitor" (April 2015); CNBC (August 2016).

Decisions

Put the time value of money to work

Scenario 1 · The Riveras

The Riveras' younger child is 4. They could start $250 a month for college now, or wait until their mortgage is smaller and put in $500 a month for the last seven years. Which should they do?

WaitThe same $42,000 ends up about $13,000 smaller, because the money has half as long to compound (Figure 2.6). Waiting also bets that their budget will really have room later.
Start nowUsually better, if the cash flow allows it. A 529 plan (INV-034) adds tax-free growth for education and, in many states, a state tax deduction.
Retirement firstA reasonable priority many planners share: students can borrow for college; nobody lends for retirement. The best answer may be both, in proportion — but not retirement at zero.
The principle: for a fixed total, earlier dollars do more work.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers

The Harpers are 57 and 55. Tom says that with only five to eight working years left, compounding "doesn't really apply" to them anymore, so extra saving is pointless. Is he right?

YesThis mistakes the retirement date for the end of the investment horizon. Money saved at 57 may not be spent until their 80s.
NoRight. Their portfolio will keep compounding throughout retirement, and savers aged 50 or older can make additional "catch-up" contributions to workplace plans and individual retirement accounts, or IRAs (INV-031, INV-032). Late saving still counts — it just works for fewer doublings.
The principle: your horizon is when the money is spent, not when you stop working.

Apply it

What compounding means for each household

Capture the match now. Denise has the most doublings ahead of her. Every dollar she invests at 24 is worth roughly 16 dollars at 65 at a 7% return. Starting at 6% instead of 3% roughly doubles her projected balance.
Start the college fund now, even small. For the same total, starting at the younger child's age of 4 instead of 11 is worth about $13,000 more at 6%.
Use the catch-up years. At 58, the $400,000 Maya has already saved matters more than new contributions, but her last working decade still counts. She saves 12% of pay ($11,040) and receives a $3,680 match. For 2026 she could defer up to $32,500 of her own pay ($24,500 plus the $8,000 catch-up for age 50 and older). Each extra $1,000 invested at 58 is worth about $1,840 at 67 at a 7% return, and more if it stays invested into her 80s.
Late saving still compounds. With high income, catch-up contributions, and money that will stay invested into their 80s, the Harpers' remaining five to eight working years of saving are far from pointless.
Compounding in reverse is her tool now. Present value helps Ruth compare annuity offers, pension-style payouts and when to spend. Inflation, compounding against her at about 3%, is the force she must plan around.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

$10,000 grows at 7% a year for 10 years. What is it worth at the end?

FV = 10,000 × 1.0710 = 10,000 × 1.9672 = $19,672 — almost exactly double, as the Rule of 72 predicts (72 ÷ 7 ≈ 10.3 years).
Exercise 2

Using the Rule of 72, about how many years does money take to double at 6%?

72 ÷ 6 = 12 years (exact: 11.9).
Exercise 3

You will receive $100,000 in 10 years. At a 5% discount rate, what is it worth today?

PV = 100,000 ÷ 1.0510 = 100,000 ÷ 1.6289 = $61,391.
Exercise 4

A loan charges 12% APR compounded monthly. What is its effective annual rate (APY), in percent?

(1 + 0.01)12 − 1 = 1.1268 − 1 = 12.68%.
