Sequence Risk and Monte Carlo

While you are saving, a bad year early and a good year late come out the same as the reverse. Once you start withdrawing, they do not. This module shows why, replays the 1966 and 1982 retirements from the data, and builds a Monte Carlo simulator from 98 years of history, including an honest list of what such simulations cannot tell you.

The question this module answersHow much does the luck of my first retirement years matter, and how should I read a "probability of success"?

Start here

Multiplication does not care about order. Withdrawals do.

If you invest $100 and earn +50% one year and −50% the next, you end with $75. Reverse the years and you still end with $75, because 1.5 × 0.5 = 0.5 × 1.5. For someone who is only saving, the order of good and bad years does not change where they end up.

That is exactly why the idea in this module, sequence-of-returns risk, surprises people. The moment money starts coming out, order matters a great deal. Take the real (after-inflation) returns of a 60/40 portfolio in the 30 years from 1966 to 1995. Left alone, $1 million grows to about $3.3 million in 1966 dollars whether the years run forward or backward. Now withdraw $40,000 a year in real terms. Run forward, the portfolio is empty in its 26th year. Run backward, with every single yearly return identical, it ends with about $1.65 million.

The fragile decade

Why early losses do lasting damage

Sequence-of-returns risksequence risk
What it is
The risk that poor returns arrive early in retirement, while withdrawals are being taken, so that money sold at low prices is gone before the recovery comes.
Used for
Explaining why two retirees with the same average return can have completely different outcomes, and why the years just before and after retirement deserve special care.
What it means for you
Your plan's weakest point is roughly the five years before and ten years after you stop working. The average return over thirty years matters less than the returns in that window.

The mechanism is simple arithmetic. A $40,000 withdrawal from a $1 million portfolio is 4%. If the portfolio falls to $700,000, the same withdrawal is 5.7% of what is left, and every dollar withdrawn is a dollar that cannot participate in the recovery. Losses early in retirement are therefore "locked in" by the withdrawals that follow them; the same losses late in retirement fall on a smaller balance that has already done most of its job.

The first-decade return is not the whole story. Several retirements starting in 1970–1973 also had negative first decades and survived, because the strong 1980s arrived soon enough. But no retirement in the record failed at 4% without a poor first decade.

With withdrawals W taken at the start of each year, the ending balance is the starting balance grown by all the returns, minus each withdrawal grown by the returns after it was taken. The first term does not depend on order. The second does: a withdrawal taken just before a run of strong years loses all that growth. Poor early returns make the subtracted term large relative to the first. That is also why, as Bengen observed, "excess returns" early in retirement are best not spent: they may be needed to offset weaker returns later.

The most striking line is the third. The 1966 retiree's last twenty years were better than the 1982 retiree's last twenty. It did not matter. By 1976 the 1966 retiree had withdrawn ten years of inflation-adjusted spending from a portfolio that had lost real value, and the good years that followed were earned on a balance too small to recover. The 1969 retiree makes the point even more sharply: their 30-year compound real return of 5.62% was higher than the 1928–2025 average of 5.15%, and a 4% withdrawal still ran out.

Going furtherInflation is part of the sequence

Sequence risk is usually described in terms of stock crashes, but the 1966 retiree's problem was as much inflation as markets. A fixed real withdrawal rises with prices; from 1966 to 1975, consumer prices rose about 75%, so the nominal withdrawal had to grow from $40,000 to nearly $70,000 while nominal stock returns were weak and bond prices fell. Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (INV-010) and Social Security's cost-of-living adjustments (INV-078) are defenses aimed specifically at this form of the risk.

Simulated history

How a Monte Carlo simulation works

History gives us only 69 overlapping 30-year retirements since 1928, and they share most of their years. A Monte Carlo simulation manufactures thousands of alternative histories by drawing returns at random, then counts how many of those imagined retirements would have lasted.

Monte Carlo simulation
What it is
Running a plan through thousands of randomly generated return paths and summarizing the spread of results, named after the casino because it relies on chance.
Used for
Estimating the share of possible futures in which a withdrawal plan lasts, and the range of balances along the way.
What it means for you
The "probability of success" in most planning software is a Monte Carlo result. It is only as good as the returns fed into it.
Bootstrappingresampling history
What it is
Building a simulated path by picking calendar years from the historical record at random, with replacement, and using each chosen year's actual stock return, bond return and inflation together.
Used for
Keeping the real shape of returns, including bad years like 1931 and 2008, without assuming a bell curve; and keeping the link between stocks, bonds and inflation within each year.
What it means for you
It uses nothing but real history, but it shuffles it. A bootstrapped path can put 1931, 1974 and 2008 back to back, or skip them entirely.
Random seed
What it is
The starting number for a computer's pseudo-random number generator. The same seed always produces the same "random" sequence.
Used for
Making a simulation reproducible, so that you and someone else get identical results from identical inputs.
What it means for you
If a result changes a lot when you change only the seed, you need more simulations; the difference is noise, not information.

With the defaults ($1 million, $40,000 a year, 60/40, 30 years, 1,000 simulations, seed 2026), 89.6% of simulated retirements paid every withdrawal. The median retiree ended with about $1.63 million in today's dollars, the 90th percentile with about $5.26 million, and the 10th percentile with nothing: more than one in ten simulations ran out, typically in year 26.

Percentile band
What it is
A line showing, for each year, the balance that a given share of simulations stayed above. The 10th-percentile line is the level that 90% of simulations beat.
Used for
Seeing the spread of outcomes over time, not just the single success number at the end.
What it means for you
Look at the 10th or 25th percentile in the first ten years. That is the realistic "bad but not disastrous" path you should be emotionally and financially ready to live through.

Using the output wisely

What a "90% chance of success" does and does not mean

A success rate is the share of simulated paths in which every planned withdrawal was paid. Three cautions follow directly from that definition.

  • It counts failures but not their size. A plan that runs dry at 94 and one that runs dry at 75 are both "failures." The percentile bands and the typical depletion year show how bad the failures are.
  • It assumes you never adjust. Real retirees cut spending after a crash. A 75% success rate for rigid spending might be a 99% success rate for someone following guardrails (INV-076). Treat low numbers as a signal to plan adjustments, not as a 25% chance of destitution.
  • Higher is not always better. A 99% success rate usually means a large expected surplus: money unspent. Many planners aim for 75–90% combined with a willingness to adjust.

Six limits of any Monte Carlo

  1. Independence. Bootstrapping draws each year separately, so it destroys any pattern across years: the tendency of inflation to persist for several years, and any tendency of markets to recover after crashes. The 1970s were a run of related bad years, not a coincidence.
  2. One country's history. The US market was among the best-performing of the twentieth century. Sampling only US years may overstate future returns.
  3. Today is not an average year. Resampling ignores starting conditions such as bond yields and stock valuations, which do affect the next decade's returns.
  4. No fees, taxes or behavior. Subtract your costs from returns, and remember that simulated retirees never panic-sell.
  5. Fixed spending. Most simulators assume spending rises exactly with inflation; real spending adapts.
  6. False precision. "87.3% success" suggests an accuracy the inputs cannot support. Read it as "high," "moderate" or "low."
💡

Two methods, two different questions. The historical sequences in INV-076 lasted in 94% of 30-year periods at 4%; this simulator gives about 90–91%. The historical test asks "what if the future repeats one of the past's actual sequences?"; the bootstrap asks "what if the future is a random shuffle of past years?" Neither is the truth. Consistent answers across methods are more reassuring than any single number.

What to do about it

Six ways to reduce sequence risk

The two most powerful tools are often the least discussed, and both are aimed at longevity risk as much as market risk. Spending flexibility changes the question from "will the money last?" to "how much will I spend?", which is a problem you can manage year by year. Delaying Social Security (INV-078) spends portfolio money in the early years to buy a larger, inflation-adjusted income for life, which reduces how much the portfolio must pay in the decades that follow. For the Harpers, Tom's benefit at 70 is 24% higher than at 67 and 77% higher than at 62, for as long as either of them lives.

Going furtherWhy the danger zone starts before retirement

A market fall just before retirement does the same damage as one just after: it shrinks the balance from which withdrawals are about to start. That is why target-date funds and many advisers reduce stock exposure in the five to ten years before the target date (INV-050). The cost is lower expected growth; the benefit is a narrower range of starting balances. Once the fragile first decade has passed, some retirees deliberately let stock exposure rise again.

Three real retirements from the data

1929, 1966 and 2000

Case study 1Retiring into the Great Depression, 1929
What happened
Stocks lost 8.3% in 1929, 25.1% in 1930 and 43.8% in 1931. But consumer prices fell about 6–10% a year in 1930–1932, so an inflation-adjusted withdrawal actually shrank in dollars, and Treasury bonds held up. A 60/40 retiree taking 4% survived 30 years.
What it teaches
A deflationary crash, painful as it was, was easier on a fixed real withdrawal than a slow inflationary decline. Bengen made the same observation in 1994.
Source
Computed from Damodaran (2026), Historical returns on stocks, bonds and bills, 1928–2025; Bengen (1994).
Case study 2Retiring into the inflation decade, 1966
What happened
Consumer prices rose 74.6% in the first ten years, stock returns were weak, and 10-year Treasury prices fell as rates rose. The real 60/40 return over the first decade was −1.76% a year. The 4% withdrawal ran out in the 26th year, even though the last 20 years compounded at 7.11% after inflation.
What it teaches
The most dangerous sequence combines poor real returns with high inflation early in retirement. In Bengen's 1994 data, 1966 was also the start year with the shortest portfolio life at 4%: 33 years with 50% stocks and 32 years with 75%.
Source
Computed from Damodaran (2026); Bengen, W. P. (1994), Journal of Financial Planning.
Case study 3Retiring at the 2000 peak
What happened
The S&P 500 lost 9.0%, 11.9% and 22.0% in 2000–2002 and 36.6% in 2008. The 60/40 real return over 2000–2009 was just 0.40% a year. Bonds cushioned both crashes. After 26 years, the 4% retiree had withdrawn every year's inflation-adjusted amount and still held about 65% of the starting real balance, with four years to go.
What it teaches
A bad first decade does not have to be fatal if inflation is modest and the second decade is strong. The 2000 retiree is on course to finish, but with much less margin than the median retiree.

Decisions

Living with sequence risk

Scenario 1 · The Harpers, two years before retirement

The Harpers' plan shows a 94.8% Monte Carlo success rate ($1.69 million, $61,000 a year, 60/40, 30 years). Then stocks fall 25% the year before Tom's planned retirement. What should they do?

Sell stocksThis locks in the loss and removes the recovery that the plan depends on. It turns a sequence risk into a certainty.
Rerun and adjust the leversRight. A fall just before retirement is sequence risk arriving early. The best responses shrink early withdrawals: more working income, more Social Security later, or flexible spending. A year of extra work both adds savings and removes a year of withdrawals.
Ignore itAverages are exactly what the 1966 retiree relied on. A sizable fall at the start of withdrawals deserves a response, even if the response is small.
The principle: respond to an early bad sequence by reducing early withdrawals, not by abandoning the allocation.
Scenario 2 · Ruth's two advisers

One adviser shows Ruth a 99% chance of success; another, using different software, shows 84%. Which should she believe?

The higher numberA success rate is an output of assumptions. A difference that large almost always comes from different return assumptions or from one model including taxes and fees and the other not.
Compare assumptionsRight. In this module's simulator, $25,200 a year from $780,000 at 50/50 for 25 years succeeds 99.1% of the time; adding $6,000 of taxes to the withdrawal lowers it to 95.8%. Assumptions, not arithmetic, drive the difference.
The principle: never compare two success rates without comparing the assumptions behind them.

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

Order B: start with $100,000, withdraw $10,000 at the start of each year, and earn −50% in year one and +50% in year two. What is the ending balance, in dollars?

Year one: ($100,000 − $10,000) × 0.5 = $45,000. Year two: ($45,000 − $10,000) × 1.5 = $52,500.
Exercise 2

Order A: the same, but +50% in year one and −50% in year two. What is the ending balance, in dollars?

Year one: $90,000 × 1.5 = $135,000. Year two: ($135,000 − $10,000) × 0.5 = $62,500, $10,000 more than order B, from exactly the same two returns.
Exercise 3

In 1,000 simulated retirements, 104 ran out of money before year 30. What is the success rate, in percent?

896 ÷ 1,000 = 89.6%, the default result of this module's simulator.

Returns of +30% and −20% average 5% arithmetically. What is the compound (geometric) average return per year, in percent (two decimals)?
