Diversification and Correlation

Harry Markowitz, who won a Nobel Prize for formalizing it, showed that combining investments that do not move in lockstep can lower risk without giving up return. This module shows how that works, why most individual stocks disappoint, and where diversification stops helping.

The question this module answersHow do I avoid a single bad bet sinking my plan, without giving up the growth I need?

Start here

"Don't put all your eggs in one basket" — made precise

You already know the proverb. It works for eggs because two baskets are unlikely to be dropped at the same moment. Investments are the same: spreading money helps only to the extent that the things you own do not all fall at once.

Imagine an umbrella company and a sunscreen company. Each has good years and bad years depending on the weather. Owned together, a rainy summer that hurts one helps the other, and the combined profits are much steadier than either alone — with no loss in the average. That is the whole idea, and Figure 4.1 draws it.

The key number

Do they move together?

Correlationa number from −1 to +1
What it is
A measure of how consistently two investments move in the same direction. +1: always together. 0: no relationship. −1: always opposite.
Used for
Deciding which combinations reduce risk. Anything below +1 reduces risk when combined; the lower the correlation, the bigger the reduction.
What it means for you
Owning five funds that all track large US companies is barely more diversified than owning one. Owning stocks and high-quality bonds has usually helped a lot — but the relationship changes over time.
Going furtherWhy the stock–bond link changes

When the main threat to the economy is a recession, interest rates tend to fall as stocks fall, and bond prices rise: a negative correlation. When the main threat is inflation, rates rise, which hurts both bonds and stocks: a positive correlation. The 1970s–1990s and 2022 were inflation-dominated; 2000–2020 was recession-dominated. That is why many portfolios also hold assets aimed at inflation, such as Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS; INV-010).

Practitioner depthThe two-asset formula
σp² = w1²σ1² + w2²σ2² + 2 w1 w2 ρ σ1 σ2

The portfolio's expected return is simply the weighted average of the two returns, but its variance depends on ρ. With ρ = +1, σp is the weighted average of the two volatilities — no benefit. With any ρ < 1, σp is lower than that weighted average. That gap is the "free lunch" of diversification (Markowitz, 1952). Correlations estimated from calendar-year data are noisy; with only ten points per window, Figure 4.3 is indicative, not precise.

Company-specific risk

Most individual stocks disappoint

Here is one of the most important findings in modern finance. Hendrik Bessembinder studied every US common stock in the main research database since 1926. He found that four out of every seven had lifetime buy-and-hold returns lower than one-month Treasury bills, and that the best-performing 4% of companies accounted for the entire net wealth creation of the US stock market; the rest, together, matched T-bills.

💡

Why this matters for you. If you own a few stocks, the odds are that you miss most of the rare big winners. Owning the whole market guarantees you own them. That is the strongest practical argument for broad index funds (INV-044).

The model above shows the other half of the story: adding stocks quickly removes company-specific risk, but a floor remains. That floor is market risk, which no number of similar stocks can remove. Only adding different kinds of assets lowers the floor.

The concentration trap

When your job and your savings depend on the same company

Many employees hold a large share of their savings in their employer's stock: it feels familiar, the company may match in shares, and employee stock purchase plans offer a discount. But if the company fails, you can lose your job and your savings in the same month.

Case studyEnron, 2001
What happened
Enron matched 401(k) contributions entirely in Enron stock. The Department of Labor reported that 63% of the plan's assets were invested in company stock at the end of 2000. The company collapsed into bankruptcy in December 2001; its stock became nearly worthless, and thousands of employees lost both their jobs and much of their retirement savings.
What it teaches
Your paycheck is already an investment in your employer. Adding your savings doubles the bet on one company. Many planners suggest keeping any single stock, including your employer's, to a small share of your portfolio — figures such as 5–10% are common rules of thumb, not law.
Source
U.S. General Accounting Office (2002). Private pensions: Key issues to consider following the Enron collapse (GAO-02-480T), testimony before the Senate Committee on Finance.
Going further"Human capital" is part of your portfolio

Economists call the present value of your future earnings your human capital. For young workers it is usually the largest asset they own. A teacher's human capital is bond-like (stable pay); a sales rep's at a small tech firm is stock-like, and tied to one industry. Good diversification looks across both your savings and your job. An engineer at a chip company who also owns mostly chip stocks is highly concentrated, even with dozens of holdings.

Ways to spread risk

Diversification has more than one dimension

Case studyJapan, 1989–2024: why geography matters
What happened
Japan's Nikkei 225 index closed at a record 38,915.87 on December 29, 1989, at the height of an asset bubble. It did not close above that level again until February 22, 2024 — more than 34 years later.
What it teaches
In 1989 Japan was one of the world's largest stock markets, and many investors assumed its leadership would continue. A Japanese investor who owned only Japanese stocks waited a generation to break even on price. (The Nikkei 225 is a price index; with dividends reinvested, the wait was shorter.) The US has been the long-run winner; spreading across countries is insurance against assuming that will always be true.
Source
Nippon.com, "Nikkei Index Sets First Record High Since 1989" (February 2024).

What it cannot do

Four limits of diversification

  1. It does not remove market risk. When the whole economy suffers, most stocks fall together. Only different asset classes help then, and even they can fail together (2022).
  2. Correlations often rise in a crisis. Risky assets — stocks, corporate bonds, real estate, many "alternatives" — tend to fall together just when you most want them not to.
  3. It guarantees you will always own something that is doing badly. That is the point, but it tempts people to sell the laggard at the wrong time.
  4. More funds is not more diversification. Owning several funds that hold the same large companies adds cost and complexity, not safety. Some call this "diworsification."

Decisions

Spot the concentration

Scenario 1 · Jordan

Jordan has $56,000 of their $140,000 in employer stock, built up through a discounted stock purchase plan. The stock has done well. What should they do?

Keep it allPast performance is exactly what makes concentration feel safe. As a single parent with one income from the same company, Jordan is exposed twice. Try the concentration calculator with 40%.
Reduce graduallyA common approach: many plans let employees buy at a discount, and selling soon after purchase keeps the discount while avoiding the concentration. Tax treatment depends on holding periods and account type (INV-040 covers stock purchase plans in detail).
Sell all todayRemoves the risk at once, which is defensible, but may trigger avoidable taxes if shares are in a taxable account. Spreading sales, or selling the highest-cost shares first, often reaches the same place more cheaply.
The principle: no single company — especially your employer — should be able to derail your plan.
Scenario 2 · The Riveras

The Riveras hold six funds across two 401(k)s: an S&P 500 index fund, a large-cap growth fund, a large-cap value fund, a "dividend growth" fund, a total US market fund and a technology fund. Are they diversified?

YesThe number of funds is not the measure. These six funds overlap heavily in the same large US companies.
PartlyRight. Consolidating into fewer broad funds — for example a total US market fund, an international fund and a bond fund, or a single target-date fund — would cut overlap and often cost (INV-055).
The principle: count the dimensions covered, not the number of funds.

Apply it

What diversification means for each household

One broad fund is enough to start. A target-date or total-market fund in her 401(k) gives Maya thousands of companies in one purchase. Diversification is cheap and simple at her stage.
Consolidate the overlap. Six overlapping large-cap funds can become two or three broad ones, adding bonds and international exposure they currently lack.
Unwind the employer bet. With 40% of savings and 100% of income tied to one company, reducing that concentration is Jordan's single most important risk decision.
Look beyond technology. A 25-stock portfolio concentrated in one sector behaves like one big bet. Their taxes on selling winners need planning (INV-035, INV-037).
Keep the cushion real. Ruth's safe assets must actually be safe when stocks fall; high-quality, short- to intermediate-term bonds and cash, not riskier "income" products that fall with stocks.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A 50/50 mix of an asset with 20% volatility and one with 8% volatility, with zero correlation. What is the portfolio's volatility, in percent?

σp² = 0.5² × 20² + 0.5² × 8² + 0 = 100 + 16 = 116, so σp = 10.77% — well below the 14% weighted average.
Exercise 2

Same two assets, 50/50, but now perfectly correlated (+1). What is the portfolio's volatility, in percent?

With ρ = 1, σp = 0.5 × 20 + 0.5 × 8 = 14%. No diversification benefit at all.
Exercise 3

An employee has 63% of their 401(k) in company stock, which falls 99%. The rest falls 10%. What is the total change, in percent?

0.63 × (−0.99) + 0.37 × (−0.10) = −0.624 − 0.037 = −66%.
