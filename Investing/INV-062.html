Scams, Fraud and Hype

Americans reported $8.6 billion of investment-fraud losses to the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) in 2025, more than any other kind of internet crime. Fraudsters do not rely on stupidity; they rely on trust, urgency and the same biases covered in INV-060. This module explains how the main schemes work, the warning signs they share, how to check anyone before you send money, and what to do if you are targeted.

The question this module answersHow do I tell a real opportunity from a fraud before it costs me money, and what do I do if I have already been approached?

Start here

The best scams feel like opportunities

Most people picture a fraud victim as careless or naive. The evidence says otherwise. Fraudsters target people with savings, and they succeed by building trust, creating urgency and offering exactly what their target wants to hear: steady returns, insider access, a way to catch up.

The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) received 1,008,597 complaints in 2025, with reported losses of $20.9 billion, a 26% increase from 2024. The largest category, by far, was investment fraud: 72,984 complaints and $8.65 billion of losses. People 60 and older filed 201,266 complaints and reported $7.7 billion of losses across all crime types, an average of $38,500 each.

Almost every investment scam is a variation on a few patterns, and they share warning signs. If you learn the patterns and adopt one habit, checking the seller and the investment independently before sending money, you will avoid the great majority of them.

Paying old investors with new money

Ponzi and pyramid schemes

The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) describes a Ponzi scheme as "an investment fraud that pays existing investors with funds collected from new investors." The organizers promise high returns with little or no risk, but often invest little or nothing. Early investors are paid, which makes the scheme look real and brings in more money. The scheme is named after Charles Ponzi, who ran a postage-stamp scheme in the 1920s.

Ponzi scheme
What it is
A fraud in which "returns" are paid from other investors' deposits rather than from real earnings. Statements show balances that do not exist.
Used for
Stealing from investors while keeping them happy long enough to recruit more. "With little or no legitimate earnings, Ponzi schemes require a constant flow of new money to survive" (SEC).
What it means for you
Being paid on time is not proof of legitimacy; it is how Ponzi schemes work. If the returns are unusually high or unusually steady, verify where they come from.
Pyramid scheme
What it is
A scheme in which participants earn mainly by recruiting others, who pay to join, rather than from selling a real product or earning an investment return.
Used for
Moving money up from late joiners to early ones and the organizers.
What it means for you
If the pitch is more about recruiting than about what is being sold, most participants must lose, because recruiting cannot grow forever.

The simulator below shows why collapse is arithmetic, not bad luck. Reported balances grow at the promised rate; the cash that actually exists grows only by new deposits, minus withdrawals and the operator's take. The gap between them widens every year.

At the defaults, $10 million raised, $5 million of new money each year, 12% promised and 8% of balances withdrawn a year, the scheme runs out of cash in year 17. By then statements show about $138 million that does not exist; investors who withdrew along the way had been paid about $86 million, every dollar of it from later investors. Faster recruiting postpones the collapse, but only for as long as recruiting can keep accelerating, and it cannot do that forever.

Going furtherThe SEC's Ponzi red flags

Investor.gov lists seven: high returns with little or no risk; overly consistent returns; unregistered investments; unlicensed sellers; secretive, complex strategies; problems with paperwork, such as account statement errors; and difficulty receiving payments. On the last point, the SEC notes that promoters sometimes offer even higher returns to persuade investors not to cash out.

Practitioner depthClawbacks: why "profits" may have to be returned

When a Ponzi scheme collapses, a court-appointed trustee or receiver collects what remains and sues to recover money paid out, including fictitious profits paid to investors who withdrew more than they put in, so it can be shared among the victims; these suits are called clawbacks. In the Madoff liquidation under the Securities Investor Protection Act, the trustee reports recoveries and settlements of about $15.5 billion and distributions to customers of about $14.8 billion as of August 21, 2026. Early investors who took out more than they deposited were among those sued.

Manufactured excitement

Pump-and-dump schemes and hype

A pump-and-dump has two parts, in the SEC's words. First, promoters "try to boost the price of a stock with false or misleading statements about the company." Then they sell their own shares into the demand they created. When the hype stops, the price falls, and the people who bought on the tip are left holding it.

Pump-and-dump
What it is
Market manipulation: raising the price of a thinly traded stock or token with hype, fake news or fake trading, then selling at the inflated price.
Used for
Transferring money from late buyers to the promoters. It works best on securities with little public information and little trading, such as microcap stocks and new tokens.
What it means for you
Any message urging you to buy a little-known security quickly, often claiming "inside" information, is most likely someone else's exit plan.

Social media and messaging apps have made pumping cheaper and faster. Newer schemes also fake the trading itself. Wash trading, trading an asset back and forth with yourself or a partner, makes a token look popular. In October 2024 the Justice Department charged 18 individuals and entities, including four cryptocurrency "market makers," with fraud and manipulation of this kind. To catch them, law enforcement directed the creation of its own token, NexFundAI. One market maker explained the business to a prospective client: "we have to make [the other buyers] lose money in order to make profit."

Going furtherHype that is not illegal, but still costly

Not every hyped investment is a fraud. Popular "story" stocks, new funds that launch at the peak of a trend and celebrity-endorsed tokens can all be legal and still be terrible investments at the price you pay. INV-060 showed that the average dollar flowing into spot bitcoin funds, which arrived mostly after price jumps, lost about 5.8% a year while the funds themselves gained 8.5% a year. The same defenses apply: ask what the price already assumes, and size any speculation as money you can afford to lose.

Fraud that travels through trust

Affinity fraud, romance scams and "pig butchering"

The most damaging scams today do not start with an investment pitch. They start with a relationship: a fellow church member, a friendly stranger who texted the wrong number, a match on a dating app. By the time money comes up, the victim trusts the person.

Affinity fraud
What it is
Investment fraud aimed at a group, such as a religious, military or senior community. The SEC notes that fraudsters often are, or pretend to be, members of the group, and "enlist respected leaders from the group to spread the word"; "many times, those leaders become unwitting victims."
Used for
Replacing evidence with trust. Victims may try to work things out within the group rather than go to the authorities.
What it means for you
"Never make an investment based solely on the recommendation of a member of an organization or group to which you belong" (SEC). Check the seller and the investment yourself.
"Pig butchering"cryptocurrency investment fraud
What it is
A long con, named from the Chinese term sha zhu pan. The scammer "fattens" the victim with friendship or romance, then introduces a trading platform that shows large fake profits. When the victim tries to withdraw, they are told to pay "taxes" or "fees" first; then the scammer disappears (FinCEN, 2023; FBI, 2025).
Used for
Stealing very large sums: victims are encouraged to add more money, even borrow, because the fake balance keeps rising.
What it means for you
Anyone you know only online who brings up investing, especially in cryptocurrency or gold, is a red flag. A profit you cannot withdraw is not a profit.

In its 2025 report, the FBI calls cryptocurrency investment fraud "the highest source of financial losses to Americans in 2025," with 61,559 complaints and $7.2 billion of reported losses, a 48% increase in complaints from 2024. The FBI says these scams are "largely perpetrated by organized criminal enterprises based in Southeast Asia using victims of human trafficking as forced labor to run the scam operations." Older Americans lost the most.

Going furtherHow the script usually runs

The FBI describes the pattern: contact "through text messages, social media sites, advertisements, or dating applications," then a quick move to a private messaging app; introduction to an "investment group" of supposed insiders; fake "profits" shown on the platform; offers of loans to encourage larger investments; and, at withdrawal, demands for taxes and fees. Victims are then often targeted a second time by recovery scams promising to get their money back for a fee. The FBI's 2025 report also counted about 1,600 complaints and $160 million of losses from fake "investment clubs" on social media and messaging apps, some using artificial intelligence (AI) to generate videos and voices of celebrities or executives.

What every scam has in common

Twelve red flags

Scams change their costumes (stamps in the 1920s, hedge funds in the 2000s, tokens and AI today) but not their skeleton. Nearly all of them combine three things: a return that is too good or too smooth, a seller or product that is not properly registered, and pressure that keeps you from checking. Tick every statement that fits an offer you have received.

⚠️

Steady returns are a warning, not a comfort. From 1928 to 2025 the S&P 500 with dividends lost money in 26 of 98 calendar years (computed from Damodaran, NYU Stern). An investment that reports gains every month, whatever the market does, needs an explanation. Madoff's did not have one.

Two minutes that can save a lifetime of savings

How to check registration

Federal and state law requires people who sell securities or give investment advice for pay, with limited exceptions, to be registered, and most securities offered to the public must be registered or qualify for an exemption. Registration does not guarantee honesty or good advice, but its absence is the single strongest warning sign. The checks are free.

FINRA BrokerCheckbrokercheck.finra.org
What it is
A free database from the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA) of brokers and brokerage firms, with their registrations, employment history, licensing exams and any customer complaints or disciplinary actions.
Used for
Checking anyone who sells you stocks, bonds, funds or annuities through a brokerage.
What it means for you
If the person or firm is not there at all, stop. If there are disclosures, read them.
Investment Adviser Public Disclosure (IAPD)adviserinfo.sec.gov
What it is
The SEC's free database of registered investment advisers and their representatives, including each firm's Form ADV disclosure document; it redirects to BrokerCheck for brokers.
Used for
Checking an adviser's registration, fees, conflicts and disciplinary history before you hire them (INV-103).
What it means for you
Investor.gov's search box sends you here. Search both the person and the firm.

Where the money is held matters. It is safest when your money sits with an independent custodian, such as a large brokerage, that sends statements and confirmations directly to you, so the person managing the money is not the only source of information about it. The Securities Investor Protection Corporation (SIPC) protects customers of a failed member brokerage, up to $500,000 including $250,000 for cash, if their securities and cash are missing. It does not protect against a fall in value, and SIPC states that it "does not protect individuals who are sold worthless stocks and other securities."

Going furtherPrivate offerings and exemptions

Some legitimate investments are sold privately under exemptions from registration, often only to accredited investors (INV-014). "Private" is not the same as "legal," and an exemption does not mean any regulator has reviewed the investment. Ask in writing which exemption applies, and ask your state securities regulator to confirm it. The SEC notes that Ponzi schemes typically involve investments that are not registered with the SEC or with state regulators.

What to do

If you are targeted, or already lost money

Victims often delay because they feel embarrassed, or because the scammer tells them to keep quiet. Speed matters: the FBI says its Recovery Asset Team works best "when complaints are filed as quickly as possible." Here is the order that protects you most.

Where to reportFor
FBI IC3 (ic3.gov)Online and cryptocurrency investment fraud, romance and impersonation scams
SEC tips and complaints (sec.gov/tcr)Securities fraud, Ponzi schemes, pump-and-dumps, problems with a broker or adviser
Your state securities regulator (listed at nasaa.org)Local sellers, unregistered offerings, affinity schemes
FINRA Securities Helpline for Seniors, 844-574-3577, weekdays 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. EasternOlder investors' questions and concerns about brokerage accounts
Federal Trade Commission (ReportFraud.ftc.gov)Consumer scams of all kinds
Trusted contact personFINRA Rule 4512
What it is
A person age 18 or older whom your brokerage may contact about your account, for example if it suspects financial exploitation or cannot reach you. FINRA requires brokerage firms to make reasonable efforts to obtain one for customer accounts other than institutional accounts.
Used for
Giving the firm someone to call before money leaves in a suspicious way.
What it means for you
It gives the person no authority to trade or withdraw. Naming one takes a minute and is one of the best protections for anyone who lives alone or is getting older.

FINRA Rule 2165 adds a second safeguard for "specified adults," people 65 and older or 18 and older with an impairment. If a brokerage reasonably believes one of them is being financially exploited, it may place a temporary hold on a disbursement for up to 15 business days, extendable by 10 more and then by up to 30 more under stated conditions, while it investigates.

From the public record

Three frauds, three lessons

Case study 1Bernard Madoff, 1960s–2008
What happened
Madoff, who founded Bernard L. Madoff Investment Securities in 1960, ran what the Justice Department calls the world's largest Ponzi scheme. According to the SEC, the firm reported more than $17 billion under management at the start of 2008. On December 10, 2008 he told two senior employees it was "basically, a giant Ponzi scheme," and estimated losses of at least $50 billion; the SEC charged him the next day. He pleaded guilty to 11 federal felonies on March 12, 2009 and on June 29, 2009 was sentenced to 150 years in prison and ordered to forfeit $170.8 billion.
What it teaches
The red flags were there: unusually consistent returns, a strategy few could explain, and doubts about whether the claimed trading was happening at all. The SEC's Inspector General found that between June 1992 and December 2008 the SEC received six substantive complaints raising red flags, and conducted three examinations and two investigations without uncovering the fraud. Regulators are not a substitute for your own checks. Recovery was unusually strong: the Justice Department's Madoff Victim Fund paid more than $4.3 billion to 40,930 victims in 127 countries, bringing them to 93.71% of their fraud losses by its final distribution in December 2024, separately from the bankruptcy trustee's distributions.
Source
SEC press release 2008-293 (Dec. 11, 2008); SEC Office of Inspector General, Report OIG-509 (2009); U.S. Attorney's Office, Southern District of New York, via FBI (June 29, 2009); U.S. Department of Justice press releases (Sept. 28, 2022; Dec. 30, 2024).
Case study 2Operation Token Mirrors, 2024
What happened
On October 9, 2024 federal prosecutors in Boston announced charges against 18 individuals and entities, including leaders of four cryptocurrency companies and four "market makers," for fraud and manipulation. According to the charges, the companies made false statements about their tokens and used wash trades to create fake activity, then sold at inflated prices: a pump-and-dump. NexFundAI, a token created at the direction of law enforcement, was used to catch market makers offering these services. More than $25 million in cryptocurrency was seized. One firm, Gotbit, later pleaded guilty; in June 2025 its founder was sentenced to eight months in prison and the firm was ordered to forfeit about $23 million.
What it teaches
Trading volume and "trending" lists can be manufactured. The Justice Department called these "cases where an innovative technology — cryptocurrency — met a century old scheme — the pump and dump."
Source
U.S. Attorney's Office, District of Massachusetts, press releases (Oct. 9, 2024; June 13, 2025). Charges not resulting in a plea or conviction are allegations.
Case study 3Prince Group, charged 2025
What happened
On October 14, 2025 the Justice Department unsealed an indictment charging Chen Zhi, chairman of Cambodia-based Prince Holding Group, with wire fraud conspiracy and money laundering conspiracy. Prosecutors allege Prince Group ran forced-labor compounds whose trafficked workers carried out "pig butchering" cryptocurrency scams against victims worldwide, including over 250 people in the United States reached through a Brooklyn-based network. The department filed a civil forfeiture complaint against about 127,271 bitcoin, then worth about $15 billion, the largest forfeiture action in its history. The Treasury designated Prince Group a transnational criminal organization. When the charges were announced, the defendant was at large.
What it teaches
The friendly stranger on the other end of a "wrong number" text may be a trafficked worker reading a script for an industrial operation. These are not individual con artists but organized businesses.
Source
U.S. Department of Justice, Office of Public Affairs, press release 25-1,031 (Oct. 14, 2025). An indictment is an allegation; the defendant is presumed innocent unless proven guilty.

Decisions

Three approaches, three households

Scenario 1 · Ruth

Ruth gets a call from someone who says he is from her bank's fraud department. Her IRA "has been compromised," he says, and she must move $200,000 to a "secure account" today, or withdraw cash to buy gold that a courier will collect for safekeeping. He knows her address. What should she do?

Follow the instructionsThis is an impersonation scam. No bank or government agency asks you to move money to a "safe account" or hand cash or gold to a courier. The FBI's 2025 report counted about 725 gold-courier complaints with $311.8 million of losses.
Hang up and call backRight. Using a number she finds herself defeats the scam. Knowing personal details proves nothing: they are widely available. If Ruth has named a trusted contact on her accounts (FINRA Rule 4512), her brokerage can reach that person if something looks wrong, and at 68 she is covered by the temporary-hold rule for people 65 and older.
Ask for an ID numberAny number he gives her is made up, and staying on the line lets him build pressure. The only safe verification uses contact details she finds independently.
The principle: urgency plus secrecy plus an unusual way to move money is a scam, whoever the caller claims to be.
Scenario 2 · Jordan

Jordan joins a free "investment club" on a messaging app, led by a "professor" whose daily stock picks have risen. Members post screenshots of big gains. The club now invites Jordan to move money to a special trading app for "VIP" picks, starting with $5,000 from Ava's college savings. What should Jordan do?

Try it with $5,000This is the investment-club scam the FBI warns about: the free picks may be real or may be pumps, the other "members" may be part of the operation, and the app may show invented balances. About 1,600 people reported $160 million lost to such clubs in 2025. Ava's college money, needed in three years, should not be at risk in any case.
Leave, check and reportRight. A legitimate adviser is registered and does not recruit through anonymous chat groups, and money should only ever go to a brokerage you have verified yourself.
The principle: a string of winning tips is how trust is bought before the real request.
Scenario 3 · Maya

After three weeks of friendly chats, a dating-app match who says they work in finance shows Maya screenshots of cryptocurrency profits and offers to teach her. Her $200 test deposit on the recommended platform shows $340 a week later. Now they suggest she invest her $3,000 of savings. What should she do?

Invest the savingsThis follows the "pig butchering" script exactly. The balance on a fake platform is a number on a screen, designed to encourage larger deposits.
Test a withdrawalBetter than investing more, and the result is predictable: a demand for "taxes" or "fees" before release is the FBI's signature of these scams. But any further contact gives the scammer another chance to persuade.
Stop and reportRight. Someone she has never met who brings up investing is the red flag; the "profit" is the bait. Reporting quickly gives investigators the best chance of tracing the $200.
The principle: a profit you cannot withdraw is not a profit.

Apply it

Each household's most likely approach

A simple rule. Maya never sends money or cryptocurrency to anyone she has not met in person, and only invests through her 401(k) and a brokerage she opened herself.
Idle cash attracts pitches. The $41,000 in checking makes the Riveras a target for "safe, high-yield" private notes offered through friends or community groups. A two-minute BrokerCheck and IAPD search, and a call to Arizona's securities regulator, come before any discussion of returns.
Protect Ava's college money. As a single parent under time pressure, Jordan is exactly who investment clubs promise to help "catch up." The college fund stays in its planned account, and no chat group gets a cent.
Wealth brings "exclusive" offers. With $1.1 million saved, the Harpers will be offered private placements. They should ask which exemption applies, where the money will be held, and check everything on EDGAR and IAPD, and ask their advisor to put a recommendation in writing (INV-014, INV-103).
Name a trusted contact now. Widowed and living alone, Ruth is a prime target for impersonation and romance scams. Naming Anna as trusted contact on her IRA and brokerage accounts, and agreeing that Ruth will call Anna before moving any money, are simple and powerful protections.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A promoter promises "only 1% a month." What annual return is that, compounded, in percent?

1.0112 = 1.1268, so 12.68% a year, every year, with no losing months. Compare 10.0% a year for US stocks since 1928, with a loss in 26 of 98 years.
Exercise 2

A scheme raises $10 million, promises 12% a year, takes in $5 million of new money, pays withdrawals of 8% of the starting balance ($0.8 million), and the operator takes 3% of the starting cash ($0.3 million). Nothing is invested. After one year, how big is the hole between reported balances and cash, in millions of dollars?

Reported: 10 × 1.12 + 5 − 0.8 = $15.4 million. Cash: 10 + 5 − 0.8 − 0.3 = $13.9 million. Hole: $1.5 million, and it grows every year, as the simulator shows.
Exercise 3

Of $7,228 million of reported cryptocurrency investment-fraud losses in 2025, people 60 and older reported $2,763.9 million. What share is that, in percent?

2,763.9 ÷ 7,228 = 0.382, or 38.2%, although they filed 13,685 of 61,559 complaints, about 22%. Their average loss per complaint was much larger.
Exercise 4

A Madoff victim's recognized fraud loss was $200,000. The Madoff Victim Fund's distributions brought victims to 93.71% of their losses. How much had this victim recovered, in dollars?

$200,000 × 0.9371 = $187,420, recovered over roughly 15 years with no growth in the meantime.
