The Behavior Gap and Bear Markets

Over the ten years to 2025, US funds returned 9.9% a year, but the average dollar invested in them earned 8.7%. The difference came from when investors bought and sold. This module measures that gap, walks through every major US bear market since 1929, and shows how to prepare so the next one does not become a permanent loss.

The question this module answersWhen the market falls 30%, what will I do, and how do I make sure it is the right thing?

Start here

How can investors earn less than the funds they own?

If a fund returns 10% a year for ten years, surely everyone in it earned 10%? Only if they all put their money in on day one and never touched it. Real investors add money at different times, and take it out at different times. When they buy after prices have risen and sell after prices have fallen, the average dollar earns less than the fund.

That shortfall has a name: the behavior gap. Morningstar measures it every year in a study called Mind the Gap. The 2026 edition covers US mutual funds and exchange-traded funds over the ten years to December 31, 2025. The funds' aggregate total return was 9.9% a year. The average dollar invested in them earned 8.7%. The gap, about 1.2 percentage points a year, equals about 12% of the funds' return. It was not the funds' fault; it reflects "the timing and size of investors' purchases and sales."

The good news is in the same study. Investors in US stock funds earned 12.8% a year, almost all of those funds' 13.3%. They largely held on through 2020 and 2022, and their resolve paid. The gap is widest where emotions run highest: in volatile funds, in specialized sectors, and in bear markets. That is why this module pairs the two topics.

What this module covers

  1. How the behavior gap is measured, where it is largest, and a debate about how big it really is.
  2. Every US bear market since 1929, with verified peak-to-trough declines.
  3. How long recoveries took, with and without dividends and inflation.
  4. A "stay the course" simulator that replays history for an investor who sells after a bad year.
  5. How to prepare before the next decline, and what to do during one.

Measuring the cost of timing

Total return versus investor return

A fund's published return is a time-weighted return: the growth of one dollar invested at the start and left alone (INV-057). It measures the fund manager. An investor return is a money-weighted or dollar-weighted return: it weights each period by how much money was actually in the fund. It measures the investors.

Investor returndollar-weighted return
What it is
The annual rate that, applied to every purchase and sale made by all of a fund's investors, reproduces the fund's actual ending assets. Formally it is an internal rate of return on the fund's cash flows.
Used for
Comparing what investors actually experienced with what the fund delivered. The difference is the behavior gap, sometimes called the investor return gap.
What it means for you
Your brokerage statement may show your personal rate of return. If it is well below your funds' returns over the same period, your timing, not your funds, is the problem to fix.
Starting assets × (1 + IR)T + Σ flowt × (1 + IR)T−t = ending assets  ·  solve for the investor return IR

Money-weighted returns are lower than time-weighted returns whenever more money is in the fund during its bad periods than during its good ones: when investors buy after a run-up (just before a fall) and sell after a fall (just before a recovery). Morningstar's 2026 results by fund type show where that happens most.

Allocation funds, such as target-date and balanced funds, stood out. They rebalance themselves, are often bought through automatic retirement-plan contributions, and give investors little reason to tinker. Morningstar's takeaway: "Hold fewer, more widely diversified funds and, where possible, opt for strategies that handle routine tasks like rebalancing." Volatility mattered even more, as Figure 61.3 shows.

With the defaults, $100,000 plus $6,000 a year for 30 years grows to about $2.76 million at 9.9% but about $2.06 million at 8.7%. A gap of 1.2 points costs about $700,000, a quarter of the ending wealth, without appearing on any statement.

Going furtherHow big is the gap, really? A debate

Sources disagree, and you should know it. A 2026 paper in the Financial Analysts Journal by Jon Fulkerson, Bradford Jordan, Timothy Riley and Qing Yan re-examined Morningstar's 2025 study, which had found a 1.2-point gap for the ten years to 2024. Using the same sample, they concluded that poor timing cost fund investors only about 0.10% a year; much of the measured gap, in their view, reflects the pattern of when money arrives (for example, savers who contribute more each year as their pay rises put more money in later), not bad decisions. Morningstar itself says the gap "appears to reflect a number of factors, including the context in which investors utilize funds." Both sides agree on the direction: the gap is larger in volatile and specialized funds, and investors who trade more capture less of their funds' returns.

Practitioner depthRolling gaps and how to measure your own

Morningstar's rolling ten-year gap for all US funds was −1.73 points for the period ending 2021, −1.68 (2022), −1.08 (2023), −1.20 (2024) and −1.15 (2025). To measure your own, list every contribution and withdrawal with its date, add the starting and ending balances, and use a spreadsheet's XIRR function. Compare it with the time-weighted return of a simple benchmark mix over the same dates (INV-057). A personal gap of a few tenths of a point may be noise; a gap of several points is a signal.

History

Every US bear market since 1929

Bear market
What it is
A fall of at least 20% from a previous high, measured on closing prices. A fall of 10% to 20% is usually called a correction. A bull market is the rise from one bear market's low to the next high.
Used for
A shared vocabulary for severe declines. The 20% line is a convention, not a law of nature: the S&P 500 fell 19.9% in 1990, 19.3% in 1998, 19.8% in 2018 and 18.9% from February to April 2025, and none of those counts.
What it means for you
Since 1946 there has been one about every six years. If you invest for 40 years, expect to live through six or seven, and plan for them.

Going furtherSecular bear markets

Some periods are long rather than deep; they are called secular bear markets. The S&P 500 closed at 94.06 in February 1966 and at 102.42 in August 1982: sixteen years of almost no price progress, with inflation eroding real value throughout. From March 2000 to March 2013 prices again went nowhere, through two bear markets. Dividends, rebalancing and steady saving are what make such periods tolerable: buying through a flat decade is what makes the following decade rewarding.

How long is the wait?

Recoveries: price, dividends and inflation

The table's last column counts from the price peak to the first close above it. That overstates the wait for an investor who reinvests dividends, and understates it after inflation. Three examples, computed from calendar-year returns (Damodaran, NYU Stern):

  • The Great Depression. The price index did not regain its September 1929 high until September 1954, 25 years later. But $1 invested at the start of 1929 with dividends reinvested was back above $1 by the end of 1936, eight calendar years later, helped by high dividend yields and falling consumer prices.
  • 1973–1974. With dividends, money invested at the start of 1973 was back to even by the end of 1976. After inflation, which averaged far above normal, it took until the end of 1984.
  • 2008. With dividends, money invested at the start of 2008 was back to even by the end of 2012; the price index did not regain its October 2007 close until March 2013.
Recovery time
What it is
How long an investment takes to regain a previous high after a fall.
Used for
Judging how long money might be "under water" and planning spending so that you are not forced to sell at the bottom.
What it means for you
Money you will need within about five years should not depend on stocks recovering in time. Money for later can wait, and new contributions made during a recovery buy at low prices.

Recovery is harder than the fall, because percentages work against you. A 50% fall needs a 100% gain to get back: $100,000 falls to $50,000, and $50,000 must double.

Gain needed = D ÷ (1 − D)  ·  25% fall → 33% gain  ·  50% fall → 100% gain  ·  57% fall → 131% gain
Going furtherWhy savers recover faster than the index

The tool assumes a single lump sum with no new money. Most people in their working years keep contributing through a decline, buying shares at lower prices. Their balance usually regains its old high well before the index does. The reverse is true for retirees who withdraw during a decline: they sell shares at low prices and need an even larger gain to recover. That asymmetry is the subject of sequence risk (INV-077).

Two decisions, not one

Selling after a fall versus holding

Selling in a bear market feels like one decision. It is really two: when to get out, and when to get back in. The first is easy because the pain tells you when. The second is hard, because the best days of a recovery usually arrive while the news is still bad. The seller must be right twice.

The simulator below replays history for two investors who start with the same amount in the same mix. One holds throughout. The other sells at the end of any calendar year worse than a threshold you choose, parks the money in Treasury bills, and buys back after a set number of years.

With the defaults, $100,000 invested in US stocks at the start of 2007 and held grew to about $685,000 by the end of 2025. The seller, who sold at the end of 2008 after a 36.6% fall and waited three years in Treasury bills, ended with about $466,000, roughly $219,000 less. Waiting only one year still cost about $140,000, because the seller missed 2009's 25.9% rebound. Starting in 2000 with the same rule, the seller sold twice, in 2002 and 2008, and ended with less than half of the holder's result.

⚠️

The honest exception. Start the simulator in 1929 and end in 1945, and the seller, who sold after 1930 and again after 1937, finishes about 19% ahead of the holder, mainly because stocks fell another 44% in 1931. History contains such windows. The problem is that no one knows in advance which kind of decline they are in, and in most of the record since 1946, falls were followed by recoveries within a few years.

Going furtherWhy year-end data understate the problem

The simulator uses calendar-year returns, so the seller sells at year-end and buys back at year-end. Real panics happen near intra-year lows, and real recoveries start abruptly: after the S&P 500's March 9, 2009 low, the index had risen about 65% by the end of that year. Missing a few months of a recovery can cost as much as a whole year in the simulator. Market timing is covered in depth in INV-052.

Practitioner depthWhat the simulator computes

Each year the holder's value grows by the mix's return, s × stocks + (1 − s) × 10-year Treasuries, rebalanced annually. The seller's value grows the same way while invested; if that year's mix return is at or below the threshold, the seller moves to 3-month T-bills from the next year. After the chosen number of years in bills, the seller reinvests at the next year-end. Sales can repeat. No taxes, which would further penalize a seller in a taxable account holding gains, and no trading costs.

Before and during

Prepare in calm weather

The time to decide what you will do in a bear market is before it starts. Every item below is easier to set up when markets are calm, and each one removes a reason you might be forced, or tempted, to sell at the bottom.

Before a decline

  1. Hold an emergency fund. Three to six months of essential expenses in cash is a common guideline (INV-019). Job losses often coincide with bear markets; the fund means you never have to sell stocks to pay rent.
  2. Choose a mix you can actually hold. Look at the worst historical year and the worst decline for your mix (INV-003, INV-004), convert them to dollars, and ask whether you would sell. If yes, hold less in stocks now. Your risk capacity, not only your tolerance, sets the limit (INV-025).
  3. Keep near-term spending out of stocks. Money needed within about five years, such as a house down payment, tuition or the first years of retirement withdrawals, belongs in cash, CDs or short-term bonds (INV-008, INV-076).
  4. Write it down. An investment policy statement that says "in a 30% fall I will keep contributing and rebalance" is a commitment you made to yourself when calm (INV-028).

During a decline

  1. Keep contributing. Payroll contributions buy more shares at lower prices; stopping them is a form of selling.
  2. Rebalance. If stocks have fallen from 60% to 50% of your portfolio, rebalancing means selling some bonds and buying stocks (INV-056). It is uncomfortable and mechanical, which is the point.
  3. Harvest losses and consider conversions. In taxable accounts, selling a losing fund and buying a similar but not identical one realizes a tax loss while staying invested (INV-037). Converting pre-tax IRA money to a Roth when values are low moves more shares for the same tax (INV-038).
Going furtherFor retirees: a spending reserve

Retirees who must sell stocks during a decline to pay the bills lock in losses permanently, which is why the first years of retirement carry the most risk (INV-077). A common approach is to hold one to three years of planned withdrawals, beyond what Social Security and pensions cover, in cash and short-term bonds, and to draw from that reserve when stocks are down. INV-076 compares bucket and guardrail strategies.

From real history

Three bear markets, three lessons

Case study 1Black Monday, October 19, 1987
What happened
The S&P 500 fell from 282.70 to 224.84 in a single day, a drop of 20.5%, still its largest one-day percentage fall. From its August 25, 1987 high to the December 4 low, the index fell 33.5%. It regained the August high in July 1989, less than two years after the peak.
What it teaches
Speed is not the same as severity. A crash that felt like the end of the world was, for a patient investor, a two-year interruption. For 1987 as a calendar year the S&P 500 with dividends still gained about 5%.
Source
S&P 500 daily closes (S&P Dow Jones Indices via Yahoo Finance); Yardeni Research (2024); Damodaran, NYU Stern (1987 total return 5.8%).
Case study 2The global financial crisis, 2007–2009
What happened
From its October 9, 2007 close of 1,565.15, the S&P 500 fell 56.8% to 676.53 on March 9, 2009, over 17 months. In calendar 2008, stocks with dividends lost 36.6% while 10-year Treasuries gained 20.1%. In 2009 stocks gained 25.9%. With dividends, money invested at the start of 2008 was back to even by the end of 2012.
What it teaches
High-quality bonds cushioned diversified portfolios, and rebalancing into stocks in early 2009 bought at the lowest prices in over a decade. The investors who were hurt most were those forced or frightened into selling near the bottom.
Source
Yardeni Research (2024); Damodaran, NYU Stern, historical returns (2026).
Case study 3The fastest bear market, 2020
What happened
As the COVID-19 pandemic spread, the S&P 500 fell 33.9% in 33 days, from 3,386.15 on February 19 to 2,237.40 on March 23, 2020. On March 16 alone it fell 12.0%. It closed above its February high on August 18, 2020, and finished the year up 18.0% with dividends.
What it teaches
No one could time this. Anyone who sold in March and waited for the news to improve bought back at higher prices; the recovery began while infections were still rising. Morningstar found that US stock-fund investors, who largely held on through 2020 and 2022, captured almost all of their funds' returns over the decade.
Source
FRED series SP500 (S&P Dow Jones Indices); Yardeni Research (2024); Damodaran, NYU Stern; Morningstar, Mind the Gap 2026.

Decisions

Three households in a 25% decline

Scenario 1 · The Riveras

Stocks have fallen 25%. Marcus suggests pausing their 401(k) contributions "until things settle down," and moving the $62,000 already saved into the plan's stable-value fund. What should they do?

Pause and moveThis is selling low twice: the existing balance leaves stocks after the fall, and new money stops buying at lower prices. They are 30 years from retirement; the fall matters only if they lock it in.
Keep contributing and rebalanceRight. Every contribution now buys more shares. Any employer match is an immediate return that no market timing can replace. The $41,000 idle in checking, not their 401(k), is the place to build extra safety.
Stop contributing onlyLess damaging, but it gives up any match and the chance to buy at lower prices. Contributions are the saver's built-in advantage in a bear market.
The principle: for a saver decades from retirement, a bear market is a sale, not a threat.
Scenario 2 · Ruth

Ruth needs about $1,500 a month from savings on top of her $2,900 Social Security. Stocks have fallen 25%, and her IRA is well below its peak. A friend says to move everything to CDs "before it gets worse." What should she do?

All to CDsThis makes the paper loss permanent and leaves her with CD rates for a retirement that may last 25 years or more. Inflation then becomes the risk (INV-001).
Use the cash reserveRight. This is exactly what the reserve is for. At $1,500 a month, $60,000 covers more than three years of withdrawals without selling stocks at depressed prices. When stocks recover, she refills the reserve.
Keep selling stocksSelling shares at low prices to fund spending is how sequence risk does its damage (INV-077). Her reserve lets her avoid it.
The principle: in retirement, the defense against bear markets is a spending reserve set up in advance.
Scenario 3 · The Harpers

In a 2022-style year, both stocks and bonds fall. The Harpers' 25 technology stocks are down 35%, and retirement is five years away. Tom wants to sell everything and "wait in cash." What is the better move?

All to cashIt ends the pain and swaps one risk for two: missing the recovery, and deciding when to return. It also realizes gains on positions still above cost.
Harvest, diversify, build a reserveRight. A bear market is the cheapest time, tax-wise, to fix a concentrated taxable portfolio: many positions are below cost, so selling them realizes losses rather than gains (INV-037). The reserve addresses their real risk, which is the first years of withdrawals (INV-077).
The principle: use a decline to make the portfolio you should have had, not to abandon a plan.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

The S&P 500 fell 34% in 2020. What percentage gain from the low was needed to get back to the peak?

Gain needed = D ÷ (1 − D) = 0.34 ÷ 0.66 = 51.5%. It came within five months.
Exercise 2

The S&P 500 closed at 1,565.15 on October 9, 2007 and at 676.53 on March 9, 2009. What was the peak-to-trough change, in percent? (Enter a negative number.)

676.53 ÷ 1,565.15 = 0.432, so the change was −56.8%.
Exercise 3

After a 50% fall, how many years does it take to recover at 8% a year, with no new contributions?

The value must double. ln(1 ÷ 0.5) ÷ ln(1.08) = 0.693 ÷ 0.077 = 9.0 years. The Rule of 72 gives 72 ÷ 8 = 9 (INV-002).
Exercise 4

$100,000 is invested for 10 years. The fund earns 9.9% a year; the investor, because of timing, earns 8.7%. What does the gap cost, in dollars?

100,000 × 1.09910 = $257,026; 100,000 × 1.08710 = $230,301. The gap costs about $26,725, over 10% of the ending balance.
