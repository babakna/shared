Health Savings Accounts

A health savings account is the one widely available account in the US tax code where money can go in untaxed, grow untaxed and come out untaxed (the older Archer medical savings account, now closed to most new savers, works similarly). It is tied to a particular kind of health insurance, and the 2025 tax law widened who can have one. This module covers who qualifies, the 2026 limits, why many people treat the HSA as a retirement account, and the Medicare rule that ends contributions.

The question this module answersAm I eligible for an HSA, and should I spend it now or invest it for later?

Start here

A health account that can become a retirement account

Most people with a high-deductible health plan think of their HSA as a debit card for doctor visits: money goes in from each paycheck and comes out at the pharmacy counter. That is a perfectly good use of it, and it saves tax on every medical bill.

But the health savings account has a feature no other widely available account has. Traditional 401(k)s are untaxed going in and taxed coming out; Roth accounts are taxed going in and untaxed coming out. An HSA used for qualified medical expenses is never taxed at all. The balance rolls over every year, it stays with you when you change jobs, and it can be invested. Since almost everyone has large medical costs in retirement, many savers pay current bills out of pocket and let the HSA grow for decades.

Who can have one

The HSA's four eligibility tests, and the 2026 numbers

You can contribute to an HSA for a month only if, on the first day of that month, you meet all four tests in IRS Publication 969:

  1. You are covered by a high-deductible health plan (HDHP).
  2. You have no other health coverage, apart from permitted kinds such as dental, vision and certain limited-purpose plans.
  3. You are not enrolled in Medicare.
  4. No one else can claim you as a dependent.

High-deductible health plan (HDHP)
What it is
A health plan with a deductible at least the minimum above and out-of-pocket costs no more than the maximum. Preventive care can be covered before the deductible.
Used for
Qualifying for an HSA. Premiums are usually lower than for a traditional plan, and you pay more of your routine costs yourself.
What it means for you
An HDHP plus an HSA works best for people who can cover the deductible from cash, or from the HSA, without strain. A family expecting large bills should compare total yearly cost, premiums plus likely out-of-pocket, not just the premium.
💡

What Public Law 119-21 changed. (1) Telehealth and other remote care can now be covered before the deductible without losing HSA eligibility, permanently, for plan years beginning on or after January 1, 2025. (2) From January 1, 2026, bronze and catastrophic plans are treated as HSA-compatible, whether bought through an Exchange or not (IRS Notice 2026-05). (3) From January 1, 2026, people in certain direct primary care arrangements, a flat monthly fee for primary care, can still contribute, and can pay those fees tax-free from the HSA, if the fees are no more than $150 a month ($300 for arrangements covering more than one person). Sources: IRS IR-2025-119 (December 9, 2025); IRS, "Working Families Tax Cuts" provisions page; 26 U.S.C. §223(c) as amended.

Going furtherThe last-month rule and its testing period

If you are eligible on December 1, you may contribute the full year's limit even if you became eligible late in the year. The catch: you must stay eligible through December 31 of the following year (the "testing period"). If you do not, for reasons other than death or disability, the extra contribution becomes taxable income plus a 10% additional tax. Otherwise, the limit is prorated by the months you were eligible (IRS Publication 969).

Practitioner depthCouples and family coverage

If either spouse has family HDHP coverage, both are treated as having family coverage, and the $8,750 family limit is shared between them as they agree. Catch-ups are personal: each spouse aged 55 or older adds $1,000 to their own HSA, so a couple both over 55 could contribute $10,750 for 2026. Employer contributions, including your own salary reductions through a cafeteria plan, count toward the limit. Excess contributions face a 6% excise tax for each year they remain.

The math

Untaxed going in, growing, and coming out

The triple tax advantage
What it is
(1) Contributions are deductible, or excluded from pay if made through payroll; (2) interest, dividends and gains inside the account are not taxed; (3) withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free.
Used for
Paying medical costs with pre-tax dollars now, or building a tax-free fund for medical costs in retirement.
What it means for you
Payroll contributions also skip the 7.65% Social Security and Medicare tax, a fourth saving the 401(k) does not offer. Withdrawals for anything else are taxable, plus a 20% additional tax before age 65.

With the defaults, $4,400 a year for 25 years at 6%, the HSA grows to about $256,000, all of it spendable on medical care with no tax. The same pay through a traditional 401(k) leaves about $189,000 after tax, and in a taxable account about $153,000. Even spent on non-medical things after 65, when it is taxed like a traditional individual retirement account (IRA), the HSA leaves about $205,000, because the payroll tax was never paid.

Going furtherWhat counts as a qualified medical expense

Broadly, amounts that would qualify for the medical expense deduction: doctor and hospital bills, prescriptions, dental and vision care, and many other costs, for you, your spouse and dependents. Insurance premiums generally do not qualify, with four exceptions: long-term care insurance (within age-based limits), COBRA continuation coverage, coverage while receiving unemployment compensation, and, once you are 65, Medicare premiums and other health coverage except Medigap supplemental policies (IRS Publication 969). The same expense cannot be both reimbursed tax-free from the HSA and deducted on Schedule A.

Cash or invested?

An HSA left in cash wastes its best feature

Many HSA providers hold contributions in a cash account by default, and some require a minimum cash balance before you can invest the rest in mutual funds or exchange-traded funds (ETFs). For money you expect to spend this year, cash is right. For money you plan to leave for decades, cash earning little forfeits most of the tax-free growth that makes the HSA special.

  • Check the fees and the menu. Your employer picks an HSA provider for payroll deposits, but you are free to move the money: a direct trustee-to-trustee transfer to another HSA is not taxed and is not limited in number (IRS Publication 969). Low-cost index funds matter here as much as anywhere (INV-007).
  • Match the investment to the horizon. Money for next year's deductible belongs in cash; money for costs at 75 can be invested like other long-term savings.
  • Asset location. Because nothing in an HSA used for medical costs is ever taxed, it is the natural home for the investments you expect to grow the most (INV-036).

Pay now, reimburse later

The receipts strategy

Publication 969 sets three conditions for a tax-free withdrawal: the expense was for qualified medical care, it was incurred after the HSA was established, and it was not paid or reimbursed from another source or deducted. It also says you do not have to take withdrawals each year. It does not set a deadline for reimbursing yourself. That combination makes a simple strategy possible.

⚠️

Only if the cash flow allows. The strategy requires paying medical bills from other money. For a family whose emergency fund is thin, using the HSA as intended, to pay bills as they come, is the better choice. And the receipts must survive: store them where they will still be readable in 20 years.

Age 65 and beyond

How Medicare ends contributions, and what changes at 65

Medicare stops contributions
What it is
From the first month you are enrolled in any part of Medicare, your HSA contribution limit is zero. This includes months of retroactive coverage: if you apply for Medicare after 65, Part A can be backdated, and contributions made during the backdated months become excess contributions.
Used for
Keeping Medicare enrollees out of HSAs, since Medicare is not an HDHP.
What it means for you
Medicare.gov advises that you and your employer stop HSA contributions six months before you retire or apply for Social Security benefits, because Part A coverage starts up to six months before the month you sign up or apply for Social Security benefits, though not before the month you turn 65.

When the account owner dies

If your spouse is the designated beneficiary, the HSA simply becomes your spouse's HSA. If anyone else inherits it, it stops being an HSA and its full value becomes taxable income to that beneficiary in the year of death, reduced by any of your medical bills they pay within a year (IRS Publication 969). For that reason many planners suggest spending an HSA down in retirement rather than leaving it to children, and leaving Roth IRAs instead (INV-091).

Real law and a real IRS example

Three cases

Case study 1The HSA's creation, 2003
What happened
Section 223 was added to the Internal Revenue Code by the Medicare Prescription Drug, Improvement, and Modernization Act of 2003 (P.L. 108-173, December 8, 2003), the law best known for creating Medicare Part D. It paired a tax-favored savings account with high-deductible insurance.
What it teaches
The HSA was designed to encourage people to shop for routine care with their own money, with a tax-free account to soften the higher deductible. Its use as a long-term investment account is a side effect of the rules, not the original purpose, which is one reason its rules could change.
Source
26 U.S.C. §223 and statutory notes (Legal Information Institute).
Case study 2Public Law 119-21 and bronze plans, 2026
What happened
Before 2026, many bronze plans sold on the health insurance marketplaces did not meet the HDHP definition, so their enrollees could not open HSAs. P.L. 119-21 treats all bronze and catastrophic plans as HDHPs starting January 1, 2026, and the IRS clarified in Notice 2026-05 that the plans need not be bought through an Exchange.
What it teaches
Eligibility is set by law and can widen or narrow. Self-employed people and early retirees buying their own coverage should recheck HSA eligibility each open-enrollment season.
Source
IRS, IR-2025-119 (December 9, 2025).
Case study 3The IRS's own Medicare example
What happened
IRS Publication 969 (2025) describes a taxpayer who turned 65 in July 2025 and enrolled in Medicare, with self-only HDHP coverage. Eligible only for January through June, the taxpayer's 2025 limit was prorated: ($4,300 + $1,000 catch-up) × 6 ÷ 12 = $2,650.
What it teaches
The year you enroll in Medicare, count months carefully, and remember that a late application can make earlier months retroactively ineligible. At 2026 limits the same person would be limited to ($4,400 + $1,000) × 6 ÷ 12 = $2,700.
Source
IRS Publication 969 (2025), "Enrolled in Medicare".

Decisions

Three HSA decisions

Scenario 1 · The Riveras

Open enrollment offers the Riveras a PPO or a family HDHP with an HSA and a $1,000 employer contribution. With two young children, which should they pick?

PPO by defaultMay be right for a family with a chronic condition or planned major care, but children's preventive care is covered before the deductible in an HDHP too.
Compare total costRight. The Riveras also have $41,000 of idle cash to absorb a bad year up to the $17,000 family out-of-pocket maximum. An HSA would give them tax savings at 22% on up to $8,750 for 2026.
The principle: choose a health plan on expected total cost and your ability to absorb the worst case, then use the HSA if you have one.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers

The Harpers have family HDHP coverage and plan to retire at 62–63. Tom is 57 and Rachel 55. How should they use their HSA?

Spend as they goSaves tax, but gives up years of tax-free growth they will want for medical costs in retirement, including Medicare premiums after 65.
Max, invest, save receiptsRight, given their high income and cash flow: $10,750 a year for 2026, deducted at 22% plus payroll tax if made through payroll. Retiring before 65 means buying coverage on their own; a bronze plan now qualifies for continued HSA contributions.
The principle: in peak earning years, an HSA can be the most tax-efficient savings account a household has.
Scenario 3 · Ruth

Ruth, 68 and on Medicare, hears about HSAs from a friend and wants to open one to save taxes. Can she?

YesNot while enrolled in Medicare. An HSA opened earlier could still be spent, but she cannot put new money in.
NoRight. For Ruth, qualified charitable distributions from her IRA after 70½ and Roth conversions before required minimum distributions (RMDs) are the tax tools that matter (INV-039, INV-038).
The principle: HSAs are for the working years before Medicare; spending them is for the years after.

At 50, in the 22% bracket, you withdraw $2,000 from your HSA for a vacation. What is the federal tax cost, in dollars?

Income tax $440 plus the 20% additional tax $400 = $840. After 65, only the $440.
