The Emergency Fund

An emergency fund is the part of your savings whose job is not to grow but to be there. It keeps a job loss, a car repair or a medical bill from turning into high-interest debt, or into selling investments at the worst moment. This module sizes it for your household, shows where to keep it, and uses the Federal Reserve's survey data to show how many households go without one.

The question this module answersHow much cash should I keep for emergencies, where should it sit, and when should I use it?

Start here

The money whose only job is to be there

Most people already keep some cash for surprises, even if they do not call it an emergency fund. When the car breaks down and there is money in savings, the repair is an annoyance. When there is not, the same repair becomes a credit-card balance at over 20% interest, a missed rent payment, or a hurried sale of investments.

That difference, having an emergency fund or not, is what the Federal Reserve measures every year in its Survey of Household Economics and Decisionmaking (SHED). In the survey fielded in October 2025, 63% of adults said they would cover a hypothetical $400 emergency expense entirely with cash, savings or a credit card paid off at the next statement, unchanged from the past several years. The rest would borrow, sell something, or could not pay at all.

Emergency fund
What it is
Cash, or something just as safe and quick to reach, set aside only for unplanned, necessary expenses and for loss of income.
Used for
Job loss, urgent car or home repairs, medical bills, emergency travel. Not for planned costs, which belong in sinking funds (INV-018).
What it means for you
It is insurance you pay for with a low return. Its value shows up in the bad months: no new debt, no forced sale of investments, and time to make good decisions.

What this module covers

  1. The evidence on household financial fragility.
  2. How big the fund should be, by household type and job stability, with a calculator.
  3. Where to keep it: insured deposits, money market funds, Treasury bills, certificates of deposit (CDs) and I bonds.
  4. How to build it, when to use it, and how to refill it.
  5. Special situations: two incomes, self-employment, homeowners and retirees.

The evidence

How many households are one bill away from trouble

The SHED asks adults who would not pay a $400 expense with cash how they would pay instead. Respondents could choose more than one answer.

Emergencies are not hypothetical. In the same survey, 59% of adults had at least one major unexpected expense in the prior 12 months; the most common were vehicle repairs (30%), home or appliance repairs (22%) and medical expenses (21%). And 16% did not pay all of their bills in the prior month.

Going furtherWhy the $400 question matters

The $400 question has been asked in the same form for years, so it tracks resilience over time. It measures liquidity, not wealth: a homeowner with a large retirement account can still be unable to pay $400 in cash this week. That is exactly the gap an emergency fund fills. The borrowing alternatives in Figure 19.2 can be expensive: the average interest rate on credit card accounts that were charged interest was 22.15% in the second quarter of 2026 (Federal Reserve G.19).

Size it

Three to six months is a starting point, not an answer

The familiar advice is "three to six months of expenses." It is a sensible heuristic, and the right point in (or beyond) that range depends on two questions: how likely is your income to stop, and how long could it take to restart?

Essential expenses
What it is
What your household must pay in a lean month: housing, utilities, food, insurance, transportation, minimum debt payments, childcare. Not dining out, travel or new savings.
Used for
Measuring the fund in months: the fund divided by essential monthly spending.
What it means for you
Sizing on essential spending, not on income or on your full budget, gives a realistic target. For Maya it is $2,737 a month, not her $5,167 gross pay.

Practitioner depthWhat partly replaces income in a job loss

State unemployment insurance replaces part of lost wages for a limited number of weeks; the amount, the maximum and the duration differ by state, and benefits are not immediate. Severance, a working spouse's income and a lower-spending "crisis budget" all reduce the months needed. On the other side, losing employer health coverage adds a large cost: the average employer premium for single coverage was $9,325 a year in 2025 (KFF), most of which the employer had been paying. The heuristic's extra months exist to absorb exactly these uncertainties.

Safe, liquid, separate

Where the emergency fund should live

An emergency fund needs three properties, in this order: safety (its value does not fall), liquidity (you can reach it within a day or two), and then yield (it earns what it reasonably can). Stocks fail the first test; a checking account usually fails the third. Bank deposits are protected by the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC); the other options below are protected in other ways, or not at all.

FDIC insurance
What it is
Federal insurance on bank deposits: $250,000 per depositor, per FDIC-insured bank, for each account ownership category.
Used for
Making checking, savings, money market deposit accounts and CDs at insured banks safe even if the bank fails.
What it means for you
It does not cover mutual funds (including money market funds), stocks, bonds, Treasury securities, annuities or crypto, even when bought through a bank. Treasuries are backed directly by the U.S. government instead.

On a $20,000 fund, the national-average savings rate earns about $74 a year; in Treasury bills near 4.08% it would earn about $816. A high-yield savings account pays whatever its bank sets, so compare rates before choosing. Over a decade the difference adds up to thousands of dollars, for the same safety if the account is FDIC-insured.

Going furtherEmergency money versus inflation

Safe cash barely keeps up with inflation over long periods. From 1928 to 2025, Treasury bills compounded at 3.37% a year against 3.04% inflation: about 0.3% a year after inflation, and they lagged inflation in 40 of those 98 years (Damodaran, NYU Stern). That is the price of safety, and why only the emergency fund, not all your savings, should sit in cash. INV-008 covers cash equivalents in depth.

In practice

Building it, using it, refilling it

A full fund can take a year or more to build. It does not have to be finished before anything else happens: most plans build it in stages alongside other priorities (INV-020).

When to use it

A useful three-part test: is it unexpected, is it necessary, and is it urgent? A job loss, a furnace failure in January or an emergency-room bill pass all three. A sale on a television passes none; a planned vacation is not unexpected. Irregular but predictable costs belong in sinking funds, which keeps the emergency fund intact.

After you use it

  1. Pause extra goals (not the employer match) and redirect that money to refilling the fund.
  2. If income has stopped, switch to a lean "crisis budget" at once; the fund lasts longer the earlier spending falls.
  3. Refill before resuming extra investing or debt prepayment.
⚠️

A credit limit is not an emergency fund. Lenders can lower limits in a downturn, exactly when you need them. And a balance carried at 22.15%, the average annual percentage rate (APR) on accounts charged interest in the second quarter of 2026, turns a $2,000 repair into a much larger bill.

Going furtherBackstops behind the fund

Some savings can serve as a second line of defense, with care. Regular contributions to a Roth individual retirement account (IRA), but not earnings, can be withdrawn without tax or penalty, because under Internal Revenue Service (IRS) ordering rules contributions come out first (INV-032). A health savings account can reimburse medical bills already paid (INV-033). A taxable brokerage account can be sold, though possibly at a loss or with tax due. None of these should be the plan, because each sacrifices long-term growth or tax advantages.

It depends

Two incomes, variable income, homeowners and retirees

  • Dual-income households. Two incomes rarely stop at once unless both work for the same employer or industry. Size the fund on the essentials the remaining income could not cover. Couples at the same company should lean toward the single-income figure.
  • Variable income and self-employment. Income can fall without anyone being laid off. Many self-employed people keep a separate business reserve and pay themselves a steady "salary" from it, and plan for quarterly estimated taxes (INV-101).
  • Homeowners. Roofs, water heaters and insurance deductibles are the owner's problem. Add at least your homeowners deductible, and consider a separate home-maintenance sinking fund (INV-063).
  • Employer stock is not an emergency fund. If your employer struggles, its share price and your job can fall together. Jordan's $56,000 of employer stock is exactly the wrong asset to count on in a layoff (INV-004).
  • Retirees. The paycheck risk is gone; the risks are large irregular costs (home, car, health) and having to sell investments after a market fall. A common approach is to keep one to two years of the spending that Social Security and pensions do not cover in cash and short-term bonds, a "cash bucket" (INV-076).
Practitioner depthWhy not simply keep the fund in stocks?

Emergencies cluster in recessions, and so do stock market losses. In 2008 the S&P 500 returned −36.55% (Damodaran, NYU Stern) while the annual average unemployment rate rose from 5.8% in 2008 to 9.3% in 2009 (BLS via FRED). An emergency fund in stocks would have shrunk by about a third in the very year it was most likely to be needed. The expected extra return does not compensate for failing at the fund's one job.

Real history

Three emergencies in the data

Case study 1April 2020: unemployment quadruples in two months
What happened
The US unemployment rate was 3.5% in February 2020. As the COVID-19 pandemic shut down large parts of the economy it reached 4.4% in March and 14.8% in April, the highest since the monthly series began in 1948. It was still 6.7% in December 2020.
What it teaches
Income shocks can arrive for millions of households at once, with no warning and regardless of individual performance. A fund sized for a typical job search can be tested by an atypical one.
Source
U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Unemployment Rate [UNRATE], retrieved from FRED, Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis.
Case study 22009–2012: the long search
What happened
After the 2008–09 recession, job searches became much longer. The median duration of unemployment averaged 21.5 weeks in 2010, and the average duration averaged 39.4 weeks in both 2011 and 2012, against 16.9 weeks in 2007.
What it teaches
In a deep recession the "three months" at the low end of the usual advice can run out long before a new job arrives. Households with one income, dependents or specialized jobs have the most reason to hold more.
Source
U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Current Population Survey, median and average weeks unemployed [UEMPMED, UEMPMEAN], annual averages, via FRED.
Case study 32025: the Federal Reserve's household survey
What happened
In the SHED fielded October 17–28, 2025, 63% of adults would cover a $400 emergency with cash or its equivalent (unchanged from the past several years), 55% had three months of expenses set aside, and 59% had faced at least one major unexpected expense in the previous year.
What it teaches
Unexpected expenses are normal: more than half of adults had one in a single year. The fund is not a pessimist's hedge; it is ordinary infrastructure.
Source
Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System (2026). Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households in 2025.

Decisions

Three emergency-fund dilemmas

Scenario 1 · Maya

Maya has $3,000 saved and wants to reach her $14,685 target faster. A friend suggests pausing her 6% 401(k) contribution for a year and putting that money into savings. What should she do?

Pause the 401(k)She would give up $1,860 a year of employer match, a 50% return on the money she contributes, and after tax the pause frees less than $310 a month for savings. The fund grows faster; her wealth grows slower.
Keep the matchRight for most people. She already has about one month of essentials, a sensible starter tier. At $400 a month she reaches the target in about 30 months; a roommate or half of a raise shortens that (INV-018).
No fundHer first car repair or a gap between jobs would go on a credit card at over 20% interest. Youth gives her time to recover from market losses, not from cash shortfalls.
The principle: build the fund alongside the match, not instead of it.
Scenario 2 · Jordan

Jordan has about $5,000 of cash beyond Ava's $20,000 college fund, but $56,000 of employer stock in a taxable account. Is the stock their emergency fund?

Count the stockIt is liquid, but it is correlated with the very emergency Jordan most fears: layoffs at the employer usually come with a falling share price.
Sell part to build cashRight. Moving roughly $23,500 into a high-yield savings account or Treasury bills reaches the $28,500 target and cuts the single-stock bet, killing two risks with one decision. Taxes depend on how long each lot was held and how the shares were bought (INV-040).
Borrow from collegeThat money has a date: Ava starts in three years. Using it for emergencies leaves both goals exposed.
The principle: an emergency fund must not depend on the same risk as your paycheck.
Scenario 3 · Ruth

Ruth keeps $60,000 in CDs and savings, about 15 months of her $4,000 essential spending. A friend says it is "dead money." Is it too much?

Invest it allThen a market fall would force her to sell IRA investments at a loss to pay for a roof or a hospital stay. Her IRA already holds most of her growth assets.
Keep it, organizedReasonable. About $18,000 is her emergency reserve; the rest covers a year or more of withdrawals, which lets her avoid selling after a crash (INV-076). A ladder of short CDs or Treasury bills keeps it earning without locking it all up.
The principle: in retirement, cash protects the withdrawal plan as well as the household.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

Essential expenses are $3,400 a month and you want six months of cover (no deductibles added). What is the target, in dollars?

$3,400 × 6 = $20,400.
Exercise 2

Your target is $18,000, you have $4,500, and you can add $450 a month. Ignoring interest, how many months until the fund is full?

Gap = $18,000 − $4,500 = $13,500. $13,500 ÷ $450 = 30 months.
Exercise 3

How much more interest does $20,000 earn in a year at 4.08% than at 0.37%, in dollars?

$20,000 × 0.0408 = $816; $20,000 × 0.0037 = $74. Difference = $742 a year.
Exercise 4

In August 2026 the average spell of unemployment was 26.3 weeks. Using 4.345 weeks per month, how many months is that?

26.3 ÷ 4.345 = 6.05 months, the top of the usual "three to six months." The median, 11.4 weeks, is about 2.6 months.
