Children and Education

Children are not an investment decision, but they are the largest long-running expense most households take on, and the tax code, the financial aid system and a new federal account program all respond to them. This module prices the years from birth to college, walks through every 2026 family tax break, and builds a college plan you can actually afford.

The question this module answersHow do we pay for raising children and their education without sacrificing our own future?

Start here

The longest bill you will ever pay

Nobody decides to have a child because of a spreadsheet, and nothing here suggests they should. But once children arrive, money questions come quickly: child care, a bigger home, a college fund, and whether any of it should come before your own retirement.

The most-cited estimate comes from the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA). Using the government's Consumer Expenditure Survey, its researchers estimated that a middle-income married couple with two children would spend $233,610, in 2015 dollars, raising one child from birth through age 17 — before any college. Adjusted for consumer price inflation through the end of 2025, that is roughly $320,000 in today's dollars. Figure 96.1 shows where that money goes.

Birth to 17

The cost of raising a child, year by year

The USDA estimate is an average across real households, and it moves with income. In 2015 dollars, a two-child married couple spent between $9,330 and $9,980 a year on a child if their before-tax income was under $59,200; between $12,350 and $13,900 in the middle group; and between $19,380 and $23,380 above $107,400. Costs rise as children age, and they vary with family size: an only child cost 27% more than each child in a two-child family, while families with three or more spent 24% less per child.

Direct and indirect costs
What it is
Direct costs are what you spend: housing, food, transportation, clothing, health care, child care and education. Indirect costs are what you give up: time, and the earnings and career progress of a parent who works less.
Used for
Budgeting and life insurance. The USDA counts only direct parental spending through age 17 and explicitly excludes college, time costs and forgone earnings.
What it means for you
For many families the largest cost is indirect. A parent who steps back from a $60,000 job for five years gives up more than the USDA's direct estimate for those years, and also the retirement saving and Social Security credits that job would have earned.
Future yearly cost = Today's cost × (1 + inflation)years from now   (the USDA's projection method)
Going furtherWhy housing is counted as a child cost

The USDA assigns each child a share of the household's housing, transportation and other shared spending, using a per-person method, and measures other items directly from survey data. Housing was 29% of the middle-income total. Families who already own a home large enough may spend less than the average; families who move to a bigger home or a better school district may spend much more. The estimate is a planning guide, not a bill.

Practitioner depthUsing the numbers for insurance

A common way to size term life insurance for a young family is to add up the income the family would need until the youngest child is independent, plus debts and a college goal, and subtract existing savings. For the Riveras, 14 years of child costs for Leo and 11 for Sofia at the middle-income USDA rates, plus a share of college, runs well into six figures per parent on its own — one reason INV-023 treats term life insurance as a core protection for parents with young children.

Tax year 2026

The child tax credit, the dependent care FSA and the care credit

Child tax credit2026: up to $2,200 per child
What it is
A credit that reduces your federal income tax dollar for dollar for each qualifying child under 17 at the end of the year. The 2025 tax law (P.L. 119-21) set it at $2,200 for 2025 with inflation indexing afterward; the IRS confirms $2,200 for 2026, of which up to $1,700 per child can be refunded if your tax is too low to use it (Rev. Proc. 2025-32).
Used for
Lowering families' taxes. The child must be your son, daughter, stepchild, eligible foster child, sibling or a descendant of one; live with you more than half the year; and be claimed as your dependent. You (or your spouse, if you file jointly) and each child must have a Social Security number valid for employment, issued by the return's due date (IRS).
What it means for you
The credit shrinks by $50 for each $1,000 (or part) of modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) above $200,000, or $400,000 on a joint return (26 U.S.C. 24(b), (h)). A dependent who does not qualify — a 17- or 18-year-old, or a parent you support — can bring a $500 credit for other dependents instead.
Child tax credit = $2,200 × qualifying children − $50 × ⌈(MAGI − $400,000 joint) ÷ $1,000⌉

For the Riveras, with income around $145,000, the credit is 2 × $2,200 = $4,400 for 2026. Jordan receives $2,200 for Ava, who is 15. When Ava turns 17 in 2028, that becomes the $500 credit for other dependents.

Paying for child care: two tax breaks that interact

Dependent care FSA2026 limit: $7,500 per household
What it is
A dependent care flexible spending account, offered through an employer's benefits plan, lets you pay for work-related child care with pre-tax pay. The 2025 law raised the annual exclusion from $5,000 to $7,500 ($3,750 if married filing separately) starting in 2026 (IRS Publication 15-B).
Used for
Day care, preschool, before- and after-school care and day camps for children under 13, and care for a spouse or dependent who cannot care for themselves, so that you can work.
What it means for you
FSA money avoids federal income tax, usually state income tax, and the 7.65% Social Security and Medicare tax. In the 22% bracket, $7,500 saves about $2,224 of federal and payroll tax before any state saving.
Child and dependent care creditnew rates from 2026
What it is
A nonrefundable credit for a percentage of up to $3,000 of work-related care costs for one qualifying person, or $6,000 for two or more. From 2026 the percentage starts at 50%, falls by one point for each $2,000 of adjusted gross income over $15,000 until it reaches 35%, and falls again — by one point for each $2,000 over $75,000 ($4,000 over $150,000 on a joint return) — to a floor of 20% (26 U.S.C. 21, as amended by P.L. 119-21).
Used for
Families who pay for care without an FSA, or whose costs exceed the FSA.
What it means for you
The $3,000 or $6,000 limit is reduced by every FSA dollar you exclude. With a full $7,500 FSA, no costs are left for the credit. Most middle-income families with an FSA at work do better using it; families with low income tax or no FSA use the credit.
Going furtherTwo more family rules: the kiddie tax and the adoption credit

The kiddie tax taxes a child's investment income above a threshold at the parents' rate. For 2026 the amount used in the calculation is $1,350: the first $1,350 of a child's unearned income is covered by the child's own standard deduction, the next $1,350 is taxed at the child's rate, and the rest at the parents' rate (Rev. Proc. 2025-32). It is one reason large custodial accounts are less attractive than they look. For 2026, the adoption credit covers up to $17,670 of qualified expenses, up to $5,120 of it refundable, phasing out between $265,080 and $305,080 of modified adjusted gross income (Rev. Proc. 2025-32).

New in 2026

Trump accounts: a starter investment account for children

The 2025 tax law created a new kind of account for children, officially named a Trump account and codified as section 530A of the tax code. The IRS describes it as a new type of individual retirement account (IRA). Its rules are still being filled in by Treasury regulations; everything below comes from the IRS program page (updated July 2026) and IRS Notice 2025-68.

Trump account
What it is
An account set up by the Treasury for a child, funded during a "growth period" that ends on December 31 of the year before the child turns 18. Money can be invested only in low-cost mutual funds or exchange-traded funds that track an index of mainly U.S. stocks, such as the S&P 500, with no leverage and annual fees of no more than 0.1%. From the year the child turns 18, it is generally treated as a traditional IRA.
Used for
Giving every child an early start on investing, with contributions from family, employers, governments and charities.
What it means for you
It is a long-term account, not a college fund. Nothing can be withdrawn before the child turns 18, and after that withdrawals follow traditional IRA rules: earnings are taxed as ordinary income.
RuleWhat the IRS says
Who can have oneA child who has not turned 18 by the end of the year the election is made and has a Social Security number issued before the election (U.S. citizenship is required only for the $1,000 pilot deposit)
How to openA parent, guardian or other authorized person files Form 4547, including online through an IRS account
$1,000 federal depositA one-time pilot program contribution for U.S. citizen children born January 1, 2025 through December 31, 2028

Going furtherTrump account or 529 for college?

For college, a 529 plan is usually better: 529 earnings are tax-free when spent on qualified education, while Trump account earnings are taxed as ordinary income when withdrawn after 18. Traditional IRA rules do waive the 10% early-withdrawal tax for qualified higher education expenses (IRS), but the income tax still applies. The Trump account's advantages are the free money — the $1,000 deposit for children born 2025–2028, and any employer contribution up to $2,500 — and a very long runway toward a first home or retirement. A sensible order: claim the pilot deposit if eligible, take any employer money, and put college savings in a 529.

Sticker price and net price

What college really costs in 2025–26

College prices are quoted in two very different ways. The published price, or sticker price, is what a college charges. The net price is what a student actually pays after grants and scholarships — money that does not have to be repaid. The gap is large, and it is where planning happens.

Source: College Board, Trends in College Pricing and Student Aid 2025, Table CP-1 and Figure CP-1. *Budgets add books and supplies, transportation and other expenses as allotted by colleges.

Grants change the picture. For first-time, full-time in-state students at public four-year colleges, the College Board estimates average grant aid of $9,650 in 2025–26, so the average student needs to cover about $16,200 of tuition, fees, housing and food, rather than $25,850. At private nonprofit four-year colleges, the average net figure is about $32,830 rather than $60,920.

💡

Every college that takes part in federal student aid must post a net price calculator on its website (20 U.S.C. 1015a(h)). Enter your own income and assets to see an estimate of your family's net price at that school. It is the single most useful number for comparing colleges and setting a savings target.

Going furtherHow fast will college prices grow?

Nobody knows. Published tuition at public four-year colleges rose by $3,760, or 42%, after inflation between 2005–06 and 2015–16, then fell $860, or 7%, in real terms over the following decade (College Board). Planning with price growth of about general inflation plus a small margin is a reasonable middle case; the calculator in Tab 7 lets you test faster and slower growth.

Financial aid

How the FAFSA counts your income and savings

The Free Application for Federal Student Aid (FAFSA) is the form that unlocks federal grants, work-study and federal student loans, and most colleges use it for their own aid too. By law, the Department of Education must open the application no later than October 1 before the year the student plans to enroll (20 U.S.C. 1090).

Its output is a single number, the Student Aid Index.

Student Aid Index (SAI)
What it is
A number calculated from the FAFSA that measures a family's financial strength. It replaced the old Expected Family Contribution and can be as low as −$1,500.
Used for
Deciding need-based aid. Financial need equals the college's cost of attendance minus the SAI minus other aid (20 U.S.C. 1087kk).
What it means for you
Income counts far more than savings. For a dependent student, parents' reportable assets above a protection allowance are counted at 12% and then assessed at graduated rates from 22% to 47%, so each extra parental dollar raises the SAI by at most about 5.6 cents. A dollar in the student's own name raises it by 20 cents (20 U.S.C. 1087oo).

Rules families trip over

  • A 529 is a parent asset. For a dependent student, a 529 or Coverdell account designated for the student counts as the parent's asset, whether the parent or the student owns it (20 U.S.C. 1087vv(f)(3)).
  • The home is excluded. The net value of the family's principal residence is not a reportable asset. From the 2026–27 award year, a family farm the family lives on and a family-owned small business with no more than 100 employees are also excluded (P.L. 119-21, section 80001).
  • Divorced parents. Only the income and assets of the parent who provides the greater share of the student's financial support are counted; if that parent has remarried, the stepparent's income counts too (20 U.S.C. 1087oo(f)).
  • Parent PLUS limits. Beginning July 1, 2026, parents may borrow no more than $20,000 a year and $65,000 in total in federal Parent PLUS loans for each dependent student, with a transition rule for students already enrolled and borrowing before then (20 U.S.C. 1087e, as amended by P.L. 119-21).
Practitioner depthThe dependent-student SAI in one line
SAI = assessment of parents' adjusted available income + assessment of student's available income + 20% × student's assets

Parents' adjusted available income is their total income minus allowances (taxes, a living allowance, an employment allowance), plus 12% of their assets above the asset protection allowance. It is then assessed on a schedule running from 22% up to $9,494 plus 47% of amounts over $35,100 (20 U.S.C. 1087oo(e)); the Department of Education adjusts those dollar brackets for inflation each year. Because the tax return from two years earlier is used, the income that matters for a student starting college in fall 2029 is the family's 2027 income.

Putting it together

A 529 plan strategy for Sofia and Leo

A 529 plan is a state-sponsored investment account for education. Contributions are not deductible federally, but growth is tax-free when spent on qualified education (INV-034 covers the account in full). Recent laws have widened what counts: from 2026, up to $20,000 a year per student can pay for kindergarten through 12th grade expenses, now including curriculum, books, online materials and tutoring, and qualified postsecondary credentialing expenses count too (26 U.S.C. 529, as amended by P.L. 119-21). Leftover money in an account open at least 15 years can move to the beneficiary's Roth IRA, up to a $35,000 lifetime limit, through the 529-to-Roth rollover.

Four steps to a college number

  1. Pick a target share. Few families save 100%. Many aim to cover a set share — one-third, one-half — from savings and the rest from current income, grants and modest borrowing.
  2. Price it. Start from today's cost of a realistic school type (public in-state tuition, fees, housing and food: $25,850 in 2025–26) and grow it to the enrollment years.
  3. Solve for the monthly amount. The calculator below does this.
  4. Keep retirement first. Students can borrow for college; nobody lends for retirement. A family that is not yet capturing its full 401(k) match should fix that first.
Going furtherInvesting a 529: the age-based glide path

Most 529 plans offer age-based or enrollment-date portfolios that hold mostly stocks when a child is young and shift toward bonds and cash as college approaches — the same idea as a target-date fund (INV-050). Money needed within about three years should not be exposed to a large stock decline. For Jordan, with Ava starting in three years, that means most of the $20,000 should already be in conservative holdings.

Real numbers, real law

Three stories behind the rules

Case study 1Sticker price up, net price down, 2006–2026
What happened
Between 2006–07 and 2025–26, average published tuition and fees at public four-year colleges rose 29% after inflation, from $9,260 to $11,950 in 2025 dollars. Over the same years, average grant aid per first-time, full-time in-state student rose about 85%, from $5,230 to about $9,650. The average net tuition such students paid fell to an estimated $2,300.
What it teaches
Headlines about rising tuition describe the sticker price. What a family pays depends on its income, the college and the aid it offers. Compare net prices, not published prices.
Source
College Board (2025), Trends in College Pricing and Student Aid 2025, Figure CP-9.
Case study 2The 2025 tax law makes the larger child credit permanent
What happened
The 2017 tax law temporarily expanded the child tax credit for 2018 through 2025, raising the phase-out thresholds to $200,000 and $400,000 and adding a $500 credit for other dependents. P.L. 119-21, signed July 4, 2025, made the expansion permanent, set the credit at $2,200 per child for 2025 with inflation indexing afterward, and required a work-eligible Social Security number for the taxpayer (on a joint return, at least one spouse) as well as for each child.
What it teaches
Family tax benefits are set by Congress and can change. Plans that depend on a credit should be checked each year against the IRS figures for that year.
Source
26 U.S.C. 24(h); IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32, section 2.03; IRS, Child tax credit (2026).
Case study 3The $1,000 account for every new baby
What happened
P.L. 119-21 created Trump accounts and a pilot program under which the Treasury deposits $1,000 for each U.S. citizen child born from 2025 through 2028 whose parent or guardian makes the election on Form 4547. Contributions from families and employers could begin July 4, 2026.
What it teaches
A new program's value depends on families actually claiming it. Parents of children born in those years should file the election; the money is invested in low-cost U.S. stock index funds until the child turns 18.
Source
IRS, Trump accounts (updated July 2026); IRS Notice 2025-68; IR-2025-117.

Decisions

Three families, three education decisions

Scenario 1 · The Riveras

The Riveras have $41,000 sitting in checking, a $9,000 emergency fund and no college savings. Sofia is 7 and Leo is 4. What should they do with the idle cash?

All to 529sIt would cover a large part of the half-of-public-college target, but it leaves only $9,000 of accessible savings for a family of four, and money withdrawn for non-education uses owes tax and a 10% penalty on the earnings.
Balance the goalsRight. A larger emergency fund protects everything else (INV-019). Lump sums of, say, $10,000 per child plus about $470 a month in total would meet the 50% target, and their 401(k) saving continues.
Retirement onlyRetirement comes first, but they already save in 401(k)s. Skipping college saving entirely means borrowing later, and Parent PLUS loans are capped at $65,000 per child from July 2026.
The principle: emergency fund, then retirement match, then college — but idle cash beyond the emergency fund should be put to work.
Scenario 2 · Jordan

Ava starts college in three years. Jordan has $20,000 saved for her. Where should Jordan focus?

Chase returnsThree years is too short for money that must be there on time. If it were all in stocks, a year like 2008, when the S&P 500 lost about 37%, could erase a third of it right before enrollment.
Plan around net priceRight. On $88,000 of income as a single parent, Ava may qualify for meaningful grant aid, and the $20,000 counts at most about 5.6 cents per dollar in the SAI. Knowing net prices early lets Jordan and Ava pick colleges the family can afford.
The principle: close to enrollment, protect what you have and shop on net price.
Scenario 3 · The Harpers

Ethan, 20, is in college. The Harpers wonder whether to add $15,000 of Rachel's bonus to his 529 or put it in her 401(k).

The 529A contribution this close to spending earns little tax-free growth. It can still make sense if their state gives a deduction, but the benefit is small.
The 401(k)Usually right for the Harpers. Their federal marginal rate is 24% at $260,000, and they are five to seven years from retirement with most savings still to be positioned (INV-031).
The principle: late in the college years, retirement saving usually beats new 529 money.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

A married couple with two children under 17 has modified adjusted gross income of $412,500 in 2026. What is their child tax credit, in dollars?

Excess over $400,000 = $12,500, which is 13 thousands "or fraction thereof." Reduction = 13 × $50 = $650. Credit = $4,400 − $650 = $3,750.
Exercise 2

A parent in the 22% federal bracket puts the full 2026 limit into a dependent care FSA. Ignoring state tax, roughly how much federal income and payroll tax is saved, in dollars?

The 2026 limit is $7,500. Savings = $7,500 × (0.22 + 0.0765) = about $2,224. The credit alone, at 35% on $6,000 for two children, would be $2,100.
Exercise 3

A family already above the asset protection allowance and in the top 47% assessment band adds $10,000 to a parent-owned 529. By how much, at most, can the student's SAI rise, in dollars?

12% of the $10,000 ($1,200) is added to adjusted available income, which is assessed at 47% at the top: $1,200 × 0.47 = $564. In the student's name it would be 20% × $10,000 = $2,000.
Exercise 4

A child born in 2026 receives the $1,000 Trump account pilot deposit. If it earns 6% a year for 18 years, what is it worth, in dollars?

$1,000 × 1.0618 = $1,000 × 2.854 = $2,854. The whole amount is taxable when withdrawn, because pilot deposits create no basis.
