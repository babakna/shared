Market Efficiency and the Evidence

Millions of well-paid, well-informed people compete every day to find mispriced stocks. This module explains what it means for a market to be "efficient," what the scorecards say about professionals who try to beat it, and where the evidence says prices do go wrong — and why that is still hard to profit from.

The question this module answersCan I, or anyone I pay, reliably beat the market — and how would I know?

Start here

"If it were that easy, everyone would do it"

You already use the idea behind this module. If a stranger offers you a $20 bill for $10, you assume there is a catch, because anything that good would have been snapped up already. Financial markets are the most crowded bargain hunt in the world.

That does not mean every price is right. It means that when a price is wrong in an obvious way, thousands of professionals with computers, data and billions of dollars race to correct it, and the race is usually over before an ordinary investor hears the news. The practical question is not "are markets perfect?" (they are not) but "after costs, can I or my manager reliably do better than simply owning the whole market?" For more than two decades, S&P Dow Jones Indices has kept score. Figure 43.1 shows the result for the most closely watched group: US large-company stock funds.

💡

The ordinary case first. Plenty of active funds beat the index in any one year — a third did in the first half of 2026. The hard part is doing it consistently, after fees, and knowing in advance which fund will be the one. That is what the evidence in this module measures.

What this module covers

  1. What the efficient market hypothesis says, in its three forms.
  2. The evidence that prices absorb news fast.
  3. Sharpe's arithmetic: why the average active dollar must trail, whatever markets do.
  4. The latest SPIVA (S&P Indices Versus Active) scorecards, including survivorship.
  5. Persistence: do winners keep winning, or is it luck?
  6. Anomalies: where prices do seem to go wrong, and why that is hard to exploit.

The idea

What an "efficient" market is — and is not

Start with what a price is: the point where the people who want to buy and the people who want to sell agree. Each of them brings what they know. If a well-informed buyer learns that a company's new drug has worked, she buys, and the price rises until the news is no longer a bargain. In that sense a price is a summary of everything its traders know.

Efficient market hypothesisEugene Fama, 1970
What it is
The idea that security prices "fully reflect" available information, so that no one can earn returns above what the risk they take would justify by trading on that information — at least not reliably, and not after costs.
Used for
A benchmark for testing markets, and the intellectual foundation of index funds: if prices are hard to beat, owning the whole market cheaply is a sensible default.
What it means for you
When you read news about a company, the price has almost certainly moved already. Your edge, if you have one, is not in knowing public facts.

Fama organized the tests into three "forms," each defined by the information the market is supposed to reflect. Each form includes the one before it.

Weak-form efficiency
What it is
Prices already reflect everything in their own history, so past prices alone cannot predict future ones.
Used for
Testing trading rules based on charts and trends.
What it means for you
A stock that has "fallen a lot" is not cheap for that reason alone, and one that has "run up" is not necessarily expensive.
Semi-strong-form efficiency
What it is
Prices adjust quickly and without bias to all public information: earnings, economic data, news, filings, analysts' reports.
Used for
Event studies that measure how fast prices react to announcements.
What it means for you
Reading the annual report carefully is useful for understanding what you own; it is rarely enough to outguess a price that thousands of analysts have already read.
Going furtherWhy a perfectly efficient market cannot exist

Sanford Grossman and Joseph Stiglitz (1980) pointed out a paradox. Gathering and analyzing information costs money. If prices already reflected all of it perfectly, no one would be paid for the effort, so no one would do it — and then prices could not reflect it. The resolution is that markets must be slightly inefficient: just inefficient enough to reward the people who do the work, after their costs. That is a useful way to think about the whole debate: a small edge exists, it is competed for fiercely, and its rewards go mainly to the lowest-cost, best-informed professionals.

Practitioner depthThe joint-hypothesis problem, and a split Nobel

Every test of efficiency is also a test of a model of what returns should be. If value stocks earn more than the model predicts, either the market is inefficient or the model has left out a risk. Fama called this the joint-hypothesis problem: efficiency can never be tested on its own. It is why the same data are read as "mispricing" by behavioral economists and as "compensation for risk" by others (INV-046, INV-048).

In 2013 the Nobel economics prize went jointly to Eugene Fama, Lars Peter Hansen and Robert Shiller "for their empirical analysis of asset prices." Fama is the best-known defender of efficiency; Shiller is the best-known critic, whose work on excess volatility and bubbles is behind the cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings (CAPE) ratio (INV-006). The committee honored both, which is a fair summary of the state of knowledge.

How fast is fast?

Prices react to news in minutes, not months

The most direct test of the semi-strong form is the event study: pick a type of announcement — an earnings surprise, a merger, a stock split — line up hundreds of examples on the day each was announced, and see how prices moved before and after. If the market is efficient, prices should jump when the news arrives and then drift neither up nor down. Fama, Fisher, Jensen and Roll (1969), studying stock splits, published the first modern event study; thousands have followed.

A striking real example is in the Case studies tab: after the Space Shuttle Challenger exploded in 1986, the stock market identified the contractor whose part had failed within minutes, although the official investigation took more than four months.

Alpha
What it is
The part of a return that cannot be explained by the market's movement or other risks the investment took. A fund that earned 9% when its benchmark earned 8%, with the same risk, has an alpha of about +1%.
Used for
Judging whether a manager added value beyond what an index fund of similar risk would have delivered.
What it means for you
Alpha is what an active fee buys, if it buys anything. After costs, the average alpha across all active funds is negative, for reasons the next tab explains.
Going furtherEfficient does not mean "right," and it does not mean "stable"

Two common misreadings. First, efficiency does not mean prices are correct in hindsight; it means they are unbiased forecasts given what is known. A stock that falls 40% after an unforeseeable lawsuit was not "mispriced" before it. Second, efficiency does not mean prices are calm. New information arrives constantly and much of it is surprising, so efficient prices move a lot. Volatility is not evidence of inefficiency.

The argument that needs no data

Why the average active dollar must trail

Most arguments about active management are empirical: they depend on which funds and which years you look at. William Sharpe, a Nobel laureate, made one that does not. It is pure arithmetic, and it holds in every market, every year.

The arithmetic of active managementWilliam F. Sharpe, 1991
What it is
Divide all investors into passive ones, who hold every security in market proportions, and active ones, who do anything else. Before costs, the passive group earns exactly the market return. So the active group, which owns the rest of the market, must earn it too. After costs, the average active dollar therefore earns less than the average passive dollar, by the difference in costs.
Used for
Showing that underperformance by active funds as a group is not a sign that managers are foolish; it is a mathematical consequence of their higher costs.
What it means for you
Paying more for active management buys you a chance of beating the market and a larger chance of trailing it. It cannot raise the average.
Average active return after costs = Market return − Average active cost   <   Market return − Average passive cost
Practitioner depthObjections to the arithmetic

The argument uses the market as the benchmark, so it applies to the whole group of stocks both sides hold. Critics note that "passive" investors in practice are not perfectly passive: index funds trade when stocks enter or leave an index, when companies issue or buy back shares, and at initial public offerings, and active traders can profit slightly from those trades. Lasse Heje Pedersen (2018) argued that this lets active managers, as a group, be worth positive fees. The effect is small relative to the difference between a 0.05% index fund and a fund charging 0.60% or more, so the practical conclusion survives: costs are the one variable you control, and they decide the average.

Survivorship: the funds you never hear about

Survivorship bias
What it is
The distortion that comes from looking only at funds that still exist. Funds with poor records are often closed or merged into siblings, so the survivors look better than the full set of funds investors actually had to choose from.
Used for
Explaining why a fund company's list of "funds with 20-year records" flatters the industry.
What it means for you
When you pick a fund today, you are picking from all funds, including those that will close. SPIVA counts closed funds as having failed, which is how an investor would experience it.
Going furtherThe average dollar, not just the average fund

Counting funds treats a $10 million fund the same as a $10 billion one. SPIVA also reports asset-weighted returns. Over the 20 years to June 2026 the S&P 500 returned 11.39% a year; the average large-cap fund, weighted by assets, returned 10.33%, and the simple average fund 9.47%. Big funds did better than small ones, largely because the biggest funds tend to be cheaper, but the average invested dollar still fell about one point a year behind. On $100,000 over 20 years that is roughly $865,000 versus $714,000.

Practitioner depthRisk-adjusted results and style drift

SPIVA's Report 1b repeats the comparison using returns divided by volatility. On that basis 97.68% of large-cap funds underperformed over 20 years, worse than the 92.61% on raw returns: the funds that beat the index often did so by taking more risk. Report 2 also measures style consistency — whether a fund stayed in its category. Over 20 years only 58.26% of surviving large-cap funds kept the same style, so a fund bought as "large value" may not remain one.

Do winners keep winning?

Picking last year's winner rarely works

Suppose you accept that most funds lag. The natural next step is: "Then I'll pick one of the minority that wins." To do that you need winners to stay winners. S&P's Persistence Scorecard tests exactly this, and so can you, with nothing more than a coin.

Performance persistence
What it is
The tendency, if any, for funds that did well in one period to do well in the next.
Used for
Testing whether good results reflect skill (which should persist) or luck (which should not).
What it means for you
A strong past record is the main reason people choose a fund. If records do not persist, that reason is weaker than it feels.

The coin-flip model shows why a five-year winning streak proves little. Among 1,000 managers with no skill, about 31 would beat their benchmark five years running by luck. Some of them will be on magazine covers. The Persistence Scorecard adds a second finding that is more useful: poor performance does persist. Over consecutive five-year periods, 23% of the worst-quartile domestic equity funds were merged or liquidated within five years, against 10% of the top quartile. High costs are a dependable cause of lasting underperformance.

Going furtherWhat the academic studies found

Carhart (1997) studied a sample free of survivorship bias and found that common risk factors and investment expenses "almost completely explain" persistence in fund returns; the only strong persistence was among the worst funds. Fama and French (2010) ran simulations and concluded that few funds produce returns sufficient to cover their costs, although, before fees, there is evidence of some skill in the extreme tails. That is the honest conclusion: some managers are skilled, but they are few, hard to identify in advance, and often charge enough to keep the benefit for themselves (INV-045).

Where prices seem to go wrong

Anomalies, and why they are hard to exploit

If markets were perfectly efficient, no simple, publicly known rule would earn more than its risk justifies. Researchers have found many rules that seem to: they are called anomalies. The best established are the subject of INV-046 and INV-048; here is the overview.

Market anomaly
What it is
A pattern in returns that the standard model of risk and return does not predict — for example, cheap "value" stocks or recent winners earning more on average than their measured risk implies.
Used for
Challenging efficiency, and building "factor" strategies that tilt toward the pattern.
What it means for you
An anomaly on paper is not money in your pocket. It may be a reward for a hidden risk, it may shrink once published, and it may disappear for a decade.

Three findings temper the excitement:

  1. Publication shrinks them. R. David McLean and Jeffrey Pontiff (2016) studied 97 published predictors of stock returns. Returns were 26% lower out of sample and 58% lower after publication, consistent with investors learning about mispricing from academic papers and trading it away.
  2. Many are statistical flukes. With hundreds of factors tested on the same data, some will look significant by chance. Campbell Harvey, Yan Liu and Heqing Zhu (2016) argued that a new factor should clear a t-statistic above 3.0, not the usual 2.0.
  3. Some mispricing cannot be traded away. The 3Com–Palm case (Case studies tab) shows a price that was plainly wrong, yet arbitrage was blocked because the overpriced shares were nearly impossible to borrow and sell short.
Limits to arbitrage
What it is
The costs and risks — borrowing fees, short-sale restrictions, the chance a mispricing gets worse before it gets better — that stop professionals from correcting every wrong price.
Used for
Explaining how a price can be wrong without offering anyone an easy profit.
What it means for you
"The market is wrong about this stock" and "I can profit from it" are different claims. The second is much harder to make true.
Going furtherBubbles

The strongest case against efficiency is the bubble: long stretches when prices rise far above any reasonable valuation. Robert Shiller's cyclically adjusted P/E for US stocks reached 44.2 in December 1999, the highest in his data back to 1881, before a three-year bear market (INV-006). Yet timing a bubble is notoriously hard; the same measure was already above 30 in 1997, and investors who sold then missed two more years of large gains. INV-052 covers market timing.

Real history

Three episodes that bracket the debate

Case study 1The Challenger explosion, January 28, 1986
What happened
The Space Shuttle Challenger broke apart shortly after launch. Several public companies were major shuttle contractors, and in the confusion no one publicly knew which part had failed. A presidential commission took more than four months to name the cause, in its June 1986 report. Michael Maloney and Harold Mulherin studied trading that day and concluded that "the stock market discerned the guilty party within minutes of the announcement of the crash": Morton Thiokol, maker of the booster-rocket seals the commission later blamed. They also found that this price discovery "occurred without large trading profits."
What it teaches
Thousands of traders, each knowing a little, aggregated their knowledge into prices far faster than any formal investigation. That is the semi-strong form at work.
Source
Maloney, M. T., & Mulherin, J. H. (2003). The complexity of price discovery in an efficient market: The stock market reaction to the Challenger crash. Journal of Corporate Finance, 9(4), 453–479.
Case study 23Com and Palm, March 2000: a market that could not subtract
What happened
On March 2, 2000, 3Com sold about 5% of its Palm subsidiary to the public and announced it would give its shareholders about 1.5 Palm shares for every 3Com share. Palm closed its first day at $95.06, so each 3Com share carried a claim to about $145 of Palm stock. 3Com closed at $81.81. The market valued the rest of 3Com — a profitable business holding more than $10 a share in cash — at minus $63 a share, about minus $22 billion.
What it teaches
Prices can be obviously wrong. But the easy trade — sell Palm short, buy 3Com — was blocked because Palm shares were extremely expensive or impossible to borrow. Owen Lamont and Richard Thaler call this market efficiency "with very wrong prices": no money left on the table for ordinary investors, yet the price was not right.
Source
Lamont, O. A., & Thaler, R. H. (2003). Can the market add and subtract? Mispricing in tech stock carve-outs. Journal of Political Economy, 111(2), 227–268; working paper NBER w8302.
Case study 3Warren Buffett's ten-year bet, 2008–2017
What happened
In December 2007 Buffett bet that an unmanaged S&P 500 index fund would beat a selection of hedge funds over ten years. Protégé Partners chose five funds-of-funds, which together held interests in more than 200 hedge funds. The index fund gained 125.8% (8.5% a year); the five funds-of-funds gained between 2.8% and 87.7% in total (0.3% to 6.5% a year). Buffett estimated their fees averaged about 2.5% of assets a year.
What it teaches
Even highly skilled, highly paid managers start each year roughly one fee-level behind. In Buffett's words: "Performance comes, performance goes. Fees never falter."
Source
Berkshire Hathaway Inc. (2018). 2017 annual letter to shareholders.

Decisions

Three pitches, three households

Scenario 1 · Jordan

A coworker tells Jordan to put her 401(k) contributions into the plan's "Aggressive Growth" fund: it beat the S&P 500 in each of the last five years. The plan also offers an S&P 500 index fund at 0.04% and target-date funds. What should she weigh?

Chase the streakAmong 1,000 funds with no skill, about 31 would win five years running by luck. Large-cap growth funds did especially poorly on SPIVA: 99.5% trailed the S&P 500 Growth index over 20 years to mid-2026.
Investigate, default to low costRight. If the fund's fee is high, its holdings are simply large growth companies, and its longer record is ordinary, the streak is likely luck plus a style that happened to be in favor. Her 50% match matters far more than the fund choice (INV-022).
Split the differenceNot unreasonable as a hedge against regret, but it keeps half her money in the higher-cost option on the strength of weak evidence.
The principle: a winning streak is the start of an investigation, not the end of one.
Scenario 2 · The Harpers

The Harpers' advisor, who charges 1.25% a year, says their 25 technology stocks "beat the market" last year, so his stock picking is paying for itself. The technology sector index rose even more. How should they judge the claim?

Accept the claimBeating a broad index while holding only one sector says more about the sector than about the picking. In a year technology falls, the same portfolio will trail badly.
Use the right yardstickRight. Against a technology index they lagged, and the fee comes on top. The concentration is a separate risk they should address with tax-aware planning (INV-035, INV-037).
The principle: judge a manager against a benchmark with the same kind of risk, after all fees.
Scenario 3 · Monica

Monica manages billing at a hospital. In a confidential meeting she learns, before any announcement, that the hospital system will move all of its billing to a publicly traded software company, a contract large enough to move that company's stock. A colleague suggests they each buy the vendor's shares in their taxable accounts before the news breaks.

Buy before the newsThis is exactly the information the strong form says the market does not reflect — and exactly the trading securities law prohibits for insiders. The personal risk far outweighs any gain.
Do not trade on itRight. Information learned in confidence at work belongs to the employer, and securities law can reach trades in a supplier's or customer's stock, not only the employer's own. Monica's taxable account holds broad index funds (INV-044); her plan should run on a schedule, not on tips.
The principle: the only reliable information edge is one you are not allowed to use.

Six things people say about beating the market

Apply it

What this module means for each household

Start with the default that wins most of the time. A low-cost index or target-date fund in her 401(k) puts Jordan ahead of most professionally managed funds over the decades she will invest. Her savings rate and the match matter far more than fund picking.
Look inside the six funds. If some of their overlapping funds are actively managed large-cap funds, SPIVA's 92.6% 20-year underperformance rate applies to exactly that category. Their plan's index options are the natural replacements (INV-044).
No tips, no timing; watch the costs. Confidential news from work is not an edge Monica can legally use. At 58, with $370,000 invested across her 401(k), Roth IRA and taxable account, low costs matter more than any hunch: in Figure 43.11, trailing by one point a year for 20 years would cost that balance about $205,000.
Ask for the right benchmark. A 25-stock technology portfolio should be measured against a technology index, after the 1.25% fee. If the advisor's value is planning, not picking, the portfolio can be indexed and the fee judged on the planning.
Be skeptical of market-beating promises. Retirees are prime targets for products and newsletters that claim to beat the market safely. Ruth can ask the four questions in Figure 43.9, and remember that a guaranteed edge would not be sold door to door.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

The market returns 8%. Index funds cost 0.05% and the average active dollar pays 0.60%. By how many percentage points does the average active dollar trail the average passive dollar?

Before costs both groups earn 8%. Passive: 8 − 0.05 = 7.95%. Active: 8 − 0.60 = 7.40%. Gap = 0.55 points, whatever the market does.
Exercise 2

1,000 managers have no skill; each has a 50% chance of beating the benchmark in any year. How many would you expect to beat it five years in a row?

1,000 × 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 1,000 ÷ 32 = 31.25.
Exercise 3

SPIVA counted 690 large-cap funds at the start of the 20 years to June 2026, and 36.52% survived. About how many were still operating at the end?

690 × 0.3652 = 251.99, so about 252 funds survived; about 438 closed or merged.
Exercise 4

$100,000 grows for 20 years at 11.39% (the S&P 500) or at 10.33% (the asset-weighted large-cap fund average). What is the difference in ending value, in dollars?

100,000 × 1.113920 = $864,817; 100,000 × 1.103320 = $714,286. Difference ≈ $150,530.
