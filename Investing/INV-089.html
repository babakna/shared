State Estate and Inheritance Taxes

The federal estate tax reaches only estates above $15 million in 2026. State death taxes can start at $1 million, or, in Pennsylvania, at the first dollar an adult child inherits. This module maps which states tax estates or inheritances, explains the two kinds of tax, and works through Pennsylvania's inheritance tax in detail for Ruth and Anna.

The question this module answersWill my state, or a state where I own property, tax what I leave, even though the federal government will not?

Start here

Two layers of death tax

After INV-088, you might conclude that death taxes are only for the very rich. At the federal level that is right. But states set their own rules, and some of them reach ordinary middle-class estates.

As of January 1, 2026, 12 states and the District of Columbia levied an estate tax and 5 states levied an inheritance tax, with Maryland levying both, according to the Tax Foundation's compilation from state statutes. Most estate-tax states exempt the first $2 million to $7 million, so the tax still reaches only well-off estates. Inheritance taxes are different: they are charged on what each heir receives, and the rate depends on the heir's relationship to the person who died.

Only one of the five households with a stated state lives in a death-tax state: Ruth, in Pennsylvania, where children and grandchildren pay 4.5% from the first dollar. Arizona, Colorado, Ohio and North Carolina levy neither tax. The Shahs' state is unspecified, so their estate review must identify domicile and every state where they own property before drawing a conclusion.

Two different taxes

Who pays, on what, at what rate

State estate tax
What it is
A tax on the total value of a decedent's estate above a state exemption, paid by the estate before heirs receive anything. It works like the federal estate tax, but with lower exemptions and rates that usually top out at 12% to 20% (Washington's reached 35% for deaths from July 1, 2025 through June 30, 2026).
Used for
Raising revenue from large estates of residents, and from real and tangible property located in the state owned by non-residents.
What it means for you
The size of the whole estate matters, not who inherits. Spouses are usually protected by a marital deduction.
Inheritance tax
What it is
A tax on the value each heir receives, at a rate that depends on the heir's relationship to the decedent. See inheritance tax.
Used for
Taxing transfers to more distant relatives and non-relatives more heavily than transfers to close family.
What it means for you
Spouses are exempt in every inheritance-tax state. Children are exempt in most; Pennsylvania taxes adult children at 4.5% from the first dollar, and Nebraska taxes close relatives, including children, at 1% on amounts above $100,000. The "exemption" is often per heir, not per estate.
Going furtherThe federal deduction for state death taxes

For the few estates that owe both, the federal estate tax allows a deduction for state estate, inheritance and similar death taxes paid, which reduces the combined burden. The IRS's 2024 statistics show that 1,280 of 7,195 federal estate tax returns claimed a state death tax deduction (IRS Statistics of Income, Table 1). For estates below $15,000,000 the deduction does not matter, because there is no federal tax to reduce.

New York's cliff

Most estate taxes apply only to the amount above the exemption. New York is different. Its credit is available only when the New York taxable estate (including certain gifts) is not greater than 105% of the basic exclusion amount. Between 100% and 105%, the credit shrinks; above 105%, it disappears entirely and the whole estate is taxed (NY Department of Taxation and Finance, TSB-M-14(6)M). For deaths in 2026 the exclusion is $7,350,000, so the credit is gone at $7,717,500.

NY credit base = exclusion × [1 − (taxable estate − exclusion)
÷ (5% × exclusion)]
for estates between 100% and 105% of the exclusion
Going furtherNon-residents and real estate

New York requires an estate tax return from a non-resident whose estate includes real or tangible property located in New York when the federal gross estate plus includible gifts exceeds the exclusion. Other estate-tax states have similar rules. A Florida retiree with a Massachusetts summer home, or an Arizona couple with a condo in Oregon, can owe a state estate tax to a state they do not live in. A revocable trust avoids ancillary probate in that state, but does not avoid its estate tax.

Five states

The inheritance taxes: Kentucky, Maryland, Nebraska, New Jersey, Pennsylvania

Each inheritance-tax state sorts heirs into classes. Spouses are exempt everywhere. Kentucky, Maryland and New Jersey fully exempt children; Nebraska taxes them at 1% above $100,000; Pennsylvania taxes adult children at 4.5% from the first dollar. Iowa, which had an inheritance tax, repealed it: "Iowa inheritance tax is not applicable for deaths occurring on or after 1/1/25" (Iowa Department of Revenue).

Going furtherWho counts as "lineal"?

Pennsylvania defines direct descendants to include natural children (whether or not adopted by others), adopted descendants and step-descendants, and lineal heirs to include grandparents, parents and their children (REV-584). A stepchild is therefore taxed at 4.5%, not 15%. For other relatives, such as in-laws, check the statute's definitions with a Pennsylvania professional before assuming a rate. Definitions in other states differ; Kentucky, for example, puts sons- and daughters-in-law in Class B.

For Ruth and Anna

How Pennsylvania's inheritance tax works

Pennsylvania's tax is broad. The Department of Revenue says it is "imposed as a percentage of the value of a decedent's estate transferred to beneficiaries by will, heirs by intestacy and transferees by operation of law." Avoiding probate does not avoid it.

What is taxed

  • Everything a resident owned: all real and tangible personal property located in Pennsylvania, and all intangible property (stocks, bonds, bank accounts) wherever located (REV-584).
  • Joint property with anyone but a spouse: the decedent's fractional share — half for two owners — even if a name was added only for convenience. If the joint interest was created within a year of death, the full value less $3,000 is taxable (REV-584).
  • Gifts within one year of death: taxable to the extent they exceed $3,000 per recipient in a calendar year (72 P.S. § 9107(c)(3)).
  • Retirement accounts, if the decedent could withdraw without penalty. Pennsylvania's regulation taxes payments from retirement plans when the decedent had the right to withdraw benefits (61 Pa. Code § 93.131); the Department of Revenue notes that for a decedent under 59½, IRAs typically are not taxable unless the decedent was disabled.

What is exempt or deductible

  • Transfers to a spouse, and property owned jointly by spouses.
  • Life insurance proceeds, whether paid to the estate or a named beneficiary, for deaths on or after December 13, 1982 (61 Pa. Code § 93.131).
  • Charities and exempt institutions; certain farmland and family businesses; property of military members who die from active-duty injury or illness.
  • Deductions: the decedent's unpaid debts, administration costs, funeral and burial expenses including a tombstone, attorney and executor fees, and a family exemption of $3,500 that a spouse, or a child or parent living in the household, may claim (REV-584).

When it is due

The tax is due at death and delinquent nine months later. If it is paid within three months of death, a 5% discount applies (PA Department of Revenue). The return, REV-1500, is filed in duplicate with the Register of Wills in the county where the decedent lived. An inheritance is not taxable income for Pennsylvania personal income tax.

What works and what does not

Reducing state death taxes, honestly

Lifetime gifts: a trade-off, not a free lunch

Pennsylvania has no gift tax, and gifts made more than a year before death escape its inheritance tax. For Ruth, giving Anna $100,000 of cash in 2026 and living past 2027 would save $4,500 of inheritance tax. But gifts reduce the money Ruth has for her own care, which may be her largest future expense (INV-083), and gifts of appreciated property, such as the house, pass Ruth's old cost basis to Anna instead of the step-up she would get by inheriting (INV-090). For a house with a large gain, the income tax lost can exceed the 4.5% saved.

Domicile and moving

State death taxes are based mainly on domicile (where you live permanently) and on where real and tangible property sits. A non-resident's intangible property is not subject to Pennsylvania inheritance tax (REV-584). A retiree who genuinely moves from Pennsylvania to a state with no death tax changes the result for everything except Pennsylvania real estate. But states look at the facts — where you vote, file taxes, see doctors and spend your time — and a move made only on paper can be challenged.

Trusts in estate-tax states

In states with estate taxes and no portability, a married couple can lose the first spouse's state exemption by leaving everything outright to the survivor. A credit shelter trust funded up to the state exemption (INV-087) is the standard fix in states such as Massachusetts and Oregon. It does nothing for Pennsylvania's inheritance tax, where transfers to a spouse are already exempt and transfers to children are taxed whichever way they travel.

Practitioner depthRoth conversions and the Pennsylvania tax base

Converting part of a traditional IRA to a Roth IRA during life (INV-038) moves income tax forward and reduces the value that heirs must later withdraw as taxable income. For Pennsylvania inheritance tax, the conversion's income tax, paid from outside funds, reduces the estate that will be taxed at 4.5%. Whether that is worthwhile depends on Ruth's own bracket compared with Anna's future bracket; the inheritance tax effect is small next to the income tax effect. Pennsylvania's treatment of inherited Roth IRAs should be confirmed with the Department of Revenue or a Pennsylvania tax professional.

Recent changes

States keep rewriting these taxes

Case study 1Washington raises its estate tax, then reverses it (2025–2026)
What happened
For deaths from July 1, 2025 through June 30, 2026, Washington's exclusion was $3,000,000 ($3,076,000 for deaths from January 1 to June 30, 2026), and its rates ran from 10% on the first $1,000,000 of taxable estate to 35% on amounts above $9,000,000. For deaths on or after July 1, 2026, the Department of Revenue's tables show the earlier schedule again, 10% to 20%, with a $3,000,000 exclusion.
What it teaches
A family with a $4 million estate in Washington owes state estate tax under either schedule, even though it is $11 million below the federal exemption. And the rate depends on the date of death, so a plan built on one year's table can be out of date the next.
Source
Washington State Department of Revenue, Estate tax tables.
Case study 2Iowa repeals its inheritance tax (2025)
What happened
Iowa phased out its inheritance tax, and it no longer applies to deaths occurring on or after January 1, 2025.
What it teaches
The number of states with death taxes has been shrinking. Plans built around a state tax should be revisited when the law changes.
Source
Iowa Department of Revenue, Introduction to Iowa inheritance tax.
Case study 3New York's cliff, in the Department's own example
What happened
When New York introduced its phase-out in 2014, the Department of Taxation and Finance published an example with a $2,062,500 exclusion. An estate of $2,100,000 — just $37,500 above the exclusion — kept only part of its credit: the credit was the tax on $1,312,575 ($57,492), computed as $2,062,500 × (1 − 37,500 ÷ 103,125), with the fraction rounded to 0.6364. The estate owed $49,308.
What it teaches
Near the cliff, a small increase in the estate produces a large increase in tax. The same formula applies to 2026's $7,350,000 exclusion.
Source
New York State Department of Taxation and Finance. (2014). TSB-M-14(6)M, Estate tax; and Estate tax (2026 exclusion).

Decisions

Timing, gifts and where to live

Scenario 1 · Anna, as executor

Ruth has died. The estate's Pennsylvania inheritance tax is about $53,550. Anna can pay it within three months from Ruth's CDs, or wait until the house sells. What would you do?

WaitLegal, but it gives up about $2,678 (5% of $53,550) for no benefit, and the tax becomes delinquent if the nine months pass.
PrepayRight. The discount applies to the tax paid within three months, so paying an estimate early captures most of it even before every value is final. The CDs make it easy.
The principle: a 5% discount for paying three months early is one of the best returns an executor will see.
Scenario 2 · Ruth

Ruth reads that gifts made more than a year before death escape Pennsylvania's inheritance tax. Should she deed the house to Anna now?

Deed it nowThe saving is real if Ruth lives a year, but the income tax cost when Anna sells could be larger if the house has risen a lot in value since it was bought. Ruth would also no longer own her home.
Weigh it fullyRight. For appreciated property, the step-up usually beats a 4.5% inheritance tax saving. Gifting cash, or spending the IRA, can reduce the tax without losing basis.
The principle: never save a 4.5% tax by creating a larger income tax.
Scenario 3 · The Harpers

The Harpers are considering retiring near Rachel's family in Pennsylvania. Should state death taxes decide?

Avoid itA one-time 4.5% at the second death is real money, but it is not large next to 30 years of other state taxes and the value of living near family.
One cost among manyRight. States differ even more in how they tax income, property and retirement withdrawals over the years they would live there (INV-041). Compare the whole picture.
The principle: death taxes are one line in the cost of living somewhere, paid once.

Practice

Work the numbers yourself

Exercise 1

Ruth leaves her $380,000 house, $780,000 IRA and $60,000 of CDs to Anna. Deductible debts and expenses are $30,000. What is the Pennsylvania inheritance tax before any discount, in dollars?

$380,000 + $780,000 + $60,000 = $1,220,000; less $30,000 = $1,190,000; × 0.045 = $53,550. The IRA is included because Ruth is older than 59½ and could withdraw without penalty.
Exercise 2

Anna pays within three months of Ruth's death. How much tax is due after the discount, in dollars?

5% of $53,550 is $2,677.50. $53,550 − $2,677.50 = $50,872.50.
Exercise 3

Years ago, Ruth made Anna a joint owner of a $60,000 CD. At Ruth's death, how much Pennsylvania inheritance tax is due on the CD, in dollars?

Two joint owners: Ruth's share is $60,000 ÷ 2 = $30,000; × 0.045 = $1,350. If the joint account had been created within a year of death, $57,000 ($60,000 less $3,000) would be taxable, for $2,565.
Exercise 4

New York's 2026 exclusion is $7,350,000, and the credit disappears above 105% of it. At what taxable estate does the whole estate become taxable, in dollars?

$7,350,000 × 1.05 = $7,717,500.
